Architecture Overview

How the GC Curriculum Tool Works

What the tool does, why it was designed the way it was, and what that means for how far its results can be trusted: the scoring framework, how courses and careers are measured on the same scale, how the AI builds each course record, the equipment that runs it, and which of the framework's rules the software actually checks.

Audience Faculty, department leadership, reviewers and accreditors; technical notes for future maintainers Last updated 2026-10-21

§ 0The system today

A short orientation. Each point is covered in full in the section named. Throughout the page, notes marked In the code name the files, database tables and settings involved, so engineers can find them; other readers can skip them.

  • One computer, one database, campus only. A single Mac runs the web application, its database, its search index and the supporting services. The only way in is https://gcworkflow.clemson.edu:8443, which works on the Clemson network; there is no way to reach the app from off campus (§9).
  • Personal access links. Each faculty member signs in with a personal link that only works for their own courses. An administrator issues the links on /admin/access. The shared department login still works and will be switched off once every instructor has a link (§9).
  • CourseCapture: building a course record. The instructor uploads course materials. The tool reads them in the background, at a level of detail the instructor chooses, and removes student identifiers before storing anything. An AI then interviews the instructor, looking up passages in those materials as it goes, and drafts a Course Outcome Profile: a structured record of what the course is evidenced to develop. The instructor corrects and approves the draft, and approval saves a snapshot — a dated copy that can no longer be edited (§6).
  • One shared list of competencies. Every competency the department tracks is listed once, in a shared catalog, and any career target can draw on it. Each snapshot is scored once against every competency in the catalog; the per-career views are built by combining those scores (§4, §11).
  • Career targets linked to real jobs. Each of the five career targets lists the occupations GC alumni actually entered in their first jobs, linked to the federal O*NET occupation database (version 31.0). Faculty are reviewing cards that place O*NET job elements under catalog competencies, on /admin/bundles (§4).
  • Explore. A chat tied to one course that estimates how a proposed change to that course would ripple through the curriculum. Its scenarios can be saved, compared and adopted (§5, §11).
  • The wiki and these documents. A curriculum wiki is rewritten automatically from approved snapshots. A public copy of the wiki, and these documents, are published to Clemson web space, which can be read off campus (§9).
  • Built but switched off. The comparison of career demand against course attainment — "do we teach this deeply enough?" — is built, and its database table exists, but it is switched off, and no employer data has been fed into it yet (§4). In the code: setting DEMAND_COVERAGE_SEAM.

The engineering record of what is live, what is deferred and why is STATE.md.

§ 1Overview

The GC Curriculum Tool keeps a structured record of what each captured course in Clemson's Graphic Communications department is shown, by evidence, to develop in students. Each competency is scored on three dimensions — what students know, understand and can do — at a depth from 0 to 5. The tool then combines these course records into program-wide views. The purpose is to make visible things that committee discussion and periodic review tend to miss at this level of detail: competencies no course develops, how a competency builds from course to course (where it is introduced, practiced and brought together), courses that expect skills no earlier course teaches, and prerequisites that do not prepare students for what follows.

The system has four layers:

  • a framework (KUD+, §3) that defines what counts as developing a competency, and how deeply, and what evidence is needed to say so;
  • two kinds of capture that apply the same framework to two different things — what our courses develop (attainment) and what careers require (demand) (§4);
  • the pages people use: per-course capture and analysis tools for faculty, and program-level views (§5);
  • the infrastructure: a single Mac running the web server, the database, a search index over course materials, a document reader, connections to AI services, and a wiki that rewrites itself (§9).

For a shorter, stakeholder-facing account of what the tool does and why it matters, see the executive brief. For the intended end state and how it will be governed, see the vision document. For the day-to-day faculty workflow, see the faculty guide. This document describes the system as it is built, not as it is planned.

§ 2The Driving Questions

The design follows from two questions. The department has historically answered them through committee discussion, judgment and occasional formal review:

Q1 How well does the GC curriculum build students toward the careers we claim to prepare them for?
Q2 For any individual course, do the prerequisites students walk in with actually support what the course expects?

Q1 is a program question. Answering it means knowing what each course actually develops (not what its syllabus says it will) and what the careers actually require, and then comparing the two. Q2 is a course question. Answering it means comparing what a course expects students to bring in against what its prerequisite courses are shown to produce. Neither question can be answered without structured, evidence-based records of what courses do — and producing those records is what the capture process (§6) is for.

Q1 has two parts, and the difference between them runs through the rest of this document:

  • Breadth — "is this competency developed anywhere in the program?" The tool can answer this today.
  • Sufficiency — "is it developed deeply enough for what the career requires?" This needs career demand measured on the same depth scale as the courses, so that one can be subtracted from the other. The tool now captures both sides. The subtraction itself is built but switched off, waiting for a decision to turn it on and for the first employer data (§4).

The tool does not claim to settle these questions. It produces AI-drafted readings, each tied to specific evidence, that faculty can review, dispute and correct. Two things make a reading open to dispute: it points at specific evidence rather than syllabus language, and when a faculty member disagrees, the disagreement is kept on record rather than overwritten.

§ 3The KUD+ Framework

Every judgment the tool makes about a course and a competency is split into three parts. The Know / Understand / Do vocabulary comes from Carol Ann Tomlinson's differentiated-instruction work, where it is used to plan a unit before teaching it, and it fits with Stage 1 of Wiggins and McTighe's Understanding by Design. Two things are this framework's own additions, not something either source prescribes: scoring each part at graduated depths, and using the result to map a whole program (more on this below).

  • Know (K) — recalling and identifying content: terms, facts, conventions, sequences of steps. Typical question: "What is X?" / "Which of these is X?"
  • Understand (U) — reasoning about why: principles, relationships, consequences. Typical question: "Why does X work?" / "What follows from X?" / "When would you use X vs. Y?"
  • Do (D) — producing something: artifacts, performances, demonstrated skills. Typical instruction: "Make X. Produce X. Demonstrate X."

Each part is scored from 0 to 5. Each level describes what the student does or grasps, not what the syllabus says:

  • 0 — Not present (or listed only in aspirational syllabus language)
  • 1 — Exposure / restates the explanation as given / performs with per-step direction
  • 2 — Recognize / explains in own words / performs using a reference
  • 3 — Recall / predicts consequences / performs independently in familiar conditions
  • 4 — Uses correct terminology across the domain / reasons through novel cases / adapts to new conditions
  • 5 — Fluent across edge cases / critiques and extends / performs creatively with judgment, can guide others

Why levels instead of a yes/no? In practice most courses touch most relevant competencies at some level, so a "covered / not covered" grid ends up almost entirely "covered" and can no longer tell a passing mention from an assessed performance. The depth levels restore that difference.

In the code: the full rubric is lib/ai/prompts/shared/depth-scale.md, included in every scoring instruction sent to the AI; the profile format in lib/ai/capture/schema.ts accepts each depth only as a whole number from 0 to 5.

The evidence rule

A score above the lowest exposure level needs evidence that students actually engaged with the material, not just that the syllabus intends it. Specifically: K=1 needs evidence the content was delivered (a lecture, a module item, a reading-list entry); K above 1, and any U or D above 0, need evidence of assessed student work — answers, reasoning or performance. This is called the evidence-above-zero rule. It is the rule that separates an evidence-based map from a list of what courses say about themselves.

The rule is not only an instruction to the AI. Before a drafted profile is saved, the software checks it. If any score above the threshold lacks a quoted piece of evidence, the whole profile is rejected and the AI is asked to produce it again. So a profile that raises a score beyond its evidence cannot be saved. In the code: three Zod .refine() checks in the profile schema.

Foundational competencies

Six program-wide competencies — Agency, Attention to Detail, Resilience, Curiosity, Written Communication and Spoken Communication — are scored on Do only. Written and spoken communication have been recorded separately since 2026-10-19; profiles captured before then have five, with a single Communication row, and remain valid. Their Know and Understand scores are stored as empty (null), not as zero, and the software refuses to save a profile that fills them in. The reason: these are dispositions, shown through behavior. Students do not recall facts about Resilience or reason their way into it; they show it or they don't. A zero for Know or Understand would wrongly suggest that the course tried to teach recall or reasoning about a disposition and failed. A Do score of 0 for a foundational competency is a meaningful finding: the course does not create the conditions in which the disposition develops.

Mismatches between K, U and D

Scoring the three parts separately shows patterns that a single "covered" mark hides:

  • high Know, low Understand — students can name the thing but can't explain why it works (jargon without rationale);
  • high Understand, low Do — students understand the principle but can't carry it out (theory without craft);
  • high Do, low Understand — students can carry out the procedure but can't explain it (craft without articulation).

The scoring instructions turn this into a check the AI must apply: if it scores D at 3 or above while U is 1 or below, it must either lower the D score or write a rationale that names the mismatch. A score that does neither is treated by later steps as unsupported inflation.

Why not Bloom's Taxonomy?

Bloom's revised six-level taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) is the right tool for judging how demanding a particular assignment or test question is. It is a poor fit for mapping a whole program, for two reasons. First, across hundreds of course-and-competency pairs, choosing among six levels makes faculty review harder, not easier; telling Apply from Analyze consistently becomes a burden at that scale. Second, Bloom describes thinking; it does not treat "can produce it" as separate from "understands it." GC needs that separation, because craft skills in the field — pressing, color management, layout, prepress — involve performance that is distinct from understanding the same content. So the interviewing AI uses Bloom inside the conversation (it may ask which Bloom verb best describes a major assignment), but no Bloom level is stored in the profile. What is stored is K, U and D with depths.

How closely the software follows the research

A fair question about any framework is whether the software actually does what the research it cites describes, or only borrows the names. The table below takes five research-based elements. For each, it gives what the research says and how the software applies it. In four of them the software itself applies the rule — either a check that refuses to save a record breaking the rule, or a fixed calculation with no AI involved. The second row is different: the mismatch check is an instruction to the AI, which it may or may not follow (§12 rates it partial).

Each result is labeled with what it rests on: "measured" (captured courses only), "mixed" (captured courses, but a syllabus claim from a prerequisite with no capture raised the result above what the captured courses alone show), "intended" (syllabus claims only) or "none" (nothing). A prerequisite that has not been captured gives "no data," never a false zero. In the code: prereq-gaps.ts; the label is the basis field, values measured / mixed / intended / none.
What is a design choice, not a research finding

The background documents say where the framework makes its own choices rather than inheriting them from research, and the software keeps that distinction:

The 0–5 scale is the framework's own invention — a "working-hypothesis instrument." The cited traditions (Tomlinson, Wiggins & McTighe, AAC&U, EU Tuning) are consistent with a short, leveled scale; none prescribes this one.

KUD+ scores describe what a course gives students the opportunity to develop, not measured student attainment. They record, with evidence, what a course is designed to develop and what it assesses — not a measured outcome for any group of students. The planned Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study checks a narrower question — whether what the tool measures is relevant to the first jobs GC graduates actually take — and does not turn these scores into attainment claims either.

The 3-Act program structure is a proposal, not institutional policy, and the three named acts are not used in scoring. The scaffolding analysis checks a generic sequence — introduction, then practice, then integration — by course order (the research-based part); the named acts appear only in wiki pages and other prose.

The six foundational competencies are a "defensible working set," not "the right set", and can be revised as more courses are captured. And the highest evidence level, artifact_verified (verified against actual student work), is defined but cannot be reached yet, because the software has no way to cite a student artifact. So the strongest level any score can reach today is "materials-supported."

The full academic case — the theory, the relationship to Constructive Alignment, the evidence for adding depth levels — is in the Background document. The problem-solving position is in the problem-solving deep-dive, and the 3-Act structure in the 3-Act deep-dive. The table above samples five elements; §12 goes through every main claim and says, for each, whether the software enforces it, only instructs the AI to follow it, treats it as a design choice, or has not built it yet.

§ 4Courses and Careers on One Scale — Attainment & Demand

The tool uses one scale to measure two different things. Every competency is scored on the same K/U/D depth scale (0–5). CourseCapture applies it to our courses and asks: "how deeply can we show that students can do this?" Position Capture applies the same scale to a job. Its scores answer "how deeply does a new hire need to do this on day one?", and it also records other information about the job, kept apart from those scores (described below). Because both sides use the same units, and both refer to the same competency in the shared catalog, the first question (Q1) becomes a subtraction: what the job demands minus what the courses develop. In the code: both sides are linked by the catalog's sub_competency_id.

The attainment side — CourseCapture

CourseCapture records what students are shown to attain, one course at a time (the full process is in §6). An AI interviewer works from the syllabus, the Canvas materials and a conversation with the instructor. A separate drafting step then produces one structured Course Outcome Profile, scoring each competency on K, U and D against the shared rubric. Its defining feature is the evidence rule (§3): no score is saved higher than its evidence supports. Each approved profile becomes a snapshot that cannot be edited and is kept as a numbered version.

A further scoring step then reads each snapshot against every competency in the catalog, using each competency's own K/U/D descriptions. No career target is mentioned in that step, so a score means the same thing whichever career uses the competency. The results — one row per snapshot and competency — are the attainment depths behind the program heat map on /program. In the code: table snapshot_competency_coverage.

The demand side — Position Capture

Position Capture records what a career requires, using the same scale as the course side. An employer partner receives a personal link (a "magic link" that signs them in without an account) and works through six pages: uploading a job description; what makes the role distinctive; the questions the employer would ask in an interview; how the role grows over time; ten AI-suggested "experiences worth having," which the employer rates from 1 to 7 for importance; and a follow-up AI interview. The interview looks for gaps and contradictions in what the employer has said — for example, a growth path that does not fit the day-one expectations. A drafting step then produces one Position Profile, which cannot be edited afterwards. It holds:

  • Day-one K/U/D scores — how deeply each relevant competency is required of a new hire on day one at entry level. These are the only scores compared with the course side, and they are anchored to day one on purpose: a graduate's courses can only be expected to prepare them for the start of the job, so day-one demand is the fair comparison with what a graduate should arrive with. In the code: field required_for_success.
  • The role's growth path — what the job asks of someone in year 1 and in years 2–3. It is recorded separately and kept out of the day-one scores. In the code: field trajectory (year_1, year_2_to_3).
  • The rest of the employer's picture — what makes the job distinctive, the interview questions, and the ten rated experiences.

The growth path here comes from one employer describing one role. It is a different source from the "growth" career stage in a career target's alumni occupation mix (below), which records which occupations alumni later moved into.

Three rules aim to keep the day-one scores from being inflated, matching the course side. Only the third is checked by the software when a Position Profile is saved; the first two depend on the AI following its instructions (§12 rates this partial).

  • The same competency list. Each competency in a Position Profile, and each rated experience, can carry the identifier of the matching catalog competency — the same identifier the course side uses. The drafting step fills it in where it can, from the catalog list it is given. It is allowed to leave it empty, and nothing requires it to be filled. In the code: a nullable sub_competency_id.
  • Day one, not eventual mastery. The AI is instructed to score day one only and to keep the growth path out of the scores, so that a typical entry-level role comes out around D3 rather than being pushed toward expert depth. The software does not check this. In the code: instruction in lib/ai/prompts/position-synthesis.md.
  • Evidence for demand, too. Just as a syllabus verb cannot raise a course score, a vague endorsement cannot raise a demand score. Any depth above the minimum must carry a note of what the partner actually said, plus a confidence level. The software refuses to save a Position Profile with a depth above the minimum and no such note, so the AI is instructed to score a vague endorsement at the minimum instead: "We want good communicators" is K1, not D3. In the code: field evidenced_by, checked by a superRefine in lib/ai/position-capture/schema.ts.

Several positions for one career are combined into a summary for that career target. Today that summary is a side-by-side listing produced by a fixed procedure; combining them with AI is deferred. Because the app works only on the Clemson network, employers off campus cannot open a partner link at present.

The shared competency catalog

Both sides refer to one catalog of competencies rather than a separate list per career. Each competency is listed once, with its own K/U/D descriptions and a record of where it came from (carried over from the earlier per-career lists, written by GC faculty, or seeded from O*NET). A separate table records, for each career target and competency, how much the competency matters at each career stage (entry or growth) and on what basis. Which competencies a target shows follows from that: a competency can serve several targets, or none; a competency no target uses is still a valid catalog entry. When two competencies are merged, the retired one stays in the catalog, marked as merged into the one that replaced it, and a lookup table maps every old identifier to its current one, so older records still point to the right place.

In the code: catalog table sub_competencies with origin (migrated, gc_authored, onet_seeded); weights in career_target_requirements (one row per target, competency, horizon entry/growth, and weight source onet, employer, faculty or ai); which competencies each target shows in the derived table career_target_membership; views target_sub_competencies and target_coverage read that table and keep the older per-target shapes for code that still reads them; merges via merged_into and sub_competency_map.

Two kinds of outside evidence sit beside the catalog. First, each career target has an occupation mix for each career stage: which occupations (by federal SOC code) GC alumni actually entered, imported from the department's alumni data; faculty can add rows of their own. Second, the occupations in those mixes that O*NET rates are loaded into a local copy of O*NET 31.0. On top of that, each competency gets a bundle card — what the competency includes and excludes, and how it differs from its neighbors — and a review queue that places O*NET job elements and groups of tasks under each competency as core, related or fringe. The AI proposes these placements; a faculty reviewer confirms or rejects each one on /admin/bundles. The review page includes a blind sample checked independently by people, and a report of how often the AI agreed with them, so the AI's placements can be measured rather than assumed.

In the code: career_target_occupations; O*NET tables onet_release, onet_occupations, onet_element_ratings, onet_task_statements, onet_task_ratings; bundle_cards, bundle_members; AI function bundle-map-onet.

How much each competency matters to a career

Each competency has an importance weight for each career target, from 0 to 1 on O*NET's importance scale: 0 not important, 0.25 somewhat important, 0.5 important, 0.75 very important, 1 extremely important. A weight can come from four sources, and the first one present wins:

  1. A faculty decision — a weight faculty set and confirmed.
  2. An AI draft — a provisional rating for every competency and target, made from the competency descriptions and the occupations graduates actually enter, each with a one-sentence reason. Everything resting on an AI draft is labeled provisional until faculty review it.
  3. O*NET and employers — O*NET's importance ratings for the competency's confirmed core items in the target's occupations, blended with Position Capture ratings from employers when there are any.
  4. Nothing — no weight yet.

The O*NET calculation is stored but does not decide anything yet. A trial run showed that broad O*NET activities (such as "Getting Information", rated highly important in nearly every job) lift almost every competency to the top for every career, so the method needs refining before it can stand in for faculty judgment.

A target shows a competency when faculty pinned it there, or when its weight for that target is at least 0.5 (important), unless faculty hid it. That list drives the program views, the coverage totals and scoring. People reading weights see more: the /program page has a filter ("show competencies at least somewhat / important / very important"), and the wiki's career-target pages list every competency that is at least somewhat important, grouped by importance with the reason beside each. Faculty pin, hide or reset competencies per target on /admin/membership. Every change to these lists runs one at a time, so a removal can never be undone by an update running at the same moment.

In the code: lib/catalog/career-weights.ts (weights, which source wins); lib/catalog/membership.ts (the rule and the bands); lib/db/membership-queries.ts (rebuilds career_target_membership under a database lock); scripts/catalog/recompute-career-weights.ts (O*NET and employer weights, rerun after bundle reviews, blind checks and employer submissions); scripts/catalog/import-ai-weights.ts (loads the AI draft); /admin/membership. Required K/U/D depths per target are not filled yet. Full mechanics: How the Competency Catalog Works.

Where the two sides meet

Because both sides score K, U and D on the same rubric and refer to the same catalog competency, a career's required depth and the program's evidenced depth for that competency can be compared directly. The part of the system that does this is called here the comparison layer (in project documents, the "sufficiency seam" or demand/coverage layer). It works like this:

  • Demand: for each competency, the required depth across a career's positions is averaged, giving more weight to the employers faculty judge most representative. The results are stored in their own table. In the code: career_target_demand.
  • Attainment: for the same competency, the deepest level any course reached (the "ordinal MAX" from §3 — levels are ranks, so they are not added together).
  • Gap: demand minus attainment. Where a career needs a 4 and graduates' courses show a 2, the gap of 2 is where the curriculum falls short.
Built vs. not yet switched on

Built and running: both kinds of capture. On the course side: the CourseCapture process, the shared catalog with per-competency scoring, and the program coverage map. On the demand side: Position Capture (the six-page flow, position records that cannot be edited, the combined summary). Of the three demand-side rules, only the evidence rule is checked by the software when a Position Profile is saved; day-one anchoring is an instruction to the AI, and the link to a catalog competency may be left empty.

Built, but switched off: the comparison layer exists from start to finish. It includes the calculation (weighted demand against the deepest course level); a rule against inventing gaps when data is missing, so a competency no employer has asked for is reported as "no demand," and one that is demanded but has no course data as "no coverage"; a table for the demand figures (present in the live database, empty); a fixed procedure that fills it from positions; and a read-only panel on each career target's page. It is covered by automated tests and does nothing until it is switched on. So Q1 sufficiency can be computed, once the decision is made to switch it on and the first employer data arrives.

In the code: setting DEMAND_COVERAGE_SEAM; labels no_demand, no_coverage; table career_target_demand.

§ 5The Pages People Use

Every page is served from one secure web address, https://gcworkflow.clemson.edu:8443, on the Clemson network. There are three kinds of access: faculty pages, which need you to be signed in (with a personal access link, or the department login while it remains on); public read-only pages, which need nothing; and the partner survey, which uses a personal link for each employer. §9 explains sign-in.

CourseCapture
HTTPS · Faculty (signed in, limited to the course) · /capture/<code>

An interview, one course at a time, that produces an evidence-based Course Outcome Profile. It opens with a materials step: three boxes (Syllabus / Canvas / Other materials), each showing where every file came from and whether it is ready; the instructor cannot continue without a syllabus. Next, the instructor sets how closely each material is read. The tool reads Canvas exports, Google Drive files and uploads. During the interview, the AI looks up passages in that course's own materials. For a course taught as a paired lecture and lab, the Canvas box has one import slot per course code, and materials from both go into the same index. The instructor corrects and reviews the draft; approval saves a dated snapshot that cannot be edited. A short how-to with screenshots is at /curriculum/howto.

Explore
HTTPS · Faculty (signed in) · /explore/<code>

A chat for thinking through a proposed change to one course. The AI draws on the wiki, an index of materials across all courses, and the course's neighbors (its prerequisites and the courses that build on it). It gets numbers from one place only — an impact-estimate tool (estimate_impact) that returns a scenario card: the change, the predicted changes to K/U/D, and a list of effects on earlier courses, later courses and career targets. These predictions are estimates, not measurements. Scenarios can be saved, compared and adopted. Adopting writes the predicted depths, together with the revised design, into the course's capture draft as intended targets — a field kept separate from measured depths.

Program Coverage Matrix
HTTPS · Faculty (signed in) · /program

A heat map built from the approved snapshots of the career-building courses — the GC Core plus the major requirements every student takes, which the tool marks as building toward a career. There is one row per course and instructor, and one column per competency of each career target; color shows depth. Clicking a cell shows the evidence quote, the AI's confidence and its reasoning, and lets you flag the reading to dispute it. Scores come from the per-competency scoring step (§4); refreshing out-of-date scores and resolving flags are admin-only. A paired lecture/lab course appears as one row under a combined label (for example GC 3460/3461). This view answers the breadth half of Q1.

Scaffolding Strip
HTTPS · Faculty (signed in) · /program/scaffolding

From Phase 1B of the plan. For each career target, a heat map of depth, of the conditions for productive struggle, and of signs of structured reflection, calculated by fixed rules (no AI) from approved snapshots. It points out brittle scaffolds: courses that expect mastery of a competency no earlier course actually introduces. An additional AI-written commentary (Phase 1B Stage 2) is designed but not built.

Course Catalog
HTTPS · Faculty (signed in) · /courses

A list of all catalog courses, grouped by level, showing whether each has been captured, when, what data exists, and a button to ask about it. New courses are added from /admin/access by picking them from the Clemson catalog. Each row links to /courses/[code], the prerequisite page, where faculty propose and confirm which prerequisite course supplies which skill, and read the resulting gap analysis (§11).

Wiki + Ask

§ 6How a Course Profile Is Built

A Course Outcome Profile is produced in three phases: reading the materials, the interview, and drafting the profile. The main design decision is that the AI looks up specific passages while it works (in project documents, "agentic retrieval") rather than being handed all the materials at once. The reason: when an AI sees everything at once, a finding can come from a general impression of the whole pile, and nobody can check which document it rests on. When the AI has to look up passages, every finding it reports is tied to a specific passage that can be cited and checked.

Phase A — Reading the materials

When an instructor adds materials (a Canvas import, a file upload, a Google Drive file, a YouTube transcript), each one goes through the steps below before the interview starts. This happens in the background: the upload returns at once, and the page checks back until the material is ready.

  1. Convert to text

    Each file is turned into clean text. PDFs and Office files go through Docling, a document reader (§9), which also produces descriptions of figures using an AI that can read images. Scanned PDFs are detected and run through text recognition (OCR). Older Office formats are first converted by LibreOffice, a free office suite run without its screen. Spreadsheets are cleaned of empty rows, broken tables and embedded images stored as text, which can make a file 20–30 times longer than its actual content.

  2. Remove student identifiers, and hold back risky files

    Two safeguards. First, a privacy scrub runs inside the one function that stores extracted text, so nothing reaches the database, the summaries or the search index without being scrubbed. It uses fixed patterns (not AI) to replace Clemson CUIDs, Social Security numbers and email addresses with placeholders, and records how many it replaced. Email addresses are kept in syllabi, which are public documents. Names are deliberately not removed automatically: an AI name-removal step was tried and dropped because it also removed inventors, historical figures and industry leaders. Second, a materials policy sets aside files whose names suggest gradebook or roster data. A separate check still reads file contents to assign a risk level for display, but it no longer holds files back, since the scrub has already removed the identifiers. The same scrub runs on every wiki page before it is written, and generated profile text is scrubbed again before it appears on the public /view pages. In the code: lib/privacy/; counts in course_materials.redactions.

  3. Read at the chosen level of detail, and index for search

    Each material is read at one of three levels of detail (its tier). The tool proposes a level at the triage step: for Canvas content by fixed rules (the syllabus and graded work get the highest level, slide decks the middle), and for other files by a small AI classifier. The instructor can move any material to another level, and it is re-read at that level.

    High: the text is split along its headings into sections, and each section into short, slightly overlapping passages, so that no idea is cut off at a passage boundary. Each passage gets a one-line note saying where it sits in the document, so a search can match on context as well as wording (a technique Anthropic published as "contextual retrieval"). The passages are stored with the rest of that course's materials in the search index (§9), apart from other courses. Reading the same file again replaces its passages rather than adding duplicates.

    Middle: instead of passages, the tool writes summaries — a note for each slide of a slide deck (by an AI that reads images), a summary for each section of other documents. These summaries are indexed and cited as the whole document, not as a specific passage.

    Background: one summary of the whole material, stored as a single entry.

    To make passages searchable by meaning rather than exact wording, each is turned into a list of numbers that represents its meaning (an embedding). This is always done by a free campus AI service. The location notes and summaries are also written by a free campus model first, with a paid OpenAI model as an automatic backup (§9).

    In the code: classifier material-classify; splitter lib/capture/chunker.ts, three levels, detail passages of ~500 tokens with ~100-token overlap; embeddings qwen3-embedding-4b (2560 numbers per passage); notes and summaries gptoss-120b, backup gpt-5.4-mini; search index Weaviate, one tenant per course; passage IDs are SHA-256 of fileName|kind|position|sample.

  4. Summarize

    At the same time, each material gets a structured summary: shorter, but faithful, written for the interviewing AI to keep in view throughout. The Canvas syllabus page, assignments and quiz content are kept word for word, because the AI needs the exact assignment wording and point values; long reference materials are summarized. The summary stands in for the full text in the interview, and the instructor can switch any material back to its full text.

When reading is finished, an AI makes one quiet review pass over the whole set of materials. It speaks up only about real problems — no syllabus, several files automatically set aside, summaries that are nearly empty — and most of the time says nothing.

Phase B — The interview

The interview is run by an AI that can use tools — it can stop to look things up — rather than one that simply replies. On each turn it:

  1. Starts from what it always has in view

    Without looking anything up, the AI always has: its instructions; the course's catalog entry (description, prerequisites, learning objectives, major projects, skills students should bring in); a summary of each material; a short brief on the related courses (their prerequisites in the Clemson catalog, and the captured profiles of those prerequisites), put together by fixed rules; and a briefing on the session so far (how ready the profile is, recent findings, and the instructor's last message from any earlier session), quoted exactly.

  2. Decides whether it needs to look something up

    If the current point needs specific evidence beyond the summaries, it looks things up — at most one or two times — before replying.

  3. Looks up passages if needed

    It has three lookup tools: one that lists the course's materials, one that fetches a specific passage, and one that searches the course's materials by both meaning and keywords. Every passage it gets back carries an identifier, which is what it later cites. In the code: list_materials, fetch_material_section, search_materials (hybrid vector + BM25 keyword search).

  4. Replies in a fixed format

    Each reply contains: the most important finding (the biggest gap in evidence, or a conflict between sources); one focused question; citations (passage identifiers, or identifiers of the instructor's own earlier messages); and a readiness signal (0–100, areas covered and remaining, and whether there is enough to draft the profile). When the instructor is done, one button asks for a last important question and then offers to draft the profile.

  5. Saves the turn

    Every message, from the instructor and from the AI, is added to the session's record in the database. Messages can be added but never replaced: the database refuses a second message with the same place in the conversation. The full conversation stays linked to any snapshot made from it.

Over the interview, the AI covers seven areas: whether prerequisites are sufficient, objectives versus evidence, conflicts between sources, the level of thinking required, threshold concepts, the foundational dispositions, and references to content it cannot see. It takes them up in whatever order the materials suggest. Replies appear word by word as they are written, so the instructor is not left waiting 3–7 seconds for each one.

Voice input

The microphone button in the interview sends audio to speech-to-text software (Whisper) running on the Mac itself (§9). Speaking helps with longer explanations — what a capstone actually requires, or what a rubric line means. The transcription replaces the text in progress, and the instructor edits it before sending. The microphone works because faculty pages are served over HTTPS on the campus address, and browsers allow microphone access only on secure pages. Audio stays on the Mac unless the operator deliberately turns on the paid OpenAI backup.

Phase C — Drafting the profile

When the instructor asks for the profile, a separate AI call — not the interview AI — reads the full interview transcript and the materials and drafts the Course Outcome Profile in one pass. It contains: competencies (5–15 technical plus the 6 baseline foundational (§3), each with K/U/D depths, evidence quotes and reasoning), what students are expected to bring in, a verification summary, and interview notes. Every finding is labeled with its source and carries citations (§7).

Correcting and reviewing the draft

The instructor then works through the draft in two steps. Correcting: section by section (apparent outcomes, what students are expected to bring in, what they leave with), the instructor says in their own words what is wrong; the AI proposes specific edits, which the instructor accepts, adjusts or rejects. Reviewing: each competency appears as a card in plain language — what students know, understand and can do, in words rather than K/U/D codes — with "Looks right" and "Needs adjusting" buttons. Lowering an estimate takes effect immediately. Raising one requires the instructor to name the evidence; the change and its reason are saved permanently in the snapshot as an instructor override. In the code: AI function reconcile-feedback.

Stress test

A second AI reviewer, whose only job is to find problems, runs automatically once after each draft (and again on request). It checks whether claims have enough citations, whether the evidence quotes support the claims they are attached to, whether the reasoning matches the scores, whether source labels are accurate, and whether claims that objectives are misaligned match what the instructor actually said. Cards it has concerns about are marked "Worth a look" during review. It is advisory only: its results are not saved with the profile or the snapshot. It is there to prompt a second look, not to decide.

Snapshot

When the instructor approves, the system saves a snapshot: a numbered, dated record that can no longer be edited, holding the profile, the time, the AI model that produced it, the instructor's name and a link to the interview transcript. Snapshots are what every later view reads. Drafts can still be changed; only approved snapshots feed program-level analysis. Saving a snapshot starts three separate background tasks; none of them can delay or undo the save:

  • the wiki update (§9);
  • an update of that course's share of the cross-course materials index (§9);
  • an objective assessment guide that quotes each learning objective from the syllabus and names the Canvas assignments and rubric rows that measure it. A fixed check then removes any assignment or rubric name that does not appear in the course's own materials, so the guide cannot point to work that does not exist. In the code: AI function objective-evidence-guide.

§ 7Where Each Finding Comes From

Every finding in a Course Outcome Profile carries a label saying where it came from, plus citations. The software checks this when the profile is saved; it is not optional.

Source labels

There are three:

  • materials — every citation points to a specific passage in the course materials.
  • instructor — every citation points to something the instructor said in the interview (and that is not in the materials).
  • inferred — no direct citations, or only indirect ones. The weakest kind of support.

The label is not chosen by the AI. The software works it out from the kinds of citation attached: all passages → materials; all instructor messages → instructor; a mix, or none → inferred. So anyone can check the label against the citations. On the review screens the three labels appear as "From course materials," "From your interview," and "AI's inference." On top of this, when a profile is displayed the tool assigns each score a credibility level (claimed / materials-supported / artifact-verified). A high score backed only by the instructor's word is marked as such, but not blocked.

Citations point to real sources

Every citation is either a passage identifier (pointing to a specific piece of text in that course's search index) or a message identifier (pointing to a specific thing the instructor said). Both the drafting instructions and the software's check refuse a citation that is only a quoted phrase with no identifier: the profile is rejected and drafted again. This prevents a known failure in which an AI paraphrases what it "remembers" instead of pointing to a source that can be checked. On the review screens, every source label is a button that opens the actual passage or instructor message, so faculty can see for themselves whether the evidence says what the finding claims. In the code: Zod schema validation on citations.

The oldest profiles

Snapshots made with the first version of the process, before passage lookup, do not carry source labels or resolvable citations. They are shown with a banner noting this, and their citation panel shows the quoted text only. They remain valid historical records and still feed the program views; they simply lack the source tracing the current process produces.

§ 8Instructors and Time

One capture per instructor

Different faculty can teach the same course very differently. One instructor's GC 3800 may develop Color Management at D=4 through a semester-long production project; another's may develop it at D=2 through lab exercises and a final exam. Both are valid findings: a depth score describes what students do under that instructor, not an average across sections. Capturing one and presenting it as "the course" would hide differences that may matter for advising and review.

So the same course can be captured separately for each instructor. Each session starts by choosing the instructor from a faculty list, and that name is stored with every message and with the resulting snapshot. The public course list shows "by [instructor]"; the read-only profile page notes how many other captures of the course exist, if any.

Snapshots cannot be edited

Once approved, a snapshot cannot be changed. It is a dated record of what the course was shown to develop at that time, by that instructor, from that session's materials. This is deliberate: curricula drift slowly over years, and the only way to see the drift is to keep the dated records as they pile up. Faculty can capture a course again as often as they like; each new snapshot is added to the record. The Program Coverage Matrix automatically uses the newest snapshot per course that has not been retired.

A snapshot can be retired — hidden from the program views because it has been replaced or describes a version of the course that no longer exists. Retired snapshots stay in the database as history and can still be opened directly, but they no longer feed program analysis. There is no way in the app to delete a snapshot outright.

Differences between instructors

When several instructors have captured the same course, the Program Coverage Matrix shows one row per course and instructor — each the instructor's latest snapshot that has not been retired, labeled "by [instructor]". Differences between instructors are visible side by side, rather than hidden behind whichever capture happened last. Later work will add side-by-side comparisons of snapshots; the instructor name on each snapshot and the "N other captures on file" note remain on the read-only pages.

§ 9The Technical Pieces

The tool is a single web application running on one Mac Studio on the Clemson network. The database, the search index and the document reader run beside it on the same machine; a GPU server on campus (a computer built for AI workloads) does the heaviest document reading. One codebase, one database, one machine. This section goes through each piece: what it does, and why it was set up that way.

The web server and the one address

The application listens only on the Mac itself, so nothing on the network can reach it directly. A second program, Caddy, a web server that acts as the front door, sits in front of it: it handles the secure (HTTPS) connection at https://gcworkflow.clemson.edu:8443 using a Clemson-issued certificate and passes requests through to the application. That is the only way in, and it works only on the Clemson network. Caddy also answers any unencrypted request with a pointer to the secure address, so nothing is served unencrypted. HTTPS matters for more than privacy: browsers allow microphone access only on secure pages, which is what makes voice input possible in the interview.

In the code: Next.js 15 (App Router), run in production mode (next start from a prebuilt bundle) by launchd, bound to 127.0.0.1:3000. Caddy runs as the system LaunchDaemon edu.clemson.gc.caddy, as the unprivileged user _gcproxy, from /usr/local/gc-caddy, with a Clemson InCommon certificate; it also holds the network-facing side of port 3000 and returns a 426 error for unencrypted /api requests.

Sign-in and permissions

Every request passes through one gate. A few paths are public and pass straight through: the landing page, the read-only course pages, the wiki, the partner pages, and two addresses that other programs (not people) call with a secret token. Everything else requires a grant — a record of who a person is and what they may do — presented in one of three ways:

  • A personal access link. An administrator creates it for one person, choosing which courses it covers (or all of them), whether its holder may add courses, and, if wanted, an expiry date. The database keeps only a scrambled fingerprint of the link, not the link itself, so someone with a copy of the database still could not sign in. When the person opens the link, the browser is signed in for up to 30 days and the secret part of the link is removed from the address bar.
  • Staying signed in on later visits (a sign-in cookie in the browser). Revoking the grant signs the person out.
  • The shared department login (a single shared username and password), which allows capture and adding courses on every course, but not admin. One setting turns it off everywhere; it stays on until every instructor has a personal link, after which a visitor who is not signed in sees a "Sign in with your link" page.

Permissions follow one rule table, and anything the table does not allow is refused. Any valid grant can read the faculty pages. Changing a course requires capture permission for that course. The admin pages — and resolving flags, refreshing coverage scores and changing model settings — require an all-courses grant with admin permission, which only a personal link can carry; the last such link cannot be revoked from the browser, so administrators cannot lock themselves out. Partner pages use a separate link per employer, checked against the partner's record, so partners need no account.

In the code: middleware.ts (Node runtime, because its checks query Postgres) → lib/auth/gate.ts; public paths /, /view/*, /wiki/*, partner paths, and the bearer-token endpoints /api/mcp and /api/curriculum/search. Links issued on /admin/access or with pnpm access:grant; hashes in access_grants; cookie gc_session (HttpOnly, Secure). Department login is HTTP Basic Auth, switched by DEPARTMENT_LOGIN. Rule table lib/auth/authorize.ts (default-deny); capabilities capture and admin; admin paths /admin/**, /api/admin/**; partner tokens in partners.magic_token.

Database

Every record the tool keeps is in one database (Postgres 17) on the Mac: courses and their materials, snapshots and interview messages, the competency catalog and which careers use which competencies, coverage scores, alumni occupation mixes and the O*NET copy, bundle cards and their review queue, the Clemson catalog's prerequisites, partner and position records, prerequisite links, syllabus-claimed (intended) coverage, access grants, faculty flags, AI settings and AI costs.

A course code such as GC 3460 is stored whole and also split into its parts, so courses can be sorted and filtered reliably; the whole code is what other records point to. For a paired lecture and lab, the second code is recorded alongside the first, and each material notes which of the two it came from. Changes to the database's structure are made by numbered scripts, applied in order; the larger catalog changes were written by hand, each with a script to check the result and steps to undo it.

In the code: Postgres.app on 127.0.0.1:5433 (not the default 5432, to avoid clashing with a standard install), database gc_curriculum, managed by launchd (com.gc.postgres). Course columns prefix / integer course_number / number_suffix beside the code primary key (the target of all 17 foreign keys); paired codes in course_codes; course_materials.source_code (null = the primary code). Connection pool in lib/db/client.ts (node-postgres); migrations via Drizzle.

AI services, and switching between them

The tool can use four AI services, and one setting chooses which. Every AI request goes through a single piece of code that reads that setting, so switching services means changing one line, with no other changes to the tool. That matters for an institution: if a contract, price or policy changes, the tool can move to another service without being rebuilt. The services:

  • OpenAI (in use now). Smaller, cheaper models do simple, frequent jobs and a stronger model does the rest (§10); the stronger model was chosen after a blind comparison of model outputs against the evidence. Requests go through the OpenAI connection run by Clemson RCD (the university's research computing group), under the campus account, rather than directly to OpenAI. OpenAI's strict output mode is used, so every answer comes back in exactly the expected structure. In the code: AI_PROVIDER=openai; light size gpt-5.4-mini, default and heavy gpt-6.1-sol at low reasoning effort; strict mode requires every property in required, and every schema is written to that rule; OPENAI_BASE_URL; Vercel AI SDK v6 for tool use and streaming; the tool-using AIs on gpt-6.x models use the stateless Responses API (store: false).
  • Anthropic (claude-sonnet-4-6). Can read PDF files directly; an available alternative. (AI_PROVIDER=anthropic)
  • Campus — Clemson RCD's own AI cluster at llm.rcd.clemson.edu/v1. Its default model is fast, and larger models can be chosen for individual requests. It is free; its cost is recorded as 0. Because the default model does not reliably give a structured answer and use tools in the same request, the tool asks it in two separate steps. (AI_PROVIDER=campus; default qwen3.6-35b-a3b-fp8, MoE, ~3B active params, ~180 tok/s; larger glm-5.1-fp8, deepseek-v4-pro)
  • Local — omlx, an AI server running on the Mac's own graphics processor (M4 Max) with Qwen3.6 models. No cost per request, and no data leaves the Mac. omlx loads a model the first time it is asked for and keeps it in memory. It is installed separately and not managed by the project's service manager. (AI_PROVIDER=local; localhost:8000/v1)

In the code: lib/ai/provider.ts, getProvider(), returning OpenAI / Anthropic / Local / Campus / Fake (Fake is for tests).

Making the search index always uses the campus service, whichever AI service is selected for everything else. Two more high-volume jobs also try the free campus service first — writing each passage's location note and summarizing each material — and switch to a small OpenAI model if the campus service fails. Other jobs cannot yet be moved to campus models this way, because the size settings store OpenAI model names; changing that is deferred. In the code: campus model gptoss-120b with reasoning off, backup gpt-5.4-mini; lib/ai/embeddings.ts calls the campus /embeddings endpoint with qwen3-embedding-4b (2560-dim); functions chunk-contextualize and material-digest via lib/ai/analyze/chunk-llm-provider.ts; switch off with CHUNK_LLM_SKIP_CAMPUS.

The search index for course materials

When an instructor uploads course materials, the tool breaks them into short passages and stores each course's passages separately, in Weaviate, a search database that runs on the Mac. A search finds passages both by meaning (using the embeddings described in §6) and by keywords. Keeping each course separate matters for the interview: when the AI asks an instructor about GC 3460, it can only draw on GC 3460's materials, so evidence from another course cannot leak into that course's scores.

The index holds the short passages, which are what a search matches, and the larger sections they come from. When a search finds a passage, the tool also fetches the section around it, so the AI reads a coherent piece of the document rather than a clipped fragment. The citation panel in the review screens works the same way: it looks up a cited passage by its identifier and shows it.

In the code: Weaviate v1.37.5 in Docker (OrbStack, restart policy unless-stopped), data directory on the host, anonymous access, 127.0.0.1:8090 HTTP + :50051 gRPC. Classes MaterialChunk (embedded) and MaterialSection (no embedding); tenant names coursecapture-<slug> (e.g. coursecapture-gc-4800); search hybridSearch (BM25 + vector); section fetch is "small-to-big" enrichment; lookup fetchChunkById; passages ~500 tokens. lib/capture/vector-store.ts uses Weaviate when VECTOR_STORE=weaviate, otherwise an in-memory store for development and tests; this ingestion path runs only when COURSECAPTURE_V2_INGESTION=1.

Searching materials across all courses

Keeping courses separate causes a problem for the program-wide question tools (/ask and the Ask tab in Explore). They could read the wiki summaries and the scores, but not the original course materials. So a question like "where do students practice color correction?" could not be answered from the syllabi and assignments themselves.

To fix this, the tool keeps one additional shared copy of every course's passages for program-wide searches. Nothing is re-processed; the passages are copied as they are, along with the meaning fingerprints already made for them. Each search result is a short excerpt with a note saying which course and which file it came from, so answers stay traceable to a source. One limit: a cross-course result returns the excerpt, not the surrounding section. To read more context, open that course's own materials. (Project documents call this shared copy the "spine.") In the code: shared copy = coursecapture-program tenant (tenantForProgram()) in the MaterialChunk Weaviate class; per-course copies are tenants named coursecapture-<slug>.

What is included. A course's share is rebuilt from the database and includes only materials that finished processing, are not excluded, and are not retired. Anything a reviewer has retired or excluded (for privacy, policy or irrelevance), or that was automatically set aside, drops out at the next update. Each copied passage records when its material was uploaded and which snapshot triggered the update, so a result can be traced back to its source file and the capture that made it current. In the code: listIndexableMaterialsForCourse, filter indexing_status = 'ready' ∧ ignored = false ∧ retired_at IS NULL; fields uploadedAt, snapshotId.

When it updates. Whenever a snapshot is saved, the same background step that updates the wiki then replaces that course's share of the index: it deletes the old share and copies in the current set. A full rebuild exists for the first fill and for repair if the index drifts out of step. As with the wiki, failures are logged and ignored, so updating the index can never block or undo saving a snapshot. In the code: refreshProgramIndex(courseCode, { snapshotId }) in lib/capture/program-index.ts, called after updateWikiForSnapshot; deletion via store.deleteByCourse(tenantForProgram(), courseCode); full rebuild POST /api/admin/program-index/rebuild → rebuildProgramIndex.

The search tool. The curriculum chat, and outside AI assistants (see below), search this index with one tool that works three ways: across all courses; within one course; or as a comparison that limits how many results any one course can contribute, so a course with many files cannot crowd out a smaller one when the question is "how do courses treat this differently?" Each result gives the course, the file name, the passage, its location note and the passage identifier for citing. In the code: search_curriculum in lib/ai/wiki/curriculum-search-tool.ts, running hybridSearch on the program tenant; options courseCode and perCourse: true (via diversifyByCourse); results carry courseCode, fileName, the chunk text, contextBlurb, chunkId.

Why the index feeds the AI instead of answering on its own. Search by meaning alone is not accurate enough to answer questions directly. Xu et al. (2025), benchmarking curriculum-to-skill matching, found that pure embedding-similarity retrieval reaches only ~24% precision at identifying genuinely relevant skills, whereas retrieve-then-reason (RAG — retrieve candidates by embedding, then let an LLM judge them) reaches ~70–82%, a three-to-four-fold lift that holds across document types. In this tool the index does the first half (finding candidates) and the curriculum chat does the second (judging them). Answering from the index alone would be the weaker approach; sending its results through the AI is the stronger one (arXiv:2505.02324).

The curriculum chat draws on three sources: the stored data (tools that read the coverage scores and prerequisite links), the wiki (written summaries), and the original materials (the cross-course index). The order of authority is fixed: the database is the record; the wiki and the index are both produced from it — the wiki as written summary, the index as raw evidence. A planned concept layer (Phase 2) would build on the index, writing "how each course treats this concept" pages into the wiki's concepts/ section. It is not built. In the code: tools coverage_for_target, prereq_chain, read_wiki, search_wiki, search_curriculum.

Reading documents

Files are converted to text by Docling, IBM's document reader, which runs as a small separate service. The main copy runs on the department's GPU server on campus; a copy on the Mac takes over automatically if the main one fails. Docling handles PDF, Word, PowerPoint, Excel, CSV, web pages and images, recognizing page layout and tables and running text recognition on scans. It does this much better than the simpler PDF reader built into the app (unpdf), which is kept as a backup, and it handles formats that reader cannot. An optional step describes figures in slides and papers using an AI that reads images — evidence that text-only reading misses. Per-slide notes for slide decks are produced by a model on the same GPU server, reached through a small relay on the Mac, with a local model as backup. Docling serves both course materials and the job descriptions uploaded in Position Capture.

In the code: docling-serve, selected by PDF_PARSER=docling; main instance DOCLING_URL; local fallback 127.0.0.1:5001 (launchd com.gc.docling-serve, CPU mode on Apple Silicon) via DOCLING_FALLBACK_URL; figure descriptions DOCLING_VLM_*; relay launchd com.gc.dgx-forward.

Speech to text

Audio (the instructor's microphone in the interview, and YouTube videos without captions) is transcribed by trying three options in order and using the first that works:

  1. omlx, the AI server already running on the Mac — used only if it answers within 100ms, and fast because the model is already loaded;
  2. the mlx_whisper program with the whisper-large-v3-turbo model on the Mac's GPU — free, on the machine, ~1/60 realtime;
  3. OpenAI's Whisper-1 service — a last resort, used only if the operator has deliberately allowed it. Otherwise transcription fails rather than send audio off the machine. (WHISPER_BACKEND=openai, or WHISPER_OPENAI_FALLBACK=1 when the local program is missing)

For YouTube videos without captions, the audio is downloaded and reduced to 16 kHz mono first (yt-dlp + ffmpeg), then transcribed the same way; such transcripts have (Whisper) in the file name.

Where materials are stored

Uploaded files are kept on the Mac's own disk, which is enough for a single-machine setup. When the triage step is enabled, approving a snapshot also deletes that course's original uploaded files; the extracted text, summaries and search index entries are kept. In the code: ~/.local/share/gc-curriculum-tool/materials/, served via /api/storage/materials/<key>; triage flag COURSECAPTURE_TRIAGE=1; deletion recorded as raw_cleared.

The official record of every material is its entry in the database. Everything else — the search index, the wiki text, the cross-course index — is produced from that entry and can be rebuilt from it. The entry holds the extracted, scrubbed text, how many identifiers were removed, a fingerprint used to spot duplicate uploads, whether it is the syllabus, and five settings that control what later steps can see:

  • Processing status — waiting, queued, being processed, ready, failed or skipped. Only materials marked ready go into the search index and the cross-course index.
  • Level of detail — chosen at triage: high (split into passages, given location notes, indexed), middle (summaries indexed and cited as the whole document), or background (one summary as a single entry). Faculty can change it.
  • Excluded — kept away from all AI use, for policy reasons or because it is irrelevant. Neither the curriculum chat nor the cross-course index sees it.
  • Automatically set aside — marked, with the reason, when the materials policy holds a file back (for example a spreadsheet that looks like a gradebook). The mark is kept as a record even if the instructor later lets the file through.
  • Retired — "the course no longer teaches this material." This is different from excluded, which is about AI safety and irrelevance rather than changes to the course. A retired material drops out of the search index and the cross-course index at the next snapshot update, and stays in the database as history.

In the code: table course_materials; SHA-256 digest; fields indexing_status ('pending' → 'queued' → 'indexing' → 'ready', or 'failed' / 'skipped'), tier ('high' / 'middle' / 'background'), ignored, auto_set_aside, retired_at.

The curriculum wiki (a separate repository)

The wiki is a separate collection of text files kept under version control (Git), in a repository called gc-curriculum-wiki, which an AI keeps up to date. The approach follows Karpathy's raw → compile → query LLM-wiki and Monteiro's "AI second brain" writing. The point those sources make is that the compile step — turning raw material that never changes into organized, linked pages — is the step most such systems skip, which leaves a search box over an empty shelf, or a chat AI that makes things up to fill the gap. This wiki is that compile step. It has two layers: a raw/ layer (the snapshots and interview transcripts, never changed) and a layer of written pages that can be regenerated at any time (courses/, competencies/, targets/, concepts/, plus index.md). Version control means every regeneration is recorded as a change that can be inspected and undone, and gives the curriculum chat a set of pages it can browse and cite without loading the whole database. In the code: cloned beside the app at WIKI_REPO_PATH.

This is running, not planned. When it runs: once when a snapshot is saved (course pages, concept pages and the index), and again after coverage scoring finishes. The second run is what makes competency and career-target pages possible at all, since they are built from coverage scores that do not exist yet when the snapshot is saved. It runs in the background, and a failure is logged and ignored, so it can never break a capture or a scoring run. Only one module writes to the repository, and it handles one update at a time, because two updates at once would collide; if the shared copy has changed meanwhile, it retries once. Pages are written six at a time, because a full set can exceed thirty pages, and asking the model for all of them in one reply makes the reply too long and the request can stall without any error. When the whole wiki needs rebuilding, a one-pass refresh regenerates each course and program page once, instead of once per capture.

In the code: updateWikiForSnapshot (lib/ai/wiki/update.ts) with the heavy-size wiki-update function; writeAndPush (lib/wiki/git-ops.ts) does pull/write/commit/push through an in-process queue with one pull-rebase retry; triggered by regenerateWikiInBackground; second trigger after snapshot_competency_coverage is written; WIKI_PAGES_PER_CALL = 6; full refresh scripts/wiki/refresh-all.ts.

Beside the generated pages, the wiki has a hand-written research/ section: pages on AI and GC careers, each built around a question, written for the curriculum committee. The automatic generators never write there; it changes only through a separate step limited to that folder, and only if the wiki's checker passes (In the code: scripts/wiki/sync-research.ts.) Every generated page passes through the privacy scrub before it is written.

The public copy. Because the app is campus-only, the public wiki is published elsewhere: a script copies the app's public wiki pages into a static site at tonkin.people.clemson.edu/pages/gc-wiki/, and a public visitor to /wiki is sent there; faculty keep the live pages. The public documents, including this one, are published the same way to tonkin.people.clemson.edu/pages/gc-curriculum/, only from the approved main branch, so unfinished work is never published. Both copies are refreshed nightly at 05:00 and whenever the wiki is updated, and a failed refresh posts an alert to the project dashboard. The older GitHub Pages addresses now redirect to these copies. In the code: scripts/wiki/snapshot-public.mjs; redirect in lib/wiki/public-copy.ts; refresh scripts/publish/clemson-refresh.sh (launchd com.gc.clemson-publish, plus a trigger file written by wiki updates).

What the AI writes, and what it does not (the key design decision)

Each wiki page has two parts. The fixed structure is produced by ordinary code, with no AI, and it is deliberately the larger part. It includes: which pages a new snapshot affects (worked out directly from the coverage scores), every K/U/D depth table, the lists of "which courses build this" and "which competencies feed this target," and where links point. The AI writes only the narrative: summary paragraphs and observations across courses — the part that needs judgment.

Why: the less the AI writes, the less room there is for it to invent things. Regenerating a page always produces the same structure; only the wording of the prose varies slightly between runs (the AI is run at a low randomness setting, and wording drift is accepted, structural drift is not). There is also a security benefit. The AI says which pages it is returning, but the software writes a page only if it is one the fixed structure asked for, and discards and logs anything else. So a misbehaving or manipulated AI cannot write to arbitrary files in the wiki.

In the code: computeAffectedPages, reading the target_coverage view over snapshot_competency_coverage; the path guard is in update.ts (review finding F8).

How links between pages are made and kept current

Every wiki page has a predictable name: a course page is named after its course code, a competency or career-target page after its title. So the AI can link to a page just by writing its name, without checking whether the page exists yet. The AI is told to link every mention of a course, competency, career target or concept. Each page also lists its related pages at the top, and those lists are recalculated from the database every time the page is rewritten.

Links are not stored anywhere. Each time a page is displayed, every link is checked against the wiki's files: if the page exists, the link works; if not, it is shown as visibly broken. This has two intended effects. First, broken links fix themselves: a link can be written before its page exists, the AI treats a broken link as a sign the page should be created on a later pass, and as soon as the page appears every link to it works. Second, links in both directions stay consistent without bookkeeping: a course page lists its competencies and each competency page lists its courses, and both come from the same coverage scores. They can briefly disagree if one page is rewritten before the other.

In the code: links written as [[slug]] or [[slug|label]] (e.g. gc-4800, brand-strategy, brand-strategist); header lists contributing_courses, develops_competencies, contributes_to_targets; resolved at display time by resolveWikilinks (lib/wiki/markdown-helpers.ts) against the four page folders, linking to /wiki/<type>/<slug>; no back-link index.

Safeguards on the wiki

The wiki has the safeguards its design called for (design document: 2026-06-11-wiki-trust-hardening-design.md):

  • a checker (lint) that flags broken links, pages nothing links to, missing required sections, and concept pages with too few sources (gc-wiki-lint / pnpm wiki:lint);
  • a missing-page check: if the AI leaves out a page it was asked for, the page is requested again, and if it is still missing, the failure is logged loudly rather than passed over;
  • a fingerprint of each page's inputs (input_hash) stored on the page, so out-of-date pages can be detected;
  • evidence markers on each competency (·claimed / ·materials / ·artifact, worked out from each finding's source and citations), carried into the prose on course and competency pages;
  • protection against instructions hidden in snapshot text (prompt injection): text taken from snapshots is fenced off so the AI treats it as data, not as instructions;
  • an index page built by code, always written last with a list of every page in the run, so it cannot fall behind the pages written earlier;
  • the privacy scrub on every page written (§6).

Still open: a rule that a concept page needs at least two sources before it is created (today a concept page is created on the first qualifying mention; the checker flags thinly sourced ones, but they are not held back), and a /refresh-wiki command (like /refresh-state) that would use the input fingerprints to find and regenerate out-of-date pages on demand. Both are tracked in STATE.md under Deferred/debt.

Asking structured questions of the curriculum (wiki + stored data, open to other AI tools)

The wiki answers questions in prose. Beside it, two read-only tools answer questions about relationships directly from the stored data, which prose tends to blur:

  • coverage for a career target — which captured courses build toward a target, at what K/U/D depth, broken down by competency, each with a confidence and an evidence quote;
  • prerequisite chain — a course's prerequisites, their prerequisites in turn, and the courses that require it. Prerequisites come from the official Clemson catalog first, from the GC course sheet only for courses with no catalog entry, plus any prerequisite links faculty have confirmed.

Together with the three wiki tools (read, list, search) and the cross-course search, that makes six read-only tools. They are available in two places: the in-app /ask chat, and a server using MCP (Model Context Protocol, a standard way for AI assistants to call outside tools). The MCP server lets any MCP-capable AI assistant inside Clemson ask both prose and structural questions about the curriculum, drawing on the same evidence. It requires a shared secret token, refuses all requests if the token is not set, and is campus-only like the rest of the app. A plain web interface to the cross-course search serves a related voice-interview app, under its own token.

In the code: coverage_for_target and prereq_chain in lib/ai/wiki/graph-tools.ts (the calculation is separated from the database reads so it can be tested without a database); wiki tools read_wiki / list_wiki / search_wiki; MCP server /api/mcp (Streamable HTTP, shared-bearer auth, fail-closed); REST wrapper /api/curriculum/search.

The interviewing AI in CourseCapture uses these tools too. In full interview mode it gets four of the read-only program tools in addition to its search of the course's own materials. So while interviewing about one course, it can see the wider picture — who else covers a competency and at what depth, and the prerequisite chain leading into the course — and use it to ask about handoffs between courses or to check a depth claim. There is a firm rule here: information from other courses is reference, never evidence. Another course covering something never raises this course's K/U/D scores; this course's own materials and interview are the only basis for its scores. The result is the department's curriculum kept as a connected, evidence-based record — in prose and in structured data — that people (/wiki, /ask), outside AI assistants (MCP) and the capture process itself can all query.

§ 10Cost and the Role of AI

Matching the model to the job

Every AI task in the tool has a name — there are twenty-eight — and each is assigned a size: light, default or heavy. Each size uses a particular model, so frequent, simple tasks use a cheaper model and rarer, harder ones a stronger one. An administrator can override any task to a specific model and reasoning effort on /settings; several of the busiest tasks are set that way. In the code: lib/ai/function-settings.ts.

  • Light: routine processing of materials. Summarizing and sorting materials, writing location notes, the review pass after reading, breaking down prerequisite gaps, and pulling details out of job descriptions. These run often, so cost matters more than depth of reasoning; summarizing and location notes try the free campus model first (§9).
  • Default: conversations and drafting. The interviews (course and position), drafting and correcting profiles, Explore, the curriculum chat, the objective assessment guide, proposing prerequisite links, extracting intended skills and course details, career-target and bundle-card drafting, and the other Position Capture steps.
  • Heavy: program-wide or high-stakes judgments. Scoring every snapshot against the catalog, the stress-test reviewer, writing the wiki, and placing O*NET job elements under competencies. These run rarely, in batches, or where the stakes justify the cost. Today heavy uses the same model as default.

In the code: light = gpt-5.4-mini (e.g. material-digest, chunk-contextualize, material-classify); default = gpt-6.1-sol, low effort (capture-chat-agent, the older capture-chat, capture-scores, parse-profile-fields, extract-course-kud, position-rated-items, position-interview-agent, position-synthesis, and others); heavy = also gpt-6.1-sol (program coverage scoring, the stress test, wiki-update, bundle-map-onet).

Daily cost limit

Every AI request records its cost, and the day's total is compared with a daily limit, shown on /settings with a 14-day chart. The limit can work in two ways: block, where requests past the limit are refused with an explanatory error (429); or warn, where they go ahead and the overrun is logged. Production uses warn, so a faculty member is never cut off in the middle of a capture; instead, an hourly job opens a GitHub issue when the day's spending passes the limit, and closes it on a later day that stays under. The automatic feedback job also checks the limit before each run and stops when it is reached. In the code: table daily_cost; settings DAILY_COST_CAP_USD, DAILY_COST_CAP_MODE (block / warn); job com.gc.cost-cap-check.

"AI is your assistant, not your authority"

The way AI is used follows from one position: AI produces better-informed drafts for faculty to review, not final verdicts. Every depth score can be edited. Every recommendation is a starting point. Every stress-test concern is advisory. The AI's job is to take on the work of reading dozens of assignments and rubrics, point out patterns committee review tends to miss, and produce a reading specific enough to argue with. The analysis becomes part of the department's record only through faculty discussion.

§ 11How the Pieces Connect

A Course Outcome Profile is not the end product; it feeds several other views. This section traces each connection.

Snapshots → competency scores → Program Coverage Matrix

Each approved snapshot is scored once against the whole competency catalog. One AI request per snapshot reads the snapshot's competencies and, for every competency in the catalog, decides whether — and at what K/U/D depth — the snapshot's evidence supports that competency's own description. No career target is mentioned in the request, so a score means the same thing whichever career uses the competency. Scores become out of date when a snapshot or a competency description changes; an administrator refreshes them from /program, and each refresh also regenerates the affected wiki pages. In the code: results in snapshot_competency_coverage.

Why score again rather than reuse the snapshot's own scores? The snapshot is organized around what the course teaches; the catalog is organized around what careers require. The score that matters for the program is how deeply the snapshot shows the catalog competency being developed.

Career views combine these per-competency scores using the competencies each career target shows (pinned, or at least important). Every course visible to the program counts toward a career's totals, but the matrix shows one row per career-building course and instructor. Other courses — general education, free electives and other catalog requirements never meant to build toward a particular career — are still captured and can be browsed, but do not get a row in the matrix. In the code: career_target_membership; builds_to_career = true.

Snapshots → scaffolding analysis

The Scaffolding Strip reads the same snapshots with a different question: not "does the course develop this?" but "at what depth, and with what evidence of productive struggle and structured reflection?" Fixed rules (no AI) sort each cell into one of: well-scaffolded, top-heavy, bottom-heavy, coverage-only, brittle-scaffold, or not-addressed. A brittle scaffold — a later course expecting mastery of a competency no earlier course introduces — is the finding that leads to the most redesign work.

Snapshots → Explore

Explore reads the chosen course's snapshot together with those of its neighbors — the courses it relies on and the courses that rely on it, with their competencies and what they expect students to bring in — to reason about how a proposed change would ripple through the curriculum. Its impact estimates spell out the effects on earlier courses, later courses and careers. An adopted scenario goes back into the course's capture draft as intended targets, never as measured depths.

Snapshots → prerequisite gap analysis

Which courses are prerequisites of which comes from the official Clemson catalog, synced into the database: prerequisites, "before or alongside" requirements and corequisites, including "any one of these" groups. This catalog-first prerequisite map feeds the interview's brief on related courses, the scoring context, and the prerequisite-chain tool. In the code: course_catalog_entries, course_catalog_prereqs; tool prereq_chain.

The skill-level gap analysis is a separate layer. It stores prerequisite links: each says "this course relies on that immediate prerequisite for this specific competency, at this expected K/U/D depth." The AI proposes these links from the free-text prerequisites and the course's expectations of incoming students; faculty confirm them. The gap calculation then compares what the course needs with what its confirmed prerequisites are shown to deliver, taking the deepest level reached rather than adding levels (depths are ranks, not amounts). Each result is labeled with what it rests on, as in §3: measured, mixed, intended or none. A prerequisite that has not been captured gives "no data," never an assumed gap.

To give the gap view something to show before real captures exist, a companion step reads K/U/D levels claimed in the syllabus from catalog information, labeled claimed. These intended values are never mixed into measured attainment. The gap view for each course is at /courses/[code]; a program-wide diagram is deferred. No links had been confirmed as of October 2026, so the gap view stays sparse until faculty propose and confirm them. Put another way: a map of which courses lead to which, with an evidence-backed depth at each step, has the same shape as the maps that adaptive-learning software such as ALEKS uses to decide what a student is ready to learn next (formally, a knowledge space) — see background §2 for the framing and where the comparison breaks down.

In the code: table prerequisite_edges (each row a direct foreign-key link from a course to one prerequisite, tagged with a sub-competency and expected K/U/D); calculation computePrereqGaps (ordinal MAX; each result's basis is measured | mixed | intended | none); no_data; function intended-skills-extract (migration 0031, table course_intended_coverage).

Position Capture → Q1 sufficiency

The demand side (§4) connects here. Position Capture records the K/U/D depth employers require, linked to catalog competencies, and positions are combined per career target. The comparison layer compares that demand (weighted by partner) with the matrix's evidenced attainment (the deepest level reached), turning Q1 from "is the competency covered?" (breadth, available now) into "is it covered deeply enough?" (sufficiency). That layer is built from start to finish, but switched off. In the code: setting DEMAND_COVERAGE_SEAM.

Snapshots → curriculum wiki

The wiki repository (§9) regenerates its pages from approved snapshots. The wiki update runs in the background when a snapshot is saved, writing course and concept pages and the index; competency and target pages are regenerated after coverage scoring. The curriculum chat browses the wiki with read, list and search tools and quotes it word for word. Each update also refreshes the public copy on Clemson web space. The two pieces still open (the two-source rule for concept pages and an on-demand refresh of out-of-date pages) are listed in §9.

Industry partner input → competency descriptions

The partner survey feeds two processes with different jobs; they complement each other rather than overlap. Position Capture (above) measures demand: how deeply each competency is required, scored in K/U/D and linked to the catalog. The synthesis process (/admin/synthesis) refines definitions: it gathers partner submissions into suggested changes to the competency descriptions themselves — what a competency is, not how deeply it is needed. These suggestions are advisory: a faculty reviewer adopts, changes or rejects each one before it takes effect. When a description changes, existing coverage scores against that target are marked out of date and queued for rescoring, so the program's view updates instead of quietly pairing a new description with old scores.

The value builds up over time

Most of what the system offers grows with the number of records. One snapshot is useful for its own course. Ten make the coverage matrix informative. Twenty give Explore's scenario estimates real neighboring courses to reason about. A full set makes the scaffolding analysis diagnostic. The demand side works the same way: each captured position sharpens the sufficiency picture. The trial period is about confirming that the analysis of individual snapshots is credible before investing in the program-scale pieces that depend on many of them.

§ 12What the Software Enforces, and What It Doesn't

The companion documents — the KUD+ Background, the problem-solving and 3-Act deep-dives, and the vision — describe what the framework should do. This section takes each of their main claims and says what the software actually does about it. The point is to show which rules a reader can rely on because the software makes them hold, and which depend on the AI following instructions or on people. Each row was checked against the code it names.

How to read the status

  • enforced — the software makes the rule hold, in one of four ways: it refuses to save a result that breaks the rule; it computes the result with a fixed calculation (no AI); the database itself refuses data that breaks it; or an automated test fails if the rule is broken. In every case, the AI cannot produce a saved record that breaks the rule. For trust: you can rely on this rule holding in every saved record.
  • partial — the feature exists, but nothing stops the rule from being broken. Either the AI is instructed to follow it and could fail to; or it works when inputs are as expected but has no checks for unusual cases; or it is incomplete. For trust: the rule usually holds, but individual records should be checked.
  • stipulated (a design choice) — a decision the framework makes, which its own documents say was chosen rather than derived from research. There is nothing for code to check. For trust: results depend on this choice; a different reasonable choice could give different results.
  • unbuilt — described in the documents, not implemented anywhere. For trust: the tool does not do this yet, whatever the documents describe.
Summary

13 of the 31 claims below are enforced by the software; 13 are partial, 2 are design choices (stipulated) and 3 are not built.

The enforced ones mainly fall into two groups. First, the checks made when a course profile is saved: foundational competencies have no Know or Understand score; scores above the threshold need evidence; every depth is a whole number from 0 to 5; and credit for reflection must cite graded work. Second, the prerequisite-gap calculation: it uses the deepest level reached, says what each result rests on, and looks only at a course's immediate prerequisites. Both run in the live system, and both are covered by automated tests.

The partial ones are mostly rules the AI is instructed to follow, or features that are incomplete or switched off. The rest are design choices, or parts of the planned end state that are not built.

For the tool's first question — does the curriculum build students toward the careers it claims? — both halves are built (scoring what courses develop, and capturing what employers require), and so is the comparison between them, covered by automated tests. It is switched off and has no employer data yet. So Q1 sufficiency can be computed; showing it requires switching the comparison on and collecting employer data.

In the code: the profile checks run through the schema's .parse() on the production path; the comparison layer is the career_target_demand table, the step that fills it and the per-target panel, behind DEMAND_COVERAGE_SEAM.

In the code: three Zod .refine() checks in schema.ts; the program-scoring format program-score-coverage.ts coverageCellSchema lacks them. enforced
Exposure (K1) counts for Know only; U and D start only with active engagement (background §6) This is written into the level descriptions and into an instruction to "score the lower value." Nothing in the software forces U and D toward 0 when K is 1, so a record could break it. In the code: depth-anchors.ts, depth-scale.md. partial
Foundational competencies are scored on Do only; K and U stored empty, not zero (background §8) The app refuses to save a profile in which a foundational competency has a Know or Understand score. This check is made by the app when the profile is saved; it is not a database rule. (Separately, the database itself makes sure foundational competencies in the catalog have no Know or Understand descriptions.) In the code: a .refine() on rows with type:'foundational' and non-null k_depth/u_depth; catalog constraint sub_competencies_foundational_ku_check. enforced
All six baseline foundational competencies (written and spoken communication recorded separately since 2026-10-19) must appear in every profile (background §8) Only the AI's instructions say they must appear. The software only requires at least one competency, so a profile missing Resilience is saved without complaint. Reviewers should check that all six are present. In the code: capture-scores.md; schema requires competencies.min(1). partial
Mismatches between K, U and D (for example high Know, low Understand) are made visible (background §5) The separate scores make mismatches possible to see, and the AI describes them in a written field of the profile. But no fixed rule labels them, so a mismatch the AI does not mention may go unnoticed. In the code: field dimensional_patterns. partial
Bloom is used only inside the interview, never stored (background §2, §5) The profile has no field for a Bloom level, so there is nowhere to store one. The Bloom question exists only in the interview AI's instructions. In the code: no bloom_level field in the profile schema. enforced
When displayed, each score gets an evidence level: claimed / materials-supported / artifact-verified (background §7, §12) A fixed calculation assigns the first two levels. The third, artifact-verified, is defined but can never be reached until the software can cite student work. So no score can currently show the strongest level of evidence. In the code: evidence-ladder.ts deriveEvidenceBand; branch artifact_verified. partial
Snapshots are retired, never deleted, so the history survives (vision) A snapshot has a retirement date field, and the app has no way to delete one; program views leave out retired snapshots. In the code: retiredAt column; no hard-delete route. enforced

B. Problem-solving & scaffolding

What the documents claimWhat the software actually doesStatus
Problem-solving emerges across the program, so there is no problem-solving score for a course or a graduate (deep-dive II/IV) There is no problem-solving score anywhere in the stored data, so none can be produced. The only related signals are totals across the program. In the code: scaffolding.ts aggregateSubCompetency. enforced
The interview (Area 7) records whether a course creates the conditions for productive struggle: five conditions, each rated present, partial or absent, plus one supporting depth score. The fifth, abstraction-and-bridging, records whether students must draw out a shared principle from cases that look different on the surface, then apply it to a new situation (Gick-Holyoak / Gentner — the active transfer mechanism) (deep-dive II) The software checks the form of the answer (each condition is one of the allowed values, and the depth is 0–5). Which value each condition gets is left to the AI, following its instructions, so individual judgments should be checked. In the code: productiveFailureConditionsSchema (five condition fields + the 0–5 depth). partial
Credit for a structured post-mortem must cite graded work (deep-dive II/III) If the post-mortem is rated above "absent" without a citation, the record is refused — the same evidence rule as for K/U/D, applied to reflection. In the code: a .superRefine() check. enforced
The scoring model is depth × productive failure × reflection (deep-dive III) Fixed calculations (no AI) combine a depth curve, a reflection multiplier and a conditions score, and automated tests check the results. The particular weights and cut-offs are design choices, not taken from research. In the code: scaffolding.ts. enforced
The interview flags "unproductive success" (high depth with no struggle, meaning fragile competence) (deep-dive I/II) Only the AI's instructions (in three instruction files) ask it to note this. The data needed (depth and conditions, stored separately) exists, but no code calculates the flag, so a case the AI misses will not be flagged. partial
Not assessed is not the same as absent: a course never asked about this records no data, is left out of totals, and is never scored 0 (deep-dive II) A course with no answer contributes nothing (not zero); the totals report a separate "no data" category; and older records made before this rule are reclassified as no data. All three are checked by automated tests. In the code: a null PF block contributes null; band no_data; PF_CONTRACT_EPOCH. enforced
Depth × failure × reflection built up over four years produces transferable problem-solving (deep-dive I/II) The documents themselves call this a hypothesis. Nothing links snapshots to what graduates go on to do; the data collection exists, but the test of the claim does not. unbuilt

C. Program structure (the 3-Act model)

What the documents claimWhat the software actually doesStatus
The named acts (Foundations & Agency → Integration → Specialty), which courses belong to which act, the pairing with agency, and the timing of the mid-Junior "aha" (3-Act V) None of this is used in scoring. The brittle-scaffold finding: a course expects mastery that no earlier course introduces (3-Act I) A fixed comparison: an integration-level cell with no earlier introduction or practice cell. Checked by an automated test, and shown on the live /program/scaffolding page. enforced
Courses are ordered by act or prerequisite chain for the analysis (3-Act; spec) The software uses catalog level (1000–4000) as a stand-in; ordering by act or by prerequisite chain is explicitly deferred. So "brittle across act boundaries" in practice means "integration-level depth appears before introduction or practice, in course-level order" — a course numbered lower but taken later would be misplaced. partial
