The 3-Act Program Structure

An honest accounting of what curriculum-design literature the GC program's 3-Act structure (Foundations & Agency → Integration & Mastery → Specialty & Application) is consistent with, what it isn't grounded in, and how it engages the critiques that staged curricula face.

Date 2026-05-25 Status Research-tool framing — consistency claims, not derivation claims Audience Faculty, curriculum committee, advisory partners

§ 1Why this document exists

The Vision document proposes organizing the GC program in three acts: Learn the field (Foundations & Agency), Integrate the field (Integration & Mastery), Apply a focus (Specialty & Application). The framework treats act boundaries as analytical waypoints — the Phase 1B Scaffolding Analysis (Stage 1 live) uses them to surface "brittle scaffolds," where an Act 3 course expects mastery of a competency no Act 1 or Act 2 course actually introduces.

The 3-Act structure is a proposal, not an existing institutional policy. It is load-bearing for the scaffolding diagnostic, and it makes substantive claims about how undergraduate professional education should be organized. Both deserve careful treatment.

This document does two things. First, it situates the 3-Act structure in the curriculum-design literature — what it is consistent with, what it isn't grounded in, what it shares with peer fields. Second, it engages the strongest critiques honestly, including the ones a staged structure has to answer directly. The intent throughout is honesty about which parts are research-supported, which are framework commitments, and which are working hypotheses the tool's data will eventually test.

A framing note

The framework is a research instrument. It does not need every design choice to be derived from settled literature. What it does need is honesty about which choices are derived and which are stipulated, so the diagnostic outputs can be interpreted with appropriate confidence — and so the structure can be revised as evidence accumulates.

Part I — What the structure is consistent with

The 3-Act structure is consistent with several established curriculum-design traditions. It is not derived from any of them. The distinction matters: a structure that is consistent with multiple frameworks is defensible; a structure that claims to be derived from those frameworks claims more than the consistency relationship supports.

§ 2Bruner's spiral curriculum

Jerome Bruner's The Process of Education† (1960) introduced the spiral curriculum. It starts from the hypothesis that “any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of development” (Bruner, 1960, p. 30, as quoted in Gibbs, 2014). In Gibbs's summary, “students revisit a topic, theme, or subject several times throughout their schooling, where the complexity of the topic is increased with each visit so the new learning is connected to the old learning.”

Two features of the spiral matter here: each revisit raises the complexity, and each connects new learning to old. The 3-Act structure keeps the revisiting logic — Act 2 courses revisit Act 1 competencies at a higher level of integration — but bounds it into three named phases rather than a continuous spiral. Gibbs (2014) argues for bounding the spiral in a different direction: “Bruner was right, but his scale was wrong,” and the spiral “can be and is much more powerful when scaled down to fit the individual classroom or grade level.” That supports compressing the spiral inside one course or year, with support withdrawn step by step; it does not address dividing a degree program into stages. The three-act version is a variant of Bruner's idea, not an implementation of it: Bruner is a theoretical cousin, not a parent.

§ 3Constructive alignment at program scale

John Biggs (1996) describes constructive alignment as using the “performances of understanding” named in a course's objectives to “align the teaching methods and the assessment” (p. 347). He illustrates it with a single professional-development unit but states that “the model may be generalized to most units or programs in higher education” (p. 347).

The 3-Act structure is consistent with program-level constructive alignment provided that (a) each act has defined outcome sets and (b) Act 2 and Act 3 outcomes are explicitly mapped to Act 1 prerequisites. Biggs's framework does not require that stages be named or that progression be enforced through gates — it requires only that outcomes, teaching and assessment be aligned. The 3-Act structure adds a sequencing-and-naming layer Biggs's model does not require.

§ 4Backward design at program scale

Wiggins and McTighe's Understanding by Design (2005) frames curriculum as beginning from desired results. The book concentrates on designing units, but its authors apply the same logic upward: they advocate “the design of course syllabi and program frameworks using backward design” (p. 275).

The 3-Act structure is compatible with backward-design logic: Act 3 specialty outcomes define what Act 2 integration must produce, which defines what Act 1 foundations must establish. The framework was not, however, designed using backward design — the acts were named first as an organizing model, and outcomes were specified within them. This is a retrospective compatibility, not a derivation. The honest framing: backward design and the 3-Act structure share a common logical orientation (work back from desired graduate capabilities); the framework would benefit from a deliberate backward-design pass at each act boundary to operationalize this compatibility more fully.

§ 5Cornerstone-capstone in engineering education

The closest analog to the 3-Act structure is the cornerstone-capstone model common in U.S. engineering education: a first-year design course (the “cornerstone”; Dym et al., 2005†), technical coursework in the middle years, and an open-ended senior capstone. ABET's engineering criteria require the capstone end of this: the curriculum must include “a culminating major engineering design experience that … is based on the knowledge and skills acquired in earlier course work” (ABET, 2025–2026 criteria, Criterion 5).

The 3-Act framework's most credible substantive contribution to this lineage is naming the middle act explicitly and proposing the integrative "everything comes together" moment at the Act 2 / Act 3 boundary rather than at the senior capstone. In the GC program specifically, GC 4400 and GC 4060 are mini-capstones that faculty and graduating students consistently identify as the courses where "it all came together" — where students first experience integrated practice and recognize how their developing competencies translate into professional value. The 3-Act proposal treats this empirical "aha" experience as the structural target of Act 2, and proposes completing Act 2 by mid-Junior year so that students would cross this integrative threshold while they still have 1.5+ years remaining to develop career-path-specific Act 3 specialization. This would invert the cornerstone-capstone bookend: rather than reserving the integrative experience for the senior capstone (which leaves no time for post-integration specialization), the 3-Act proposal deliberately accelerates students to the integration moment so the back half of the program can be career-targeted development.

This design rationale is the strongest pragmatic case for the 3-Act structure over a cornerstone-capstone alternative. In the bookend model the middle years are left implicit — the bookends are named, the middle is not — and a senior capstone that is the only integrative experience leaves little time to apply what it integrates. The 3-Act structure addresses both: it names the integration phase as a diagnosable stage with its own scaffolding requirements, and it positions integration early enough that specialization can follow.

The framework also differs from cornerstone-capstone in an important way: engineering's capstone requirement is set and checked externally, through ABET accreditation. The 3-Act structure's gates are currently internal to the program. The framework's planned readiness criteria are the move toward closing this gap.

§ 6Scaffolding theory at program scale

Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development (Mind in Society†, 1978) describes learning as occurring in the space between what a learner can do independently and what they can do with assistance. Applied at program scale, scaffolding logic means earlier courses provide the support under which students build competencies they will later exercise independently.

The 3-Act structure operationalizes this in a specific way: Act 1 courses provide heavy scaffolding, Act 2 courses expect partial independence, Act 3 courses expect near-professional autonomy. The “brittle scaffold” diagnostic the framework introduces corresponds to what the cognitive-apprenticeship literature calls fading — “the gradual removal of supports until students are on their own” (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1987, p. 17) — done before the student can manage without the support.

The “brittle scaffold” framing is not novel as a diagnostic. Prerequisite network analysis predates it: Aldrich (2014) treats a whole university catalog as a “curriculum prerequisite network,” with “nodes representing courses and links between nodes the course prerequisites,” and finds individual courses “serving very different roles in the overall organization, such as information sources, hubs, and bridges.” The framework's contribution is applying this kind of analysis at named phase boundaries in a design curriculum and pairing it with depth-aware K/U/D scoring. That is a pedagogical application, not a new research method.

§ 7Preparation for future learning

Bransford and Schwartz's “Rethinking Transfer: A Simple Proposal with Multiple Implications” (1999, Review of Research in Education) introduced preparation for future learning (PFL): judging transfer by how well earlier experience prepares students to learn something new. In one study they report, students who analyzed contrasting cases before a lecture learned more from it than students who summarized a text before the same lecture: “The act of analyzing the cases prepared the students to learn from the lecture” (p. 77).

That is a finding about sequencing within a unit or lesson. Reading it as a justification for a multi-year staged curriculum would be a scale jump the research does not support. Where PFL is useful for the framework is in within-Act 1 course design: Act 1 works best when students encounter problems that the foundations will later solve, not when they simply acquire foundations in anticipation of future problems. This is a commitment about how Act 1 instruction should be designed, not a justification for the existence of three acts.

Part II — Comparable models in peer fields

§ 8Multi-phase curricula across professional education

Multi-phase professional curricula are common across U.S. higher education. The 3-Act structure is consistent with this pattern, but the pattern itself is at a high level of abstraction. Most professional programs converge on "foundations before advanced work" because the alternative is structurally incoherent. Treating this as independent convergence on the specific 3-Act framework would overstate what the comparison shows.

A more honest framing: staged professional curricula are common, legitimate, and credentialed across fields, and the 3-Act structure is one principled instance of that pattern. The comparisons below illustrate the family resemblance and surface the important divergences.

Medical education

U.S. medical schools organize undergraduate medical education into preclinical years and clinical clerkships, followed by residency for specialty training. The preclinical-then-clinical sequence is commonly traced to Abraham Flexner's 1910 report (Flexner, 1910†). The other stages came later. Specialty certification grew up over the following decades: the first proposal for a specialty board dates from 1908, and the American Board of Medical Specialties was established in 1933 to coordinate the boards (ABMS). The national licensing examination is later still: the USMLE was “first implemented in 1992” (USMLE).

The 3-Act structure shares the foundations-before-advanced-work logic with the modern medical model but not its external checks: the USMLE “is part of a physician's pathway to licensure,” and specialty certification is run by external boards. The 3-Act framework's gates are currently internal to the program. This is a substantive difference, not a cosmetic one.

Engineering education

Discussed in §5. The closest empirical analog to the 3-Act framework with the most directly relevant research literature.

Graphic design programs (not the same field as graphic communication)

Comparisons to programs at RISD and other art-and-design schools require a clarification: graphic design (as taught at RISD, for example) is a different disciplinary field from graphic communication (Cal Poly SLO's program, and Clemson's) and from communication design. The fields share visual-communication concerns but diverge on technical-production emphasis, industry orientation, and disciplinary genealogy. Treating them as interchangeable instances of "the same structure" risks suppressing the disciplinary differences that shape each program's actual organization.

That said, the broad pattern is familiar: a foundational year, a developmental middle period focused on principles and methods, and a senior year that mixes specialization with capstone work. At RISD, for example, “during your first year you and your peers follow the same studio curricula” (RISD, Experimental and Foundation Studies). The 3-Act structure matches this broad pattern. The framework should be honest that the comparisons here are illustrative rather than confirming.

Cal Poly SLO Graphic Communication

The closest peer in field and emphasis. The program does not name phases. Its 2026–2028 catalog requires a common set of GRC major courses plus one of five named 21-unit concentrations — Design Reproduction Technology, Graphic Communication Management, Graphics for Packaging, Immersive Experience Design, and User Experience/User Interface — or an individualized course of study (Cal Poly catalog). The concentrations are structurally analogous to the 3-Act structure's specialty phase. The structural similarity is real and meaningful for benchmarking; the divergence is that Cal Poly's concentrations are formal curriculum artifacts with their own prerequisite chains, while the 3-Act structure's "specialty" is currently an organizing principle rather than a credentialed track.

Business education and teacher licensure

Undergraduate business programs often run from a business core to major coursework to an integrative senior course. AACSB's 2020 standards do not prescribe that sequence; they define a curriculum's structure in general terms, as “how the content is organized and sequenced to create a systematic, integrated program of teaching and learning” (AACSB, 2020, p. 41).

Teacher-preparation programs usually place foundations coursework (learning theory, child development) and methods courses (subject-specific pedagogy) before student teaching (supervised practice). Where a program requires checkpoints before student teaching, those checkpoints do formally what the “brittle scaffold” diagnostic only approximates.

Part III — Critiques the framework needs to engage

§ 9The phantom prerequisite problem

Sato et al. (2017), a mixed-methods study in CBE—Life Sciences Education, tested whether a microbiology lecture course helped students in the lab course it had been a prerequisite for. Rather than compare overall grades, they classified lab exam questions by how familiar the prerequisite should have made them, and surveyed and interviewed students. “Both sources of data provided complementary evidence that MLec does not impact success in MLab”: a listed prerequisite did not do the work assumed of it. The lesson for the 3-Act model is that a prerequisite link on paper is a hypothesis. Whether earlier courses support later ones has to be checked at the level of concepts and competencies, not assumed from the course sequence — and course completion alone is weak evidence of readiness.

The 3-Act framework's “brittle scaffold” diagnostic is aimed at the related failure — a later course relying on something no earlier course builds — but only if act-boundary assessment evaluates internalization, not merely course completion. The framework currently treats course completion plus the K/U/D depth values as the readiness signal. Strengthening this to include explicit readiness criteria at act boundaries (portfolio reviews, structured demonstrations, instructor sign-off) is one of the framework's load-bearing TODOs. Without it, the diagnostic detects scaffolding gaps after they have already produced poor downstream learning rather than preventing them.

§ 10The situated-learning critique (Lave & Wenger)

Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger's Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation† (1991) treats learning as becoming a participant in a community of practice. Duguid (2005), reviewing the idea, notes that it “was introduced as a theory of learning, drawing much of its evidence from studies of apprenticeship,” and draws the contrast it implies between learning to be a practitioner and learning about a practice — the latter “confers the ability to talk a good game, but not necessarily to play one” (manuscript pp. 9–10). Applied to program design, the critique is not that individual courses lack practical content. It is that a three-act sequence separating “learn foundations” from “integrate” from “apply” risks teaching students about the field before they can practice in it.

This is the strongest theoretical critique of the framework, and it deserves a substantive response rather than the deflection of "Act 1 includes courses with practical content."

The framework's response draws on cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989†; checked against the 1987 technical-report version). Cognitive apprenticeship brings features of traditional apprenticeship into schooling through six teaching methods: modelling, coaching and scaffolding, which the authors call “the core of cognitive apprenticeship”; articulation and reflection; and exploration (1987, p. 16). The 3-Act structure can operate as a sequenced cognitive apprenticeship: Act 1 emphasizes modeling and coaching with high scaffolding, Act 2 introduces articulation and reflection as students take on more authentic problems, Act 3 emphasizes exploration and authentic-practice participation.

This response succeeds only if Act 1 instruction is genuinely apprenticeship-shaped — with explicit modeling, structured coaching, and articulation tasks — rather than primarily declarative instruction with practical examples. The framework's productive-failure audit area is one tool for evaluating whether Act 1 courses operate this way in practice. Where they do, the cognitive-apprenticeship response holds. Where they don't, Lave & Wenger's critique stands and the diagnostic outputs need to be interpreted with that in mind.

The framework also makes an explicit fidelity trade-off: it accepts that staged school-based instruction is an approximation of community-of-practice learning, and that some loss of situated authenticity is the price of an analytical structure that can support program-level evaluation. This trade-off is named openly rather than rhetorically denied.

§ 11The artificial-boundary problem

Donald Schön's The Reflective Practitioner† (1983) describes professional competence as built through reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action. On that view, the “between acts” framing risks imposing discontinuities on what is a continuous developmental process.

The framework's response is administrative: the acts are diagnostic constructs for program-level analysis, not descriptive claims about how individual student learning unfolds. A student's developmental trajectory is continuous; the framework's analytical waypoints carve that continuum into named phases so program-level patterns can be surfaced. The acts are useful where they make patterns visible; they are misleading if treated as natural breakpoints in student development.

§ 12Flexibility and transfer students

Staged models assume linear progression and create problems for transfer students, students with prior professional experience, and students who retake foundational courses. The 3-Act structure currently has no defined protocol for credit for prior learning at act boundaries.

The honest acknowledgment: this is a structural hole, not an incremental enhancement. The framework's "brittle scaffold" diagnostic, applied to a transfer student who entered with significant self-taught competency, would consistently flag Act 1 gaps that may or may not reflect actual readiness. A prior-learning assessment mechanism at each act boundary is on the framework's TODO list.

Part IV — Foundations & Agency

§ 13The agency claim, done honestly

Act 1 is named "Foundations & Agency," pairing technical foundations with the disposition of agency. The framework treats agency as a developmental outcome — something Act 1 is meant to begin cultivating in students.

The theoretical grounding for treating agency as a developmental outcome (rather than as a feature of a motivational environment) is in the self-regulated learning literature, particularly Barry Zimmerman's work. Zimmerman's model of self-regulated learning (2002†) identifies forethought, performance, and self-reflection as cyclical phases of self-regulation that develop through guided practice. That tradition treats self-regulation as learnable through specific interventions — explicit modeling of strategies, opportunities for goal-setting and self-monitoring, feedback on regulatory choices rather than only on task performance. Marcia Baxter Magolda's work on self-authorship (most notably Authoring Your Life†, 2009) provides a developmental-stage account of how undergraduates move from external-formula-following toward internal authorship of their own commitments. Both bodies of work treat agency-like dispositions as learnable, sequentially developable, and amenable to instructional design.

Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985†; Ryan & Deci, 2017†) is sometimes cited in connection with agency development. SDT is most precisely about the motivational consequences of autonomy-supportive environments — it predicts that when learners experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness, intrinsic motivation grows. SDT is not, strictly speaking, a theory of how agency develops as a learnable competency; it is a theory of what motivational environments enable. The framework's Act 1 design should draw on SDT for how to structure the learning environment (autonomy-supportive structures, meaningful choice, genuine competence development) and on Zimmerman / Baxter Magolda for what agency development looks like as a developmental trajectory. The two should not be conflated.

The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1986†, Mind over Machine) provides a complementary developmental framing. The Dreyfus stages (novice → advanced beginner → competent → proficient → expert) place explicit constraints on agency at the novice stage: novices need rules to follow precisely because they lack the situational pattern recognition that makes meaningful judgment possible. The Act 1 framing is most defensible when agency is treated as a seed rather than a demonstrated capability — Act 1 introduces students to the concept of professional self-direction, builds the structured-choice and reflection habits that support self-regulation, and creates the conditions for agency to grow alongside competence rather than precede it. Agency as a fully realized Act 1 outcome would contradict the Dreyfus developmental account; agency as a cultivated Act 1 disposition that fully expresses itself by Act 3 is consistent with it.

Part V — Accounting and open questions

§ 14What's research-supported vs. what's stipulated

A clean accounting of which 3-Act design choices are research-supported and which are framework commitments.

Research-supported (the framework can defend these from established literature)

  • Foundations-before-advanced-work is common in professional education (ABET's required capstone built on earlier course work; medical education's preclinical-then-clinical sequence), and the revisit-with-increasing-complexity principle is Bruner's spiral.
  • The diagnostic logic behind the “brittle scaffold” has antecedents in curriculum prerequisite-network analysis (Aldrich, 2014).
  • Checking prerequisite links at the level of concepts, rather than assuming them from the course sequence, has a published precedent (Sato et al., 2017), which found a listed prerequisite that did not help the course it preceded.
  • Cognitive apprenticeship (modelling, coaching and scaffolding; articulation and reflection; exploration) is a published design for bringing apprenticeship-like practice into staged school instruction (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1987).
  • Agency development as a learnable, sequentially developable disposition is consistent with the self-regulated learning and self-authorship literature (Zimmerman, 2002†; Baxter Magolda, 2009†).

Framework commitments (defensible working choices, not derivations)

  • Three acts specifically, rather than two (cornerstone-capstone) or five (the Dreyfus stages).
  • The specific names Foundations & Agency, Integration & Mastery, Specialty & Application — and the pairing of technical content with a dispositional construct in Act 1.
  • The specific course-to-act mapping in the Vision document.
  • The "mid-Junior year" timing for Act 2 completion. This is anchored on the empirical student experience of GC 4400 and GC 4060 as the integrative "aha" courses, with the structural intent of leaving 1.5+ years of Act 3 for career-path-specific development. The timing is pragmatically grounded in observed student outcomes; it is not derived from external literature on optimal integrative-milestone timing (which the literature doesn't actually specify). The framework treats this as a working hypothesis the snapshot corpus and graduate-outcome data will eventually validate or revise.

Structural holes the framework acknowledges

  • Explicit readiness criteria at act boundaries are a framework commitment and are not yet operationalized (Phase 1B Scaffolding Analysis is the planned location for this).
  • A prior-learning assessment protocol for transfer students and students with significant external preparation does not exist.
  • The 3-Act structure is proposed as an organizing principle; even if adopted, it would not have the external checks that licensing examinations give medical education or that ABET's capstone requirement gives engineering.

§ 15Open empirical questions

Three questions the framework's data collection over time will eventually be able to address.

  1. Does the 3-Act structure produce better graduate outcomes than alternatives? This requires longitudinal tracking of graduate readiness against career-target competencies, and a comparison group from a program organized differently. The framework can generate the program-side data; the comparison-group data is harder. Advisory-board feedback and alumni outcomes can serve as proxies in the medium term.
  2. Is mid-Junior year the right timing for Act 2 completion? This is a program-specific empirical question. Snapshots over time will reveal which competencies are reached at which course-sequence positions; if the mid-Junior milestone is consistently met or consistently missed, the timing claim is testable.
  3. Are act boundaries the right diagnostic waypoints? The brittle-scaffold diagnostic uses the 3-Act boundaries. An alternative analysis might use Bloom-level transitions, Dreyfus-stage transitions, or course-prerequisite-graph cut-points. The framework's data shape is compatible with running these alternative diagnostics in parallel; doing so as the snapshot corpus accumulates would test whether the act-boundary framing is doing analytical work the alternatives don't.
