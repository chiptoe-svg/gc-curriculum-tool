Curriculum Measurement — Open Hypotheses & Research Agenda

Three interrelated hypotheses about what the KUD+depth framework measures and how: a durable-depth model, a competency-to-career cross-walk, and a convergence discipline for measurement uncertainty. Companion to the KUD+ Background; these hypotheses are forward and unvalidated — most are gated on the graduate-outcome study.

Register of this document

This document is explicitly forward and unvalidated. The Background and Problem-Solving Deep-Dive present settled (or defensibly bounded) commitments the framework rests on today. This companion presents what would need to be true — and how it would need to be tested — for the framework to do the things it aspires to: trace competencies across a curriculum, compare program output to career demand, and report uncertainty honestly. Most of the quantitative hypotheses here are gated on the graduate-outcome study. Nothing in this document constitutes a validated finding or a claimed result.

§ 1The keystone — KUD+depth as the unit of account

The three hypotheses below are not independent ideas. They are three uses of one underlying move: KUD+depth is the unit of account the whole framework is denominated in — a shared currency on which a course’s output, a downstream course’s prerequisite expectation, the program’s aggregate coverage, and a career’s measured demand are all expressible. Because all of these entities are expressed on the same Know/Understand/Do × depth-0–5 axes, boundary-crossing questions become arithmetic on a shared axis rather than translation between mismatched vocabularies.

A currency needs three properties, and KUD+depth has all three. A common denomination: every entity uses the same axes. A medium of exchange: because the units match, the questions that require crossing a boundary — prerequisite gap, curriculum roll-up, career-readiness comparison — become subtractions and aggregations rather than fuzzy title matches. An evidence-anchored store of value: the evidence-above-zero rule (see Background §6) is the hard-money discipline; aspirational syllabus verbs alone cannot inflate a score.

No other unit does this job. Course names and titles do not translate across institutions or roles. Raw text and generic embeddings are lossy and context-free — the research makes clear why (see §5). The 35 cross-occupational skills in O*NET are the context-free transferable-skill construct the framework rejects on the grounds documented in Background §11. Bloom levels have no Do axis and are not written in the vocabulary careers use, so they cannot carry a program-to-career mapping. Credit hours and grades measure seat-time and relative ranking, not attained capability. KUD+depth is the only unit simultaneously fine-grained enough to be honest, universal enough to span course, program, and career, and pegged to student-side evidence rather than aspiration.

Naming the unit of account does not make the currency frictionless. It locates where the friction is, and each friction corresponds to one of the three hypotheses:

  • Hypothesis A (Competency→career cross-walk) — the currency converts cleanly within the curriculum (one authored taxonomy) but imperfectly into careers. That is the entire reason A is many-to-many, partially matched, match-quality-stamped, with “no clean match” as a first-class finding. External: pegged but imperfect.
  • Hypothesis B (Durability & bleed-off) — instantaneous depth is not the same quantity as durable depth. Cross-level comparisons must spend durable depth, not the instantaneous end-of-course maximum. Bleed-off is the depreciation of held KUD.
  • Hypothesis C (Uncertainty & convergence discipline) — rolling up ordinal KUD treats it as interval, and its sums travel with an uncertainty band, not as points. The currency is real; its arithmetic is honest only when it carries the interval.

This is also why the embeddings question (§5) resolves the way it does: KUD+depth is not a representation competing with embeddings — it is the denomination the comparisons are settled in.

§ 2Hypothesis B — Durability & bleed-off

This is where “common threads through the curriculum” live. The animating goal of the framework is to find the threads that run between courses, through the curriculum, and into career paths. A thread is not a single similarity score — it is a typed, depth-conditioned progression: a competency that appears in one course, deepens across several, and (via hypothesis A) feeds a career destination. Durability is the model of which threads actually hold. A thread reached once decays; a thread reinforced across courses and modalities sticks. Because the through-line within the curriculum is the prerequisite for any honest claim into careers, durability is arguably the more foundational hypothesis of the two.

The gap in the current model

A KUD score certifies depth at the end of a course. The question the program cares about — how well does the curriculum prepare students for careers — concerns depth at graduation, after the rest of the curriculum has happened. These are different quantities, and the current model conflates them. A lone D4 reached in the first year and never revisited is not a D4 at graduation; it has decayed.

The two forces: decay and consolidation

Two bodies of evidence shape the hypothesis. First, decay: between exposures, attainment fades. Murre and Dros (2015) replicated Ebbinghaus’s forgetting curve with one subject relearning lists after intervals from 20 minutes to 31 days. Their measure is savings (the share of the original learning time saved when relearning), not percent recalled. In the replication, savings fell from 47% at 20 minutes to 4% at 31 days; in Ebbinghaus’s original data, from 58% to 21% (their Table 3). They conclude that the curve “is not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24 hour data point,” adding that current research on sleep would predict such a jump, though for this kind of experiment it “remains to be established.” For trained skills, Arthur et al.’s (1998) meta-analysis found “substantial skill loss with nonpractice or nonuse,” summarized as ranging from d = −0.01 immediately after training to d = −1.4 after more than 365 days (abstract); their Table 3 gives d = −1.28 for the beyond-a-year interval, from three data points. These are all-task figures. Cognitive tasks decayed more than physical ones (δ = −1.15 versus −0.75, collapsed across all intervals). The existence of permastore (Bahrick, 1984, pp. 1, 23–25 — a 50-year study of school Spanish in which retention fell for 3–6 years and then held steady for up to 30 years; the share reaching this state rose with level of training and grades, and learners with the least training and lowest grades kept almost no recall vocabulary there) would support the core distinction between instantaneous and durable depth as two different states.

Second, consolidation: repeated and varied reinforcement makes depth more durable. Three findings bear on this, from different literatures. Spacing: across the studies Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesized, spaced presentation averaged 47.3% recall against 36.7% for massed (Table 1), and “there is no hint that massed presentation was preferable to spaced, whether retention interval was very short (less than 1 min) or very long (over 30 days)” (p. 359). In a follow-up, the best gap between study sessions grew in absolute size as the delay to the final test grew, but shrank as a share of that delay: from about 20–40% of a one-week delay to about 5–10% of a one-year delay (Cepeda et al., 2008). Retrieval practice: practice tests beat restudying (g = 0.51) and beat filler or no activity (g = 0.93) (Adesope et al., 2017). Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that extra studying helped more on a test five minutes later, but prior testing helped more on delayed tests. The short-term/long-term reversal and the role of variety come from Bjork (1994): massing practice “produces better short-term performance or recall … but markedly inferior long-term performance or recall” (p. 190), and “introducing variation and/or unpredictability in the training environment causes difficulty for the learner but enhances long-term performance” (p. 189). On Bjork’s account, variety matters, not just count.

A caveat on the shape of decay. Earlier reviews proposed a negatively accelerated curve, with most loss soon after last use. Arthur et al. set out to test that curve and could not (“We were unable, however, to do this,” p. 88). Their Table 3 shows more loss at longer intervals overall (r = −0.51 across eight interval groups), but not a steady progression from one interval to the next. Murre and Dros found a jump at one day rather than a smooth curve. So “decay is front-loaded” is a plausible working assumption, not an established finding. The “roughly one level per course gap” figure sometimes used as a rule of thumb has no direct empirical support: it is not a finding in KUD levels, and the effect-size results above are not in KUD units. The parameters are not settled.

The “pull neighbors up” extension (knowledge integration)

Durability does not operate per-competency in isolation. High-depth attainment in a competency may lift related neighbors — the integration dimension of expertise. Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser’s (1981) expert–novice findings, discussed in Background §4, show that expert knowledge is organized differently from novice knowledge: “experts categorize physics problems by the underlying physics principles, a kind of ‘deep structure,’ whereas novices categorize problems by the surface structure of the problem” (p. 12). Chi et al. do not test whether this organization makes knowledge more durable or more transferable. That step is this document’s hypothesis: a high-depth node may slow the decay of related competencies by embedding them in an active, integrated schema.

This effect must be modeled conservatively. The transfer literature is a strong counterweight: far transfer is fragile. Sala and Gobet (2017) report that when training groups were compared with active control groups, “the overall effect sizes were minimal or null.” Perkins and Salomon (1992) describe high-road transfer as depending on “mindful abstraction … and a deliberate search for connections,” which instruction can encourage by “bridging,” and conclude that “the conditions for transfer are stringent.” The “rich-get-richer” effect, in which prior knowledge predicts new learning, was “domain specific” in Witherby and Carpenter’s (2021) three experiments: prior knowledge in one domain predicted new learning in that domain but not the other (abstract). The pattern was not perfectly clean — there was one cross-domain exception (truthful football items in Experiment 1), and in Experiment 2 cooking knowledge did not predict even cooking learning — so “largely within-domain” is the fair summary. A pull-up mechanism that fires automatically on depth alone would systematically overestimate cross-competency strength — the pseudo-precision the framework refuses. The appropriate boundary is Knowledge Space Theory’s surmise relation (Doignon & Falmagne, 1985†, in Background references): competence in a higher item implies competence in its prerequisites, giving a principled and falsifiable definition of “neighbor.” Any pull-up mechanism should be bounded to KST-surmise-related, same-domain pairs and should never auto-elevate on depth alone.

A borrowable computational form (for the quantitative hypothesis)

The structural durability question (is a competency a lone peak or a reinforced thread?) can be read from existing captured data without any new model. The quantitative durability question — what is the estimated depth at graduation? — would require fitting a decay-and-consolidation model to outcome data. The most reusable form from the literature is Half-Life Regression (Settles & Meeder, 2016, ACL, from Duolingo): recall probability as a function of time with a half-life that grows exponentially with repeated exposure, weighted by the learner’s counts of correct and incorrect recalls (p. 1851) — the decay-plus-consolidation shape needed. FSRS (the open-source scheduler offered in Anki†) is a more recent form, with a power-law retrievability, a Stability term that grows more when retrieval happens under partial forgetting (the spacing effect built in), and a per-item Difficulty parameter that could map to the Do dimension being harder to consolidate than Know. (An earlier draft called FSRS “empirically superior”; we have no benchmark source for that and have dropped it.)

By contrast, Bayesian Knowledge Tracing has no forgetting term, and DKT-family models need far more interaction events than a curriculum produces†. Their most portable piece is HawkesKT’s exponential decay kernel†. The HLR/FSRS form is the realistic starting point if and when outcome data are available.

Nothing in the literature we reviewed operates at curriculum or degree scale. The knowledge-tracing and spaced-repetition work we found is single-course, single-platform, or flashcard-grain. We found no published model that tracks competency mastery as it builds, decays, and consolidates across a sequence of courses taught by different instructors on different timelines. The contribution of lifting the HLR/FSRS stability model from item-level to (competency × course) cells would be genuinely novel at that scale.

The structural / quantitative split

The hypothesis divides into two parts with different dependencies:

  • B1 — structural durability. Over existing captured snapshots, flag each competency as a lone peak (one high-depth hit, never reinforced → decay risk) versus a reinforced thread (multiple, varied, across the sequence → likely durable). This is qualitative but informative. It enriches the scaffolding analysis by making it durability-aware — not just whether a competency appears in the introduce–practice–integrate pattern, but whether the spacing and variety predict durability. No new model or gated data required.
  • B2 — quantitative durability. An actual decay-rate and consolidation function producing a numeric durable depth per competency at graduation. The parameters (decay per gap, exposures-to-consolidate, variety weighting) are empirical unknowns — never hard-coded, because hard-coded parameters would import exactly the pseudo-precision the framework refuses. The graduate-outcome study gates this hypothesis and validates its direction, but with an honest limit: its data establishes where graduates land, not their retained depth — so it constrains the durability story without directly supplying the signal needed to fit the decay parameters. Fitting them would require a competency-level retained-capability measure (a longitudinal assessment, or graduate/employer competency ratings) — a contact-based instrument that an observational, LinkedIn-based destination study does not collect. B2 quantification is therefore out of scope for the planned study and would need a separate one.

Durability and prerequisite gaps

The hypothesis also sharpens the prerequisite-gap question (“do the prerequisites students walk in with support what the course expects?”). That question is itself a bleed-off question: the prerequisite’s relevant depth is its level when the dependent course starts, not when the prerequisite course ended. Bleed-off is precisely why prerequisites fail. B is therefore not only a scaffolding enrichment; it is the missing temporal dimension of the prerequisite-gap analysis.

§ 3Hypothesis A — Competency→career cross-walk

The framework’s central question — how well does the GC curriculum build students toward the careers we claim to prepare them for? — requires comparing program output to career demand on a common axis. Hypothesis A is about whether and how that comparison can be made honestly.

The data source that makes this tractable is Course-Skill Atlas (Javadian Sabet et al., 2024, Scientific Data), which computed national O*NET Detailed Work Activity (DWA) coverage by major (CIP code) over approximately 3.16 million syllabi. This turns the expensive operation of hand-extracting relevant O*NET tasks into a slice-and-benchmark operation: pull the GC slice, compare captured coverage to the national GC curriculum’s DWA profile. The benchmark the memo’s O*NET recommendations lacked.

Design commitments

Skill-to-skill mapping is not a clean operation. Names do not match — “color management” in a GC course does not string-match to an O*NET DWA, and the mismatch is not a data-quality failure but a structural feature of translating between a fine-grained authored taxonomy and a cross-industry general-purpose one. The cross-walk would need to be designed to accommodate this honestly:

  • Many-to-many and partial, never one-to-one. A weighted bipartite relation, not a lookup.
  • A match-quality stamp on every edge (strong / partial / weak / none) — mirroring the prerequisite “basis” stamp already in the framework.
  • “No clean match” is a first-class, asymmetric finding: when a GC competency maps to no DWA, that is a finding about O*NET’s coarseness for GC specifics (Typography, Color Science, Press Chemistry are predictably under-represented in a cross-industry ontology). When a high-coverage DWA in the national profile maps to none of the captured competencies, that is a candidate blind spot in the framework itself.
  • Pipeline: embeddings shortlist → depth-aware reasoning adjudicates → near-miss queue for faculty review. On a curriculum-to-O*NET matching task, an embedding-only approach had 24% of its top-ten skills rated 4 or higher on a 1–5 alignment rubric, while retrieve-then-reason (RAG) approaches reached 69.5–82%, depending on the model (“From Course to Skill,” Xu et al., 2025, Table 2). The shortlist is recall; adjudication is the reasoning step; the near-miss queue exists because the shortlist's recall is imperfect — a design assumption, not a measured figure — so faculty must be able to add matches the shortlist missed.
  • Map durable depth (B), not instantaneous maximum. Comparing career demand to end-of-course depth re-imports the bleed-off error into the career-readiness claim. The cross-walk should use B1 durability-flagged depth once that analysis exists.

Two of the five identified career destinations for GC graduates (AI Workflow Orchestrator; Creative Generalist) have no clean SOC home in O*NET. That is itself a finding the cross-walk should surface rather than suppress. Checked against our own alumni data, and refined (GC Alumni corpus, internal data): the taxonomy does not fail to place these roles — it places them inconsistently. A title search for workflow, automation, and technologist roles found 39 positions held by 31 of 1,029 validated graduates (about 3%) in September 2026, and 42 positions held by 34 of 1,337 validated graduates (about 2.5%) in the October 2026 recount. One recognizable job family is spread across several SOC major groups — including 11, 15, 17, 43, and 51 in the September count, and seven major groups in October — and two titles are coded two incompatible ways. Only 4 of these positions are first jobs, in both counts, so this is a destination graduates reach rather than an entry point. Two limits on that evidence: it is a keyword search over chosen title vocabulary, so it finds only roles that announce themselves in the title — a Creative Generalist doing AI-native work as a “Senior Designer” is invisible to it, and the roughly 3% is a floor for title-visible cases rather than an estimate of the cluster; and “technologist” alone is not a marker of strain (“Graphic Imaging Technologist”, a Clemson GC co-op title, codes consistently to Prepress Technicians all 7 times it appears).

One empirical anchor from the Course-Skill Atlas research is useful for calibrating how many course captures are needed before a program-level aggregate stabilizes: the Atlas authors found that about nine syllabi are enough for a stable skill profile for a field of study at an institution in a given year (“the sufficient number of syllabi is equal to 9,” p. 17). That is a useful prior for deciding when the captured dataset is sufficient to attempt the cross-walk.

§ 4Hypothesis C — Uncertainty & convergence discipline

This hypothesis governs how every claim produced by the framework should be stated and treated, which makes it in a sense the most foundational of the three. Its core — that neither models nor humans are a clean oracle, and that LLM errors are correlated — is well supported. How much of the remaining error is irreducible is not settled (see below).

No reliable oracle

There is no reliable oracle for KUD scoring. On mapping curricula to 21st-century competency frameworks, LLMs “perform well in coarse-grained classifications, achieving over 70% agreement with human annotations,” but “fall short of human-level performance in fine-grained assessments of competency coverage” (Xu et al., 2026, p. 22). But humans are not a clean oracle either. In the same study, two trained annotators began at Cohen’s κ = 0.29 and 0.17 on two of the three frameworks (O*NET and ESDC), against 0.84 on the third. Most disagreements came from confusion between “clearly irrelevant” (0) and “insufficient information” (NA). Once the coding guidelines spelled out that rule and the annotators re-coded, agreement rose to κ = 0.94 and 0.92 (p. 7). Agreement is also low in other open-ended scoring: essay raters have been reported at a mean r of about 0.54 (Casabianca et al., 2017†), and a high-stakes teacher performance assessment (PACT, the predecessor of edTPA) at weighted κ = 0.17 across 19 candidates (Lyness, Peterson & Yates, 2021). For reference, Landis and Koch label κ of 0.61–0.80 “substantial” agreement, while noting that “these divisions are clearly arbitrary.” The Xu et al. annotators show both sides: low agreement on abstract constructs at first, and high agreement once a clarified coding rule was agreed. So “humans correct the AI to truth” is not an accurate description of the loop; it is more accurately “multiple partially-reliable signals are combined and disagreement is surfaced as uncertainty.”

How much of the residual is irreducible? (open)

An earlier draft claimed that the remaining model error is “dominated by label noise, genuine ambiguity, and under-specified inputs.” That is this document’s hypothesis, not a finding of the sources, and the closest source points the other way in part. Xu et al. (2026) trace LLM errors to the model side: “systematic over-interpretation of instructional keywords and difficulty identifying relevant pedagogical evidence in long, unstructured texts” (p. 22). Their reasoning-based prompting strategy gave “modest but consistent performance improvements” (p. 22). Both suggest that part of the gap is reducible.

Some uncertainty is irreducible, though, and the theory for that is sound. Hüllermeier and Waegeman (2021) describe aleatoric uncertainty as “the irreducible part of the uncertainty” (p. 8), as opposed to epistemic uncertainty, which more data or knowledge could reduce. They add that the two “should not be seen as absolute notions” but are “context-dependent” (p. 9). Where labels themselves disagree, they cap what any model can score against them. In object recognition, Tschirschwitz and Rodehorst (2024) estimate the bound as mAP ≈ 0.836·α + 0.197, where α is annotation agreement, and find that state-of-the-art results “approach this upper bound for the well-studied LVIS dataset” (p. 8). They also note that language differs from vision because “language inherently involves ambiguities” (p. 8). So the formula is an analogy here, not a value to borrow. One cannot exceed the reliability of one’s own labels; how close current KUD scoring is to that ceiling is an open empirical question (§7).

Where the ceiling is reached, the right response is to characterize and report it, not to engineer against it. The data-perspectivism program (Plank, 2022; Aroyo & Welty, 2015†) makes this point: the distribution over raters is itself a finding — disagreement signals genuine ambiguity in the underlying construct, not noise to be averaged away.

The measurement-theory home: Generalizability Theory

The measurement-theory home of “manage uncertainty, don’t chase it” is Generalizability Theory (Cronbach et al., 1972; Brennan, 2001†). G-theory decomposes score variance into rater, item, and occasion facets; the D-study identifies where investment actually buys reliability. The result this document leans on: when tasks vary more than raters do, adding raters buys little while adding items or tasks buys a great deal. For example, in one illustrative two-facet design, going from 2 to 8 raters lifts Eρ² from about 0.62 to 0.69, while going from 2 to 8 tasks lifts it from 0.62 to 0.82. (These are illustrative numbers; the earlier draft gave no source for them and we have not verified them. Which facet dominates is an empirical question for each design.) If task variance dominates in KUD scoring, then when faculty disagree on a score the better answer is more or better evidence items, not more adjudicators.

Standard Error of Measurement (SD · √(1−r); Lord & Novick, 1968†) is the formal confidence stamp. At r ≈ 0.65, the SEM is about 0.59 standard deviations, so a 95% band (±1.96 SEM) is about ±1.16 SD. That is wider than ±1 depth level whenever scores spread with a standard deviation above about 0.86 levels — a condition to check on real data, not a given. Where it holds, scores within about one level of each other are effectively ties. The honest output of a derived KUD score is an interval, not a point. Many-Facet Rasch Measurement (Linacre, 1989†) is the formal treatment of rater disagreement as signal: rater severity is modeled as a facet to calibrate rather than noise to average.

The correlated-error finding: why graduate-outcome validation is necessary

One finding from the uncertainty literature is strong enough to change how the framework’s external check should be framed. Wisdom-of-crowds convergence works when errors are independent and zero-mean. But LLM errors are correlated. On the HELM leaderboard, pairs of models “agree on average about 60% of the time when both models are incorrect”; correlation is higher for models from the same developer or with the same base architecture, and “larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors, even with distinct architectures and providers” (Kim et al., ICML 2025, arXiv:2506.07962). A 9-judge panel drawn from 7 model families delivers an effective sample size of about 2.18 — an independence ratio of 24.2% — and the best single judge “matches or outperforms the full panel across all conditions” (Kohli, 2026, arXiv:2605.29800). Any bias that all LLM judges share — inflating Know because syllabi use recall verbs, rewarding detailed syllabi, underweighting assessment evidence — survives every panel size.

The only check on that error class is comparison against an external signal the models did not generate: the graduate-outcome study. This reframes that study from “nice external validation” to the structurally necessary instrument for the one error class convergence cannot reach. It is also the external anchor B2 depends on — though, as the open questions note, the destination data constrains B2’s parameters rather than directly fitting them.

The convergence machinery

Within those constraints, standard forms exist for the internal confidence signal: self-consistency sampling (Wang et al., 2022 — sample several scorings and use their agreement, which the authors call “consistency,” as the uncertainty estimate); panel-of-LLM-judges aggregation (Verga et al., 2024); calibration and Expected Calibration Error (Guo et al., 2017); and selective prediction (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017/2019† — the rigorous version of “flag low-confidence rather than guess”). Routing items with cross-pass agreement below roughly 0.70 to human review would be this framework’s own design choice, not a value from that literature. These mechanisms can catch the variable, sample-to-sample part of the error; they are blind to the shared systematic layer.

The representation contract

Hypothesis C is most usefully understood not as a standalone claim but as a representation contract every derived claim must conform to: confidence-stamped, revisable, stated as intervals rather than points, with “disagreement is surfaced as uncertainty, not falsely resolved” as the default. The framework’s honest framing — that it is stronger as a conversation engine than as a measurement instrument — is the application of this contract at the program level. The comparative claim (“more evidence-grounded, explicit, contestable, and aggregable than committee oral tradition”) is strong; the absolute claim (“correct”) is not. The basis stamp and evidence-above-zero rule in the existing framework are precedents for C applied at the course level.

§ 5Skill relatedness & embeddings

A recurring question in applying the framework is how it relates to the modern machinery of text embeddings and attention mechanisms, which seem to do something similar. The answer is that the distinction is informational rather than mechanistic.

The resolved question

A static embedding represents a term as one fixed vector: one scalar per pair of terms (symmetric cosine), no slot to condition on depth or dimension. The limitation of this representation is not mechanistic — attention can represent asymmetric, depth-conditional, and hierarchical relations. The limitation is informational: the relevant information (what dimension, at what depth, under what evidence) is not present. When that information is supplied in context, much of the gap closes: on curricular skill-matching, an embedding-only approach had 24% of its top-ten skills rated 4 or higher on a 1–5 alignment rubric, against 69.5–82% for retrieve-then-reason approaches, where embeddings retrieve a shortlist of 20 candidate skills and an LLM reasons over it (“From Course to Skill,” Xu et al., 2025, Table 2). We read that jump as mainly a matter of generic, context-free representation versus task-specific context, rather than embeddings versus reasoning. That reading is ours: the paper’s retrieve-then-reason condition adds both retrieved context and an LLM, so it does not separate the two.

KUD+depth is the orthogonal factorization a generic token embedding entangles. The framework already separates “which competency × which dimension (Know/Understand/Do)” from “how deep (0–5)” by design. A depth-factored vector space — depth as an explicit learned orthogonal axis, content on another — would be the continuous learned version of what the framework authors discretely. If, as hypothesis C suggests, much of the remaining error comes from label disagreement rather than representation, a fancier representation is unlikely to move the needle, and a depth-factored embedding would be a cost and scale optimization rather than an accuracy win. How much of the residual is irreducible is still open (§4), so this is a working expectation, not a result.

Embeddings in the durability topology

Embeddings have a legitimate role in B: defining the reinforcement topology (which competencies are neighbors) for the pull-up extension. Embeddings propose the neighborhood; depth-aware reasoning decides the pull-up strength and whether the KST surmise relation holds. This is consistent with the retrieve-then-reason principle: similarity at the embedding level is recall, not adjudication.

Domain-specific embedding engines

A distinct question is whether a domain-tuned embedder would materially improve retrieval. The reports below, none of which we have verified against a full text, suggest the gain is real but bounded and confined to the recall stage. Vendors and researchers report that domain embedders beat generic ones by meaningful margins on in-domain vocabulary: voyage-finance-2 at 54% vs. 38.5% for OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small on SEC filings, and voyage-law-2 with gains of more than 10% on legal sets after training on over one trillion legal tokens (Voyage AI, 2024†; vendor figures). MatSciBERT and ChEmbed report domain training as indispensable for materials and chemistry†. Job- and skill-specific encoders exist (JobBERT, ESCOXLM-R, CareerBERT†) and could be adapted for curriculum-to-career matching. UniSkill (Musazade et al., 2026) is not an embedder but a dataset for this task: “the first open-source dataset for aligning course learning goals to standardized occupational skills.”

The sharpest practical finding from this literature is what might be called the leaderboard trap. Per the FinMTEB benchmark (Tang & Yang, 2024/2025), a model’s general-benchmark (MTEB) rank does not predict its domain performance (Spearman rank insignificant, p > 0.05; general models drop 0.9–8.6 points on finance tasks, Table 2 of the 2024 paper). A general benchmark rank cannot be used to pick an embedder for a specialized domain task. The only valid test is an empirical bake-off on the target data.

We found no off-the-shelf print, graphics, or packaging embedder; searches in that space surfaced degree programs, not models. Adjacent options include materials-science models (MatSciBERT, MatBERT, MELT — substrates, inks, polymers, adhesives), chemistry models (ChEmbed — inks, coatings, colorants), and emerging perceptual color–text work relevant to color management. Building a GC-domain model from scratch would require large-scale continued pretraining (voyage-law-2 is reported to have used over one trillion legal tokens†), which is not available here. The realistic range is adapter-based or contrastive fine-tuning on synthesized pairs over a small labeled set and the existing course corpus — which would buy recall gains, not a commercial-embedder-grade model.

What would need to be tested

Before any domain fine-tuning, an empirical embedder bake-off on the curriculum retrieval task itself would establish whether the generic embedder is actually the bottleneck and whether the failure is in recall (a domain embedder would help) or in depth adjudication (it would not). The leaderboard trap means this cannot be inferred from general benchmarks.

From resolved question to concrete proposal: the competency-graph substrate

An update grounds this section in measurement rather than expectation. A 2026-07 audit scored the shipped supply→canonical alignment — the step that maps a course's captured competencies onto the canonical career sub-competencies so supply and demand share a key — against a fixture of 58 real production matches, judged by a cross-family model panel. The result was stark and judge-robust: on the shipped (pre-fix) alignment, roughly 80–92% of matches were not the same skill — a lenient same-family judge put it at 58%, but two stronger, independent judges (a different model family and a newer model) put it at 82–92%, and even after the deployed prompt fix and with the best available model about 70–79% remain wrong. The error is not random. It concentrates where the tool force-matches a competency the course does not really serve, and where topical resemblance is credited as skill identity (“deliver a presentation” scored as “proposal development”; a disposition like Resilience scored as the technical skill “timeline management”). Neither a stronger model nor a better prompt closed it — the best model-plus-prompt combination still left about 70% wrong. This converts §5's “resolved question” from a design expectation into a measured failure with a diagnosed cause.

The diagnosis splits the problem into two operations on one object — a competency graph. Identity (“is this course competency the same skill as this canonical node?”) is a reject/rule/dimension judgment, not a retrieval problem: on a candidate set of five to seven per target, an embedding second-opinion rescued about one in thirty-four of the LLM's errors, because topical-proximity vectors reproduce the over-crediting that causes them — and this held for a generic embedder, a domain-instruction-framed one, and a topic-only variant alike. Relatedness (“do these skills relate, stack, or feed each other?”) is the embedding-native operation — but, as Liu et al. (2024) measured on sixty-five million adverts, similarity and complementarity diverge (strongly co-occurring skill clusters can carry low semantic similarity), and expert-authored groupings miss co-occurrence links. The unifying move: a canonical competency graph whose nodes carry identity and depth (an expert-and-evidence judgment) and whose edges carry relatedness (a data judgment). Coverage-identity consumes the nodes; scaffolding, prerequisite, and gap reasoning consume the edges. This is the same nodes-and-connections shape as Dynamic Learning Maps and the surmise-linked items of Knowledge Space Theory, applied to career competencies. The split is a useful lens, not a clean partition: the framework's own prerequisite edges (§12 of the background) are faculty-authored and depth-laden — expert identity relations that happen to live on edges — and disambiguating two near-identical nodes can itself require the relation between them. What the lens gets right is the assignment of the dominant work: bulk relatedness to data, identity-and-depth to expert judgment.

The proposal is a three-stage program on that shared substrate. Stage 1 enriches the nodes: each canonical sub-competency gains explicit inclusion/exclusion boundaries, positive and negative examples, sibling distinctions, and an evidence pattern. The model for this is the coding-guideline fix that took Xu et al.’s (2026) two human annotators from κ ≈ 0.2 to ≈ 0.9 on two abstract competency frameworks: an explicit rule separating “clearly irrelevant” from “insufficient information,” followed by re-coding. That result is about humans. The same paper found that “incorporating human-defined competency descriptions into prompts does not improve LLM performance” (p. 21) — a direct caution for this stage (see the callout below). Stage 1 also tightens matching to extract-then-align: extract the course competency's pedagogical elements via guided questions, then align against the enriched boundaries with cited evidence and a default of “not served.” This step has modest outside support: Xu et al.’s reasoning-based prompting, which has models “extract and reason over structured instructional elements before making judgments,” gave “modest but consistent performance improvements,” though “its effectiveness depends on model capacity” (p. 22).

Stage 2 adds a faculty-confirm loop on the resulting matches, so trust derives from human adjudication rather than model accuracy. Stage 3 adds the relation edges — similarity from topic embeddings, complementarity from co-occurrence, as in Skill2vec (which treats “every skills in the same job description” as related) and Liu et al. (2024). The Course-Skill Atlas cannot supply this on the curriculum side: it provides no co-occurrence or complementarity measures, and its released data are aggregated “at the institution-year-FOS level” because the authors’ research contract “requires that we do not release information at the individual syllabus level” (p. 14). Curriculum-side co-occurrence would have to come from our own captured courses, or from coarser co-occurrence across the Atlas’s aggregated profiles. The stages are sequenced Stage-1-first not because enrichment is strictly required downstream — a faculty-confirm loop can run on today's thin nodes, and edges can be derived from bare node text — but because enriched nodes would strengthen both, and Stage 1 both attacks the measured failure directly and is the cheapest to pilot. (One caveat the audit itself raised: the single biggest measured lever was not enrichment but a one-line model upgrade — producing alignments with a newer model cut error more than the prompt fix did — so that upgrade is pursued in parallel and enrichment is additive, not the primary remedy.)

Why this is a proposal, not a result — and how it is gated

The load-bearing assumption — that richer node definitions make an LLM better at identity rather than merely longer-winded — is unproven, and two data cut against it. In our own audit, the single intervention that measurably helped was a terse rule (“a disposition is not technical-skill supply”), not verbose prose. And Xu et al. (2026) found that adding human-defined competency descriptions to LLM prompts “does not improve LLM performance” (p. 21); what helped their human annotators was a clarified coding rule. There is no comfortable ceiling to borrow: the closest comparable, Xu et al. (2025), reached 82% only on a coarser metric — of the top-ten skills its best retrieve-then-reason pipeline extracted, 82% were rated ≥4 on a 1–5 alignment rubric, over a pool of 2,070 O*NET skill descriptions — an easier, different quantity than our single same-vs-different identity judgment over five to seven candidates, so our own achievable ceiling is unknown and must be set empirically from the pilot, not imported. What the literature does establish is that no model reaches human-level precision on fine-grained pedagogical reasoning (Xu et al., 2026), and that humans themselves agree only moderately (Cohen's κ ≈ 0.45, “moderate agreement,” on 300 doubly annotated course-sentence/skill pairs, most of them high-similarity “hard” pairs; Musazade et al., 2026) — so the target is “defensibly better than today, human-adjudicated,” not a fixed accuracy number. The proposal is therefore pilot-gated. Enrich two or three high-collision nodes and measure on a genuinely held-out split, with three separations to avoid the contamination the first review caught: (i) author node examples from snapshots disjoint from the validation fixture — the audit fixture is ~97% not-correct and so cannot supply positive examples, which must be faculty-authored; (ii) label that authoring set with the cross-family model, not the same model that also grades the gate; (iii) have the should-match recall set authored by faculty other than the node-boundary authors, so the metric is not “the definition-author grading their own exam.” Report both precision and recall, because default-to-“not served” trivially improves precision by matching less; use a graded rubric with an explicit “insufficient evidence” code (distinct from “out of scope”); and judge with a cross-family panel plus a mandatory faculty spot-check, never a single same-family judge. Only a judge-robust improvement on held-out data greenlights the full build. Design detail lives in the node-enrichment spec (2026-07-25); the empirical backing is the alignment-quality measurement of the same date.

§ 6Scale representation — integer judgments, continuous derivations

Hypotheses B and C both depend on a representation decision that can be stated once and applied throughout: integer at the boundary, float in the model.

Faculty input, storage, and display of direct judgments stays integer (0–5). The depth scale is ordinal with anchored rungs — each integer has a behavioral definition; a 3.5 has no anchor (there is no defined behavior halfway between “recall” and “use correct terminology”). Soliciting decimals would invent precision faculty cannot reliably produce and would collapse already-fragile inter-rater reliability. The evidence-above-zero gates are themselves integer thresholds. The faculty-facing interface stays on integers.

Every derived quantity is float, because the operations themselves produce continuous values — and rounding mid-computation destroys the signal those operations exist to surface:

  • Decay / durability (B2): depth(t) = depth⊂0; · e^(−t/τ) (or an HLR/FSRS retrievability function) returns a real number. The entire point of modeling bleed-off is to catch partial erosion below the next rung — a D4 decaying toward D3.2 — which integer rounding erases.
  • Prerequisite gap (durability-adjusted): a depth-weighted aggregation over upstream prerequisites is a float. The load-bearing output is the gap between expected entry depth and supplied depth, where 0.3 versus 2.1 is a real and decision-relevant distinction.
  • Pull-up / reinforcement: a partial increment or decay-slowing contribution is continuous by nature. A D2 touch does not promote a D4 to D5; it adds a fractional boost and slows decay.

Two guardrails follow. First, a float should never be written back into a faculty-score field and should never be displayed as a faculty-facing score. Derived results are shown as bands or arrows (“entered D4 → estimated D3.1 at graduation, erosion flagged”); the raw float stays internal for ranking and thresholding. Concretely this is two stored layers, not one: the integer judgment persists as the immutable, source-of-truth observation in each snapshot, and the durability- and transfer-adjusted depths are a separate, derived float layer computed from it — so an inferred depth never overwrites what faculty actually judged, and the provenance of every value (observed versus model-inferred) is preserved. Second, treating the 0–5 ordinal as interval — so that 4−2 equals 3−1 — is an assumption, not a fact. It is the same simplification every GPA and Likert mean makes, defensible but to be stated explicitly, because hypothesis C forbids over-claiming precision. The corollary is that the honest form of every derived value is a distribution, not a point: “D3.1 ± 0.6” rather than “D3.1.” The float layer carries an uncertainty band.

The boundary between integers and floats is the hypothesis C representation contract applied to hypothesis B’s arithmetic.

§ 7Open questions

These are the questions that would need answers before the quantitative hypotheses above could move from plausible design directions to validated components.

  1. Prevalence of lone peaks vs. reinforced threads. How common are lone-peak competencies (one high-depth hit, never reinforced) versus reinforced threads in the actual captured GC snapshots? The answer determines whether the durability hypothesis addresses a real problem in this curriculum or a non-issue.
  2. Decay and consolidation parameters (B2). What decay rate applies to academic competencies across inter-semester gaps? How many exposures, at what variety and spacing, consolidate a competency into a sticking depth? These are empirical unknowns. The HLR/FSRS form supplies the structure; the parameters must be fitted against a longitudinal retained-capability signal, which graduate destination data alone does not supply (see the shared-dependency note above).
  3. Pull-up mechanism. Is the neighbor-lift effect durability-only (a high-depth neighbor slows a related competency’s decay by embedding it in an active schema), or does it include a level-floor entailment (reaching D4 in a higher competency implies a floor on its subsumed prerequisite)? The transfer literature suggests modeling both conservatively. How the KST surmise boundary should be drawn in the specific GC competency graph is an open empirical question.
  4. Neighborhood definition. Should the similarity neighborhood for the pull-up mechanism be authored (from the competency taxonomy and its prerequisite structure) or embedding-derived (and then adjudicated)? Knowledge Space Theory’s surmise relation gives a principled and falsifiable definition of “neighbor”; whether the authored prerequisite structure is rich enough to substitute for an embedding-derived neighborhood is an open question.
  5. Cross-walk feasibility. Is the Course-Skill Atlas GC slice rich enough, given O*NET’s known coarseness for GC-specific competencies, to be a useful benchmark? Would “no clean match” dominate the output for GC specifics, and if so, what does that imply about the external benchmark’s value as a check?
  6. Embedder bottleneck. Does an empirical bake-off show the generic campus embedder limiting retrieval on the curriculum matching task? And if so, is the failure in recall (where a domain embedder would help) or in depth adjudication (where it would not)?
  7. Depth-factored embedding at scale. Is the curriculum ever large enough that per-pair LLM reasoning calls become impractical, justifying investment in a learned depth-factored embedding? Or does depth-in-context remain the better tradeoff indefinitely?
  8. How much of the scoring error is irreducible? §4 separates two readings: error that comes from genuine ambiguity in the construct and disagreement in the labels (irreducible, so it should be reported, not engineered against), and error that comes from the model, such as over-reading instructional keywords or missing evidence in long documents (reducible, so better prompting, extraction, or models should help). The closest study (Xu et al., 2026) points to model-side causes and finds modest gains from structured reasoning. Measuring human–human agreement on the same KUD items, next to model–human agreement, would show how close current scoring is to the label ceiling.
  9. Shared dependency on graduate-outcome data — and its limit. The correlated-error finding (C) establishes that graduate-outcome data is the only check on shared systematic LLM bias. The B2 durability model depends on the same study as its external anchor — but the study's destination data constrains B2 without being able, by itself, to fit its decay parameters (that needs a competency-level retained-capability signal the destination study does not supply). So the two share a dependency but not the same sufficiency: the planned study (an observational, LinkedIn-based destination-and-trajectory study) is sufficient for the C bias-check and for criterion-relevance, but cannot fit B2 — that needs a contact-based competency-assessment instrument it does not run. The most the destination/trajectory data can offer toward the durability question is weak observational proxies (does a deeply-built competency keep appearing in a graduate's role demands over time? does it persist in their self-listed skills?), which describe a pattern but do not measure retention or fit a curve. B2-parameter-fitting and transference are therefore out of scope for this study and would require a separate one. The open question is whether that separate study is worth mounting.
