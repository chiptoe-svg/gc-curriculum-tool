Research synthesis & tool design

Curriculum Maps

How curriculum maps are built, why so many end up as paperwork, and what the research says a mapping tool must do to be worth a faculty group’s time — read against the design of the GC curriculum builder.

Date 2026-11-15 Status Research synthesis for the curriculum builder Audience Faculty, curriculum committee

§ 1Why this document exists

A curriculum map is a picture of a degree program: which courses teach which skills and ideas, in what order, and how well. Most departments have made one at some point, usually a spreadsheet with courses down one side, outcomes across the top, and letters in the cells. The department is now building an interactive version. Faculty stack courses into the program’s three acts, watch each competency rise toward the levels a career path needs, and see where it falls short, fades, or depends on a course that comes too late.

Before committing to that design, it is worth asking what the published work on curriculum mapping says. Three questions organize this page. What is a curriculum map, and what is it supposed to do? Why do so many maps get made, filed, and never used again? And what features make a mapping tool useful to the faculty who have to build and act on it?

The page ends with an honest accounting. Some of the builder’s choices rest on research. Some are framework commitments, defensible choices the department has made. Some are working assumptions that the tool’s own data will have to test. The fading estimate is the clearest example of the last kind.

How sources are handled

Every claim here comes from a source whose full text we hold, with the quoted passage and page recorded in the page’s citation ledger. A work we know about but do not hold in full carries a dagger (†). Some influential works on curriculum mapping are books or paywalled articles we could not obtain, so a few founding ideas are reported here through later authors who quote them.

Part I — What a curriculum map is

§ 2Origins and standard forms

Curriculum mapping took two routes into higher education. In school education, Heidi Hayes Jacobs’s Mapping the Big Picture† (1997) popularized mapping as a way for teachers to record what is actually taught across grades. In medical education, Ronald Harden’s AMEE Guide No. 21† (2001) is the guide both of the mapping accounts we hold cite. We do not hold Harden’s guide. Plaza et al. (2007) summarize it this way: “Harden detailed the 2 main functions of curriculum maps in medical education: to make the curriculum more transparent to all the stakeholders and to demonstrate the links between the various components of the curriculum.” Those components are “learning outcomes, learning opportunities, content, and assessment.”

In practice, three forms recur.

The coverage grid

Courses form the rows and program outcomes the columns. A mark in a cell means the course addresses that outcome. This is the simplest map and the most common one.

The introduce–reinforce–master matrix

The grid gains a level in each cell. In one University of Toronto Mississauga unit described by Rawle et al. (2017), “for each map element, instructors had to identify if it was taught and/or assessed, and at which level (introduced/reinforced/advanced)” (p. 80). A recent practitioner account by Nelson (2026) describes the same sequence as milestones: “early exposure and introduction to goals,” then reinforcement, then “final demonstration and mastery of goals.” This I/R/M form is the direct ancestor of the builder. The builder’s depth scales are a finer version of the same idea: K, U and D each run 0–5, where I/R/M has one three-step scale.

The alignment map

Outcomes are linked to the teaching and the assessment meant to produce and check them. Its theory is Biggs’s (1996) constructive alignment, which uses the “performances of understanding … align the teaching methods and the assessment” (p. 347). It also draws on Wiggins and McTighe’s backward design, which they extend to “the design of course syllabi and program frameworks using backward design” (p. 275). Their Stage 1 question, “What should students know, understand, and be able to do?” (p. 17), uses the same three terms as the tool’s Know / Understand / Do split (KUD+ Background).

§ 3What a map is for

Accounts of mapping name a short, consistent list of purposes. Rawle et al. note that “curriculum mapping can also be used to identify gaps (Lam & Tsui, 2013), and can lead to increased transparency (Harden, 2001)” (p. 75). Barthakur et al. (2024) describe it as being “used to identify potential gaps, redundancies and misalignments between assessments and the stated outcomes” (PDF p. 4). Put plainly, a map is meant to answer four questions:

  • Coverage. Is every program outcome taught somewhere, and where is it taught twice?
  • Progression. Does each outcome build from introduction to mastery, in an order students actually follow?
  • Alignment. Is what a course claims backed by what it teaches and assesses?
  • Transparency. Can faculty, students and reviewers see how the parts fit?

The builder adds a fifth question that grids rarely ask: how deep, and does it last until it is needed. That addition is what the rest of this page tests against the literature.

Part II — How maps fail

§ 4Stated vs. taught vs. learned

The most serious problem with curriculum maps is that they usually record the wrong curriculum. Education research has long separated three curricula. In the model used by the TIMSS international studies, “the TIMSS curriculum model has three aspects: the intended curriculum, the implemented curriculum, and the achieved curriculum”. The second two are “what is actually taught in classrooms, the characteristics of those teaching it, and how it is taught; and, finally, what it is that students have learned” (TIMSS 2011 Assessment Frameworks, Introduction, p. 10). Plaza et al. give the higher-education version: “the designed or intended curriculum is the institutional and program requirements, the delivered or enacted curriculum is the instructional delivery, and the experienced or learned curriculum is what the students actually experience.”

Most maps are built from syllabi and faculty self-report, so they describe the intended curriculum. Barthakur et al. are blunt about the consequence: “A map of the intended or declared curriculum is created, which may substantially deviate from the ways in which learners actually engage and are assessed.” They conclude that “it is, therefore, difficult to claim a curriculum map is valid, assuming that the evidentiary basis of validity is what students actually do, experience and achieve” (PDF p. 2).

The gap can be measured. Plaza et al. review a medical-school study that compared all three curricula for cultural competency and found it “was mostly ‘hidden’ and teachers reported a greater extent of coverage than did students.” A prerequisite can show the same gap. Sato et al. (2017) studied one microbiology lecture and lab pair after the prerequisite link between them was dropped, and found in that case that the lecture had not been helping students in the lab: “Both sources of data provided complementary evidence that MLec does not impact success in MLab.” Even some formal models simply assume the gap away. Frej et al. (2024) name as a limitation a “fundamental assumption” of their course recommender: “that completing a course will automatically result in the acquisition of the associated skills by the learners” (p. 8).

What this means for the builder. The toggle between a course’s stated objectives (syllabus) and its discovered outcomes (evidence-based course capture) is the literature’s central distinction built into the tool. In the builder, the stated reading is the syllabus-derived depth for each competency, and the discovered reading is how the newest approved capture (one per instructor) that has been scored against the competency catalog was scored. Stated objectives are the intended curriculum. Discovered outcomes come closer to the enacted and assessed curriculum, because the capture asks for assessment items, student reasoning and graded work. They are still not the learned curriculum of a given student cohort. A national dataset of skills inferred from U.S. course syllabi draws the same line about itself: its authors, who inferred taught skills from syllabus text, write that “it is difficult to confirm the effectiveness of our approach without wide-scale comprehensive exams to test the skills that students actually obtain during a course” and that “it is crucial to acknowledge that teaching does not necessarily equate to learning” (Javadian Sabet et al., 2024, p. 19). The builder already marks any figure that rests on a stated objective or a proposed course and never presents it as discovered. That rule is well supported and should stay visible at every level of the display, including the career-path summaries.

§ 5Presence-and-absence maps and compliance artifacts

The second failure is a map made for an outside reviewer. Accreditation is the most common reason departments start mapping. Jayalath et al. (2026) observe that mapping “is often applied in a simplistic, accreditation-driven manner that merely records the presence or absence of skills embedded in assessments” (p. 589). Barthakur et al. add that “few studies have adopted an analytical approach to ensure programme quality assurance steps are traceable to a fundamental level of these maps: single courses and their assessment items” (PDF p. 1).

A presence-and-absence map cannot tell a passing mention from sustained practice. In KUD+ terms, it cannot tell K1 from D4. Once the review is over, it has no further use. Nelson argues the opposite stance: “Curriculum mapping is not simply a one-time administrative project or bureaucratic task; it is an ongoing leadership responsibility.”

The builder already answers the first half of this critique, since depth replaces checkmarks. The second half, staying in use after the review, depends on whether faculty keep returning to the tool to make decisions (§7, §8).

§ 6Whose judgment, and how reliable

Every map rests on someone’s judgment that course X develops outcome Y to level Z. Those judgments are less consistent than the clean grid suggests. Barthakur et al. note that manual mapping from course documents “is not only time-consuming but susceptible to human error and subjective bias” (PDF p. 2). Plaza et al. note that a map built retrospectively from recollection is “subject to recall bias from faculty members and students”; their own map, built from syllabi, avoided that but rested on “the assumption that the intended curriculum was the same as the delivered curriculum.” When Jayalath et al. (2025) measured agreement directly, the expert reviewers who built their reference mapping reached a Krippendorff’s alpha of 0.70 overall, which the authors read as “a strong consensus among the two reviewers” (p. 299) and which still leaves room for the two to disagree. When the authors then compared a generative-AI mapper against those experts, agreement depended heavily on the skill: “Oral Communication stands out with near-perfect agreement” (p. 301), while “Written Communication and International Perspectives had lower alpha values” (p. 302). They conclude that difficulties persist with skills that are “less explicitly defined in course documents” (p. 305). The skill-by-skill figures come from one program and measure AI against experts; only the 0.70 is expert against expert. So they show that agreement varies by competency, not which GC competencies will be hardest to read.

What this means for the builder. A reading of D3 is an estimate with a spread around it, and the spread is wider for some competencies than others. The measurement-hypotheses spec already requires that derived values be shown as bands, not points (spec §4.2). The research supports applying the same humility to the inputs: when two readings disagree by a level, the builder should treat that as normal noise rather than a finding.

When software does the tagging

The same question arises when software proposes the cells. Two recent studies show designs that make a machine’s tag checkable. Lê, Abel and Laforge (2026) use a language model “as a constrained, evidence-producing tagger”: for each fragment of course material it may choose only among competencies a search has already shortlisted from the department’s own framework, and it must return the passage that supports each choice. Against a framework of 22 competencies from one university department, that design reached a micro-F1 of 0.57 and a macro-F1 of 0.50 (p. 1). That is better than the alternatives they tested and still far from certain. It comes from one small evaluation with a lightweight model, and the authors expect that “larger or domain-adapted models could further improve F1 and evidence quality” (PDF p. 13). Veys, Gasco and Decorte (2026) grade whether a skill fits a sentence by asking a model a fixed sequence of yes/no questions. One of them catches the error that matters most for a map: the skill is in the right field, but the activity is not there (“plausible, but the activity is not there”, p. 2). Asking the questions in order “produces labels closer to a human expert than asking the same model for the grade directly” (p. 5).

The project’s own measurement points the same way. In July 2026 it checked whether an AI could decide that a course competency is the same skill as a catalog competency; most of its matches were judged wrong, often because they shared a topic rather than a skill, and embedding similarity rescued almost none of them (internal measurement, not a published study). What this means for the builder. Its list of “related” competencies is a shortlist, not a judgment: for each competency, the three nearest competency cards in the same catalog stratum by embedding similarity, kept only above a similarity of 0.5. A model-judged pass over competency relations was considered and deliberately not run, because the July measurement gave no reason to trust its output unmeasured. That is also why related-competency support never raises a level and is off unless a plan switches it on (§12).

§ 7Buy-in and staleness

The third failure is social. Heileman et al. (2018), writing from experience with curriculum reform, describe the climate: “The mantra often stated when the subject of curriculum is broached is, ‘faculty own curriculum,’ taken by many to mean ‘stay out of the faculty’s business’” (PDF p. 2). A map imposed from outside meets that resistance. In the Toronto initiative, “one other department had to drop out because of the lack of support from other faculty members” (Rawle et al., p. 82). Language was a barrier too: “pedagogical jargon tends to be a barrier across disciplines” (p. 79).

The same accounts report what helped:

  • Start from the faculty’s own questions. “Biology tried to increase faculty buy-in by surveying all faculty at the beginning and asking them what curricular questions they needed to be answered and then making sure to follow up with those answers” (p. 82).
  • Show a working example. “Geography was able to earn faculty buy-in by showcasing curriculum maps that were being used by other departments” (p. 82).
  • Treat it as shared, paid work. Nelson’s program “approached curriculum mapping as collaborative, compensated work.”
  • Build in stages. “A master curriculum map does not come together all at once” (Nelson). Her program “started by creating a rubric for just one programme goal and then staggered remaining mapping work to align with our existing three-year review timelines.”

One payoff every department reported was the conversation itself. All of them “found that participating in the curriculum mapping process fostered a collaborative approach to discussing teaching and learning throughout the department” (Rawle et al., p. 82).

Staleness is the quieter failure. In the Toronto study, “most departments use excel to organize their curricular data” (p. 79). A spreadsheet map is out of date the first time a course changes. A map tied to live course captures, as the builder’s is, can avoid that, but only if each reading shows its date and version so faculty can tell a current reading from an old one.

Part III — What makes a mapping tool useful

§ 8Lessons from mapping projects

The held literature on tools, as opposed to mapping processes, is thin. It consists mostly of case reports from single institutions. No controlled study we hold compares a map faculty used with one they ignored. With that caution, the case reports agree on a few points.

  • Make the problems stand out. One Toronto institute set out to “create a coloured visualization that would highlight overlaps, gaps, and inconsistencies” (Rawle et al., p. 81). A map that needs careful reading to find its problems will not be read.
  • Answer questions faculty already have. These are the questions from the buy-in strategies above, such as “where do students first meet this?” or “what happens if we drop this course?”
  • Trace every claim to evidence. Barthakur et al. argue for maps that drill down from a program outcome to the specific assessment items behind it (§5), and Jayalath et al. (2026) map skills to individual assessments by weight. A faculty member who doubts a cell should be able to see why it says what it says.
  • Let software suggest and people decide. Nelson’s summary is typical: “AI cannot replace human judgment but it can quickly surface patterns and insights to help with decision-making.”

The builder’s interactive design, where faculty move a course and immediately see the consequences, fits these points. Whether faculty keep using it is an empirical question the department will answer by watching use, not by citing a study.

Objectives the map can check

An alignment map links outcomes to the teaching and assessment behind them (§2), but a written objective is usually a sentence, and a sentence cannot be checked against a depth target. The builder handles this inside a plan: a written objective belongs to a checkpoint, links to one or more catalog competencies, and is read as met or not through the K/U/D targets of those competencies; an objective with no link reads “cannot be checked.” In effect, an objective becomes a catalog competency, plus a target depth, plus the course’s own wording, and a compound objective links to several competencies, each with its own depth.

The same link does not yet exist for real courses’ syllabus objectives. There, the builder’s stated input reads the syllabus-derived depths per competency, which are not tied to any one objective sentence. A proposal, not built, would store each syllabus objective in that three-part form. Software would propose the competency (from a shortlist, as in §6) and a starting depth from the objective’s verb and its condition (“with guidance”, “independently”, “in unfamiliar conditions”), and the instructor would confirm both. There is some support for the verb step: in a study of five computing programs, two blind faculty raters assigning Bloom levels to competency statements agreed at a Cohen’s κ of 0.86 (Turaev et al., 2026, p. 1). But a Bloom level is not a KUD depth, outcomes with more than one verb were where raters split (“leveling by the leading verb … parts company with leveling by the highest-order verb”, p. 10), and Bloom says nothing about how independently a student performs, which is what the Do scale grades (KUD+ Background §5). The verb proposes; the instructor confirms.

§ 9Curriculum analytics: the structure of the program itself

A separate line of research treats the curriculum as a network and measures it. Aldrich (2014) treats “the curriculum as a complex system with … nodes representing courses and links between nodes the … course prerequisites” taken from the catalog. In that network, courses play “different roles in the overall organization, such as … information sources, hubs, and bridges” (p. 1).

Heileman et al. (2018) turn this into measures of structural complexity. Their starting assumption is that, “assuming instructional factors are held constant, student success (as measured by completion rates) and structural complexity are inversely related” (PDF p. 8). Two of their measures matter directly for a builder:

  • Blocking. When one course is the gateway to many others, “if a student is unable to pass the gateway course, they are blocked from attempting many of the other courses in the curriculum” (PDF p. 11).
  • Delay. “As long prerequisite pathways are created within a curriculum, the delay factor associated with the curriculum increases, and the degrees-of-freedom decreases” (PDF p. 11).

Their evidence is of two kinds, and both should be read with care. One is a simulation: “we developed a simulation model that allows us to study the impact of these components on hypothetical students within a simulated environment” (PDF p. 14). The other is a correlation for real students at one university. The paper also describes itself as “a prerelease created for the purpose of obtaining feedback” (PDF p. 1). At the University of New Mexico, ranking programs by complexity and regressing on outcomes showed that “every 17 point decrease in structural complexity corresponds to a 1% increase in the six-year graduation rate.” They also report “an inverse correlation between the perceived quality of engineering programs (as determined by US News & World Reports ranking) and the structural complexities of these programs.” They offer a conjecture, which they label as such, that “the simplest curricula (in terms of structural complexity) that allows students to attain a program’s learning outcomes yields the best student success outcomes” (PDF p. 21).

What this means for the builder. A competency map rewards adding: every new prerequisite link and every extra reinforcing course makes a trunk look sturdier. The analytics literature points to a cost on the other side: added links can lengthen prerequisite pathways, which raises delay, and can make a course block more of the curriculum. A builder that shows only the benefit will push faculty toward more tangled sequences. Showing a course’s blocking and delay alongside the competency trunks would let faculty weigh both.

Comparing a curriculum with demand, at program scale

Analytics can also set a whole portfolio of programs against the job market. Turaev et al. (2026) did this for the five computing degrees of one college, “comparing 1,922 course learning outcomes against 103,349 competencies extracted from a unified corpus of 5,186 deduplicated job openings” (p. 1), with each statement given a skill domain and, where it states a performance verb, a Bloom level. Most job-ad competencies state none: on the demand side “the majority of competencies are possessions for which no cognitive level can be honestly assigned” (p. 6). Two of their findings show what such a comparison can say. The shared core that every student takes “satisfies only about a third of the demanded competencies” (p. 1). And the curriculum sat “at a mean Bloom level of 2.68 against the market’s 3.63, a differential of −0.95 of a level” (p. 12). This is the shape of the builder’s question in another field: breadth (is it taught?) and depth (deeply enough?) read against the same scale on both sides. The study is a preprint about computing programs in one country, its depth is a Bloom level rather than a K/U/D depth, and its demand comes from job postings, not from employer judgment. It shows the comparison can be made and read at program scale, not what GC’s gaps are.

§ 10Learning progressions, spirals and prerequisite structure

The builder draws each competency as a trunk that rises over time. That picture has three bodies of research behind it, and each adds a caution.

Spiral curriculum. In Gibbs’s (2014) summary, “students revisit a topic, theme, or subject several times throughout their schooling, where the complexity of the topic is increased with each visit so the new learning is connected to the old learning.” The builder’s “reinforced thread” is a spiral seen from the side. The 3-Act deep dive covers Gibbs’s argument that the spiral works best at a tighter scale than whole program stages.

Learning progressions. In science education, Corcoran, Mosher and Rogat, summarizing their 2009 report, define learning progressions as “empirically grounded and testable hypotheses about how students’ understanding and ability to use knowledge and skills in core school subjects” grow. Two of their points apply directly. First, a progression is never finally proven: “we should not think that testing these propositions can establish the validity of a progression once and for all” (PDF p. 11). Second, lower levels are not always smaller versions of the top level. They “may reflect mistaken or imperfect understandings of the target concepts that have to be revised or abandoned before the student can move on, rather than, as standards seem often to conceive of them, correct but incomplete understandings” (PDF p. 5).

Prerequisite structure at the concept level. Knowledge space theory models a domain as items whose mastery depends on other items being mastered first. Its “outer fringe” of a student’s knowledge state is useful because “it tells us exactly what the student is ready to learn” (Doignon & Falmagne, 2015, p. 16). Course-level prerequisites are a rough stand-in for these concept-level relations, and Sato et al.’s case (§4) shows that the stand-in can fail.

What this means for the builder. The act targets and their starting values are a hypothesized progression, and should be presented that way: as a claim the department expects to revise as evidence accumulates. Corcoran et al.’s second point also bears on the ordinal-maximum rule (§14).

Part IV — Sequence and time

§ 11Prerequisites, flexibility and branches

Every program trades coherence against flexibility. A tightly sequenced program can promise that each course builds on the last. A flexible one lets students arrive by many routes. Nelson names the risk on the flexible side: “when students can complete the curriculum in any order, essential skills are often presumed to be learned somewhere along the way, rather than intentionally integrated and reinforced throughout.” Her own capstone expected mastery of a skill that many students were meeting there for the first time: “This made what should have been an opportunity for reinforcement and mastery into a high-stakes crash course.” Heileman et al.’s blocking and delay measures (§9) name the risk on the tight side.

The literature reviewed here does not give a rule for where the balance should fall. It does support three practical points for the builder:

  • A prerequisite link is a claim to check, not a fact. Sato et al. show, for one course pair, that a listed prerequisite can fail to deliver. The builder’s prerequisite checks (missing, out of order, in another branch) catch the structural problems. Whether the earlier course actually supplies the competency depth the later one needs is the separate question Q2 asks, and the trunk view should make that visible.
  • Each branch needs its own complete progression. If an alternative track skips the course where a competency is reinforced, students on that track get a lone peak, a skill reached once and never practiced again. The builder already judges each career path on the trunk plus that path’s own branches, which is the correct unit.
  • Count what students complete, not what the catalog lists. Turaev et al. put it directly: “A published plan of study is an inventory of opportunities rather than a record of attainment”, so a comparison “that counts every cataloged course equally therefore measures an idealized program that no student actually completes” (p. 2). The builder can draw the map for the shared courses only or for one branch’s students, which is the means to apply this: a competency reached only in a branch is reached by that branch’s students, not by everyone.
  • Flexibility has a cost students pay. Branches and electives multiply the routes through a program. The map should show the weakest route to each target, not only the typical one.

§ 12Reinforcement, spacing and fading

The I/R/M matrix assumes that “reinforced” means something. The memory and skill-retention research explains why it does, and why a single high-depth course is a weaker guarantee than a grid suggests. This section draws on sources already reviewed for the measurement-hypotheses spec (§2) and does not re-derive them.

  • Spaced practice beats massed practice for long-term retention. Massing practice “produces better short-term performance or recall of that procedure or information, but markedly inferior long-term performance or recall” (Bjork, 1994, p. 190). In Cepeda et al.’s (2006) meta-analysis, “there is no hint that massed presentation was preferable to spaced, whether retention interval was very short (less than 1 min) or very long (over 30 days)” (p. 359).
  • Unused skills decay, and cognitive skills decay faster. Arthur et al.’s (1998) meta-analysis reports “skill loss ranging from an effect size (d) of -0.01 immediately after training to a d of -1.4 after more than 365 days of nonuse” (p. 57, the abstract; the Discussion gives the first figure as -0.1, p. 85). It also found that “physical, natural, and speed-based tasks were less susceptible to skill loss than cognitive, artificial, and accuracy-based tasks” (p. 57).
  • Deep enough learning lasts. In Bahrick’s (1984) 50-year study of school Spanish, the share that survives in “permastore” “is a function of the level of original training, the grades received in Spanish courses, and the method of testing” (p. 1).
  • Transfer depends on conditions that are often not met. Perkins and Salomon (1992) conclude that “the conditions for transfer are stringent” (PDF p. 8).

What this means for the builder. The research supports the direction of the fade rule: a depth reached once and never revisited should not be assumed to hold until graduation, and repeated work near the peak should count for more than a single course. It does not supply the numbers. Arthur et al.’s effect sizes are in standard-deviation units on trained tasks, not KUD levels across semesters. The “about one level per act” figure, the halving per reinforcing course, and the halving for related-competency support are the owner’s working assumptions, as the engine’s own comments say. The fade rate is a visible, adjustable setting (none, half a level or one level per act), labeled as an assumption. Two findings bear on it. Decay differs by kind of task, so the builder’s single fade rate for Know, Understand and Do is a simplification, and the fade control says so; separate rates wait until the fade numbers can be fitted to graduate outcomes. And transfer is fragile, so related-competency support is off unless a plan switches it on, and even then it can only slow a fade, by at most half of one act’s loss, and never raises a level.

For display, the I/R/M vocabulary maps onto the builder’s threads cleanly. Introduced is a first reading at low depth. Reinforced is a later course working the competency within a level of its peak; a later passing mention does not count. Mastered is the act target met and still held at the checkpoint. The “fading” status is the case grids cannot show: reached, but not held.

Part V — Graphic communications

§ 13What the GC literature offers

Research on curriculum in graphic communications specifically is sparse. The most relevant held study is Bridges (2020), a modified Delphi study of the competencies and tools higher-education GC programs need. Its panel was drawn from “educators and industry professionals located in the southeast United States.” Of roughly 300 invited, “13 educators and 21 industry professionals committed to the research project” (p. 245), and response fell in later rounds. Panelists rated each competency “using a Likert scale ranging from one to seven” (p. 245). Its headline result: “Participants of the study overwhelmingly identified soft skills as being the most needed skills, while software applications were identified as the most needed tools” (p. 243). “Critical thinking skills were, again, the highest-ranking competency in round three” (p. 249).

How the builder can use it, and its limits. Bridges is a reasonable source for which competencies a GC career-path target should include and how heavily to weight them. It does not say how deep. The study rated importance, not required depth, and it has no K/U/D or level structure. The builder therefore keeps importance and depth apart. A career path’s importance weights decide which competencies belong to the path and weight its “% of the way” figure (and every weight is still a draft); they do not set the depth. Until faculty set required levels, every competency starts from the same values, about K2 U1 D1 after Act 1 and 3 on each dimension after Acts 2 and 3, labeled “starting values, not yet reviewed.” The panel is also small, regional, and pre-dates current AI tools. Bridges also ties back to §6. The competencies the panel ranked highest are general skills, such as “critical thinking skills, problem solving and analytical skills, teamwork, communication skills, and business and personal ethics” (p. 248), not named tools or processes. Jayalath et al. found mapping harder for skills less explicitly defined in course documents. The builder should not assume its readings for these competencies are as firm as those for a named software or process skill.

Part VI — Accounting

§ 14What is supported, what is committed, what is assumed

Research-supported

  • Mapping by more than presence or absence (Barthakur et al., 2024), including the weight each skill carries in an assessment (Jayalath et al., 2026), and the I/R/M idea of progression that underlies it (Rawle et al., 2017; Nelson, 2026).
  • Keeping the stated (intended) curriculum separate from evidence of what is taught and assessed, and never presenting one as the other (TIMSS; Plaza et al., 2007; Barthakur et al., 2024; Sato et al., 2017).
  • Treating prerequisite links as claims to check (Sato et al., 2017) and the curriculum as a network with hubs, bridges and blocking courses (Aldrich, 2014; Heileman et al., 2018).
  • Counting reinforcement and treating an unreinforced single peak as at risk (Bjork, 1994; Cepeda et al., 2006; Arthur et al., 1998; Bahrick, 1984).
  • Collaborative mapping built around faculty questions as a way to gain faculty buy-in (Rawle et al., 2017; Nelson, 2026). This rests on case reports, which describe buy-in; they do not show that the resulting maps were used.
  • Reading what students complete, branch by branch, rather than the whole catalog (Turaev et al., 2026).
  • Letting software propose tags only from a shortlist, with the evidence for each, and checking the hardest call (right field, wrong activity) as its own question, with people confirming (Lê et al., 2026; Veys et al., 2026; the project’s July 2026 measurement).

Framework commitments

  • The KUD+ split and the 0–5 anchored depth scales, which refine I/R/M (Background).
  • The three acts as checkpoints for targets (3-Act deep dive).
  • The ordinal maximum as the way to combine courses: depth is never summed or averaged. It is defensible because depth is ordinal. It also assumes growth is cumulative, and Corcoran et al.’s warning that early understandings may need to be undone is a reason to read a high maximum alongside the courses that produced it, not as a standalone figure.
  • Starting act targets, the same for every competency until faculty set them. Importance weights choose and weight competencies but do not set depth; Bridges (2020) supports the importance side only.
  • A written objective read as a catalog competency plus a target depth plus the course’s wording, live for objectives written inside a plan; for syllabus objectives it is a proposal (§8).
  • Competencies as bundles (catalog deep dive).

Working assumptions

  • The fade numbers: about one level per act, halved for each reinforcing course, slowed by a light touch or, when switched on, by a related competency at integration depth. The direction is supported; the magnitudes are not. The rate is an adjustable setting labeled as an assumption.
  • One fade rate for K, U and D, stated as a simplification in the fade control. Arthur et al. found decay differs by kind of task.
  • Time measured in acts. Acts are not equal in length, and the gap between courses varies within an act.
  • Related-competency support slowing the fade. The transfer literature (Perkins & Salomon, 1992) is a counterweight, so the support is off unless a plan switches it on, is bounded, and never raises a level. Which competencies count as related is an embedding shortlist, not a judgment.

Where the literature pushes against the current design

  • No cost side for structure. The builder shows what added links and courses do for competencies, but not what they cost students in blocking and delay (Heileman et al., 2018).
  • Uniform decay across dimensions (Arthur et al., 1998). The builder states the simplification; separate rates for K, U and D wait for the fade fitting.
  • No objective-level link for real courses. The stated reading of a course is a syllabus-wide estimate, not tied to its individual objectives, so a target cannot yet be traced to the objective sentence behind it (§8).
  • Certainty of inputs. Mapping agreement varies by competency and is weaker for skills loosely defined in course documents (Jayalath et al., 2025). The builder currently shows every reading with the same apparent firmness.

§ 15Design implications for the builder

  1. Keep the stated / discovered distinction visible at every level, including career summaries. The intended curriculum is not the learned one (TIMSS; Barthakur et al.). Supported.
  2. Let any cell be traced to the course reading and evidence behind it. Faculty must be able to see why a figure says what it says (Barthakur et al.). Supported.
  3. Show blocking and delay for each course next to the competency trunks, so adding structure has a visible cost (Heileman et al.). Supported.
  4. Flag lone peaks and count only near-peak revisits as reinforcement (Bjork; Cepeda et al.; Bahrick). Supported.
  5. Label every fading estimate as an assumption and keep the fade setting adjustable, defaulting to the cautious reading (Arthur et al.; measurement-hypotheses spec §2.5). Assumption.
  6. State that one fade rate for K, U and D is a simplification, and fit separate rates when the fade numbers are fitted to graduate outcomes (Arthur et al.). Assumption, flagged by research.
  7. Keep related-competency support off unless a plan asks for it, small, and never able to raise a level; treat the list of related competencies as a shortlist, not a judgment (Perkins & Salomon; spec §2.7; the July 2026 measurement). Assumption, constrained by research.
  8. Judge each branch as its own complete route, and show the weakest route to each target (Nelson; Sato et al.). Supported in principle.
  9. Present act targets as a hypothesized progression the department expects to revise (Corcoran et al.). Commitment.
  10. Show readings as bands, and treat a one-level disagreement between readings as normal (Jayalath et al., 2025). Supported.
  11. Build the tool around faculty questions, in plain language, and roll it out one goal or path at a time (faculty questions and plain language: Rawle et al.; staged rollout: Nelson). Supported by case reports.
  12. Use Bridges (2020) to choose and weight GC competencies, not to set depth targets (Bridges). Commitment.
  13. Judge supply by what students on each route complete, not by everything the catalog offers (Turaev et al.). Supported.
  14. Link each syllabus objective to catalog competencies with a target depth and the course’s own wording, with the verb proposing a starting depth and the instructor confirming it (Turaev et al. for the verb step; KUD+ for independence). Proposal, not built.
  15. When software proposes a tag or a relation, have it choose from a shortlist, show its evidence, and ask “is the activity actually there?” as a separate question; faculty confirm (Lê et al.; Veys et al.; the July 2026 measurement). Supported.
