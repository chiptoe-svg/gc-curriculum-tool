# More jobs or fewer: demand elasticity and GC work

This page condenses the program paper *Demand Elasticity and AI Jobs* (evidence as of October 2026), the companion to *AI and GC Careers*. Back to the [[ai-careers-brief|AI and GC careers brief]].

## What the evidence says

### The basic idea

Demand elasticity measures how much more of something buyers take when it gets cheaper. An elasticity of 0.6 means a 10% fall in price raises the quantity bought by about 6%; above 1, quantity rises by more than the price falls (Demand elasticity, §1).

The economist James Bessen writes an industry's employment as its output divided by output per worker. If productivity rises 10% and demand rises more than 10%, the industry needs more workers; if demand rises less, it needs fewer. In his words, "the rate of productivity growth determines the pace of employment change, but the elasticity of demand determines the sign." Cautions: it is an industry identity that Bessen never applies to an occupation; it uses elasticity with respect to productivity, not price; and demand is one route among several, alongside complementarity and new tasks (Demand elasticity, §1).

### What history shows

- **Industries rise, then level off or shrink.** US textiles and steel employment rose for a century or more, then fell; autos rose to the 1950s, then leveled off. Early elasticities were above 1 (textiles 1.3 in 1820, steel 2.2 in 1870, autos 2.0 in 1910); around 1950 they were below 1 (0.2, −1.3 and 0.4; Bessen suggests the steel figure may reflect a poor fit). His explanation is satiation: once large unmet needs were met, cheaper output did little to spur demand. Elasticities change slowly (Demand elasticity, §2).
- **Displacement and new tasks.** Acemoglu and Restrepo split automation's effect into displacement, productivity and reinstatement through new tasks. After 1987, displacement grew and new-task creation weakened, cutting labor demand by about 10% cumulatively. Their data are for industries, not occupations (Demand elasticity, §1).
- **Gains elsewhere, a smaller labor share.** Autor and Salomons, across 28 industries in 19 countries from 1970 to 2007, find own-industry job losses from productivity growth were more than offset by gains in customer industries and wider demand, while labor's share of value added fell by about 6 percentage points (Demand elasticity, §3).
- **The bank teller and the composing room.** ATMs roughly quadrupled between 1995 and 2010, yet US teller employment rose modestly (about 500,000 to 550,000, 1980–2010) as tellers became relationship sellers; Autor warns the case "should not be taken as paradigmatic." Closest to GC, a 1999 BLS history traces typesetting moving to customers' computers and a new desktop-publishing occupation appearing, with no employment counts (Demand elasticity, §2).

### How the AI-era models use elasticity

OpenAI's AI Jobs Transition Framework (vendor research, not peer-reviewed) sorts occupations into four archetypes. Its elasticity is a language model's answer to one hypothetical per occupation: if this occupation's output got 10% cheaper, how much more would buyers want over two to three years? The US report calls it "the least directly observed object" in the framework (Demand elasticity, §3).

- The median elasticity across the 923 occupations in OpenAI's public US data file is about 0.6 (the program's own tabulation); the European median is about 0.7.
- Graphic designers sit at about 1.45 on the US report's chart but 1.08 in the public data file; the two sources disagree elsewhere too. Any use of a graphic-designer elasticity has to say which source it uses.
- Elasticity alone does not fix the archetype; the deciding exposure and net-effect scores are not published.

Values for GC-relevant occupations in the public data file, which the paper calls model priors, not measurements (Demand elasticity, §3):

| Occupation | Archetype | Elasticity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Graphic designers | Grows with AI | 1.08 |
| Market research analysts and marketing specialists | Grows with AI | 1.03 |
| Marketing managers | Grows with AI | 1.03 |
| Customer service representatives | Higher automation risk | 0.68 |
| Printing press operators | Less immediate change | 0.65 |
| Desktop publishers | Higher automation risk | 0.61 |
| Prepress technicians and workers | Higher automation risk | 0.56 |

The size of the productivity shock is just as uncertain: Acemoglu puts AI's total productivity gain over ten years at no more than about 0.66%, while an Anthropic exercise (vendor research, "not a prediction") finds 1.8% a year under universal adoption (Demand elasticity, §3).

### What no one has measured

The paper found no measurement of AI-era demand elasticity for graphic design, prepress, packaging, marketing or print sales work; the published numbers are industry history or model priors. The early AI-era studies (all working papers, preprints, central-bank analyses or vendor research) measure no demand elasticity either (Demand elasticity, §4).

## Our reading

These are the paper's own inferences, labeled "our reading" there. None rests on a measured elasticity (Demand elasticity, §5).

- **Output may be elastic while headcount is not.** Assets, package versions and analysis can multiply as they get cheaper. Budgets and cost shares limit how much of that becomes jobs. Growth is most likely in roles that specify, govern and run the systems that produce the output.
- **Design.** The elastic part is the number of assets; the constrained part is per-asset execution under a flat budget. That fits the BLS projection and the Census earnings data better than OpenAI's "grows with AI" label does. The protected work is what AI complements: direction, judgment across media, and design decisions that carry cost, production and compliance consequences.
- **Marketing.** Cheaper analysis may bring in new buyers, such as smaller firms. Report production is the exposed layer, and the usual entry role sits in it, so graduates need evidence of judgment from the start.
- **Print and packaging.** Package demand follows the goods being sold. If artwork and prepress are a small share of a package's cost (no archived source gives that share), AI shows up as fewer prepress hours per product. Versions are the possible elastic exception, but no source yet shows cheaper prepress causing more versions. Compliance changes happen whatever they cost, and a person has to sign off.
- **Sales.** Quoting, reorders and order handling are an inelastic, substitutable layer. Technical selling grows with the complexity of what customers buy, which is complementarity more than price response.
- **The systems behind all four.** When output demand is elastic and budgets cap headcount, value moves to whoever builds and runs the system that produces the output: the Creative Technology & Systems target. New work is not automatic, since reinstatement through new tasks weakened after 1987.

Summary by career target (our reading throughout):

| Target | Likely elastic | Likely inelastic or substituted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Production & Operations | Package versions; process and quality systems | Prepress hours per standing product; routine estimating and scheduling |
| Sales Solutions & Account Management | Technical solution selling as complexity grows | Quotes, reorders, order handling |
| Brand Strategy & Experience | Measurement and insight for more buyers | Routine reporting and first-draft content |
| Purposeful Design & Creative Generalist | Number of assets; direction and design judgment | Per-asset execution under a flat budget |
| Creative Technology & Systems | Systems that produce variants and keep them accurate | Few known; evidence thin |

**Open questions** (Demand elasticity, §6): prepress and artwork's share of package cost; whether cheaper prepress has produced more versions; whether marketing budgets stay flat; how elastic demand for technical print and packaging sales is.
