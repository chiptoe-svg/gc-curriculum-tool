# AI and GC careers: what we see, what it means for the curriculum

This brief answers three questions for the curriculum committee. What do we see affecting our students' careers, particularly AI? What should we focus on? What should we be doing in the curriculum? Each answer links to a page that develops it. Every figure comes from the program's two papers, *AI and GC Careers* and *Demand Elasticity and AI Jobs* (evidence as of October 2026), or from the program's curriculum research. Most 2026 labor-market studies behind these figures are working papers, preprints, or central-bank and vendor research, so their numbers can change.

## What the evidence says

### What we see affecting students' careers

- **AI is changing tasks faster than headcount.** In US data, early-career hiring into AI-exposed work has weakened. How much of that AI caused is unresolved, and economy-wide employment shows no widespread displacement yet. On most of the 2026 evidence, remote work and the general hiring slowdown explain more of the weak entry rung so far than AI does. Two studies, from Texas and Sweden, do find AI-linked declines (AI and GC careers, §1). See [[what-is-changing]].
- **The pressure falls on the entry rung.** AI absorbs well-specified execution work, which is where new graduates have always learned. What remains asks for judgment that people used to build on the job (AI and GC careers, §1).
- **GC graduates work where AI is active.** About 64% of recent GC first jobs are in occupations OpenAI's framework labels "grows with AI," against 12% nationally. That is a label, not a forecast. GC graduates' share in higher-automation-risk occupations (17.1%) is close to the national rate (17.7%). These alumni figures are descriptive until the independent second-coder check, and may be refreshed when the alumni roster matching settles (AI and GC careers, §2).
- **Each GC market shows the same split.** In design, marketing, print and packaging, and sales, executing variants is being squeezed, while specifying, governing and integrating the systems that produce them is growing. The evidence is thin for sales roles (AI and GC careers, §2).
- **Cheaper output does not automatically mean more jobs.** Whether jobs grow depends on how much more buyers want once output gets cheaper. No one has measured that for any GC occupation; the published numbers are industry history or a language model's answers (Demand elasticity, §1, §4). See [[more-or-fewer-jobs]].

## Our reading

### What to focus on

- **Five career targets, read as one value chain.** Brand Strategy & Experience decides, Purposeful Design & Creative Generalist designs, Production & Operations makes, Sales Solutions & Account Management supplies, and Creative Technology & Systems enables. The thread across all five is understanding the downstream consequences of the work. See [[career-paths]].
- **Judgment over AI output.** The paper proposes treating judgment as the scarce skill: as AI makes production cheap, evaluating, selecting and directing work may become the scarce human contribution. The support is two workplace studies and suggestive, non-causal payroll evidence, not a tested result; the program's Background treats the same idea as a hypothesis to test, not a result (KUD+ Background, §16).
- **Systems and workflow.** Creative Technology & Systems, the target that builds the tools and systems other people use, is the program's highest priority and its least covered target (AI and GC careers, §3–§5).
- **Business acumen and measurement.** Testing, measurement, and the technical and fiscal side of selling are where growth shows; the program has added four business-acumen competencies to its catalog, not yet scored (Competency catalog, §09).
- **Domain depth that supports problem-solving.** The program's position is that problem-solving develops through domain depth, informative failure and reflection, not as a generic skill (Problem-solving deep dive). See [[areas-of-focus]].

### What the curriculum could do

**Proposed — for faculty discussion.** Things the committee could consider; none is decided, and the committee keeps its authority.

- **Re-weighting rather than replacing.** The AI evidence does not call for a new framework; it changes the weights (AI and GC careers, §5).
- **Asking for evidence AI can't fake.** A finished artifact no longer proves a student can do the work; assessment could lean on reasoning through new cases, adapting to changed conditions, and critiquing work.
- **Building the Creative Technology & Systems target**, which could start with an industry panel to define the role.
- **Teaching the content supply chain**, packaging versioning and compliance, and marketing paired with measurement.
- **Building the missing first rung into the program** through internships, co-ops and capstones that could leave students with evidence of systems and judgment work.

See [[curriculum-implications]].

### How sure we are

The direction is clearer than the size of any effect. The labor-market studies disagree about how much of the entry-rung weakness AI caused. The alumni figures are descriptive, pending the independent second-coder check. The program's coverage picture is partial and will change when coverage scoring is rerun. A quarterly review of new research, next due by 9 December 2026, will track the entry-rung question (AI and GC careers, §6).
