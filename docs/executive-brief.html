What the tool is

The GC Curriculum Tool is a working record of the Graphic Communications curriculum: what each course develops, how deeply, and how the courses connect to each other and to the careers graduates enter. It is built to help answer two questions the department has so far settled mainly through committee discussion and individual knowledge of courses:

Q1 How well does the curriculum build students toward the careers we claim to prepare them for?
Q2 For any individual course, do the prerequisites students walk in with actually support what the course expects?

Each instructor knows their own course well. What is harder to see is the program as a whole: where a skill is introduced, where it is practiced again, and whether a later course assumes something no earlier course builds. The tool records each course in a common format so those patterns can be read across the program.

How a course is described

Each competency a course develops is scored on three 0–5 scales: Know (recall and correct use of terms), Understand (reasoning about why), and Do (what a student can actually produce or perform). The approach, called KUD+, draws on Carol Ann Tomlinson’s work on differentiated instruction and is compatible with Wiggins & McTighe’s Understanding by Design.

The main rule is that scores rest on evidence of what students do, not on what a syllabus says the course aims for. A topic that is only listed in the syllabus scores zero; Know at 1 needs evidence the topic was actually taught. Know above 1, and any Understand or Do score, requires assessment evidence: a test item for Know, student-written reasoning for Understand, a graded piece of work for Do. Five foundational competencies (Agency, Attention to Detail, Resilience, Curiosity, Communication) are scored on Do only. Gaps between the three scales are treated as information — for example, a student who can use the terms but not explain the reasoning.

We use this rather than Bloom’s taxonomy for a practical reason. Bloom’s authors intend it for aligning objectives, activities, and assessments across a unit, course, or curriculum (Krathwohl, 2002, p. 212), but its levels describe kinds of thinking and it has no separate performance dimension. Jobs are usually described by the knowledge they require and the work they involve, which maps more directly onto Know and Do. The U.S. Department of Labor’s O*NET describes occupations by required knowledge, skills, and work activities, and its Level scale rates the level “needed to perform” a job — a demand rating, not a measure of what a learner has attained. Today O*NET serves as a reference point: three of the five career targets are tied to an O*NET occupation code, and the graduate-outcome study uses the same codes to classify where graduates work. The competencies themselves are written by hand for GC, not taken from O*NET. The next step will likely use O*NET’s Importance ratings to weight what each career target asks for (see below). The full academic case is in the Background document.

Problem-solving is handled differently from other competencies: the framework treats it as something that develops across the sequence of courses, through depth in the subject, attempts that fail and are revised, and reflection, rather than as a trait of any one course. Whether that actually happens across four years is one of the things still to be checked. The research behind this position is in Problem-Solving as a Program-Level Emergent Property.

What it does today

  • CourseCapture. An instructor reviews the course materials the tool has gathered (syllabus, Canvas content, uploads), then works through an AI-guided interview. The result is a dated, unchangeable profile of the course. 19 of the 59 courses in the catalog have been captured so far.
  • Explore. A chat tied to one course that helps an instructor think through a proposed change and estimate its effect on the course and its neighbors.
  • Program views. A matrix of captured courses against the competencies each career target needs, a prerequisite check for each course, and a first version of a scaffolding view that flags a course expecting a skill no earlier course introduces.
  • Objective assessment guide. On each captured course’s public wiki page: for every learning objective in the syllabus, which graded Canvas assignment or rubric row measures it, and which class-level numbers to collect at the end of the semester to show whether it was met.
  • Curriculum wiki. Pages generated from the captured courses, with a question-answering assistant that cites them.
  • Employer input. A guided form for industry partners to describe a role’s requirements. It is built; no employer has completed one yet, so the comparison of program coverage against employer demand (Q1) has not been run.

Faculty sign in with personal links. Invitations are going out now, and the shared department password will be retired once they are done. A detailed walkthrough is in Using CourseCapture & Explore.

How faculty stay in control

  • The AI drafts; faculty decide. Every score can be edited before a course profile is confirmed. Any score can be flagged with a named note, and the flag stays visible until someone resolves it.
  • Profiles belong to the instructor who confirmed them. They are visible to the program coordinator and the curriculum committee, and they can be retired but not deleted.
  • The curriculum committee keeps its authority. The tool provides information for its discussions; it does not make or approve changes.

Limits

  • Course scores describe opportunity, not attainment. They say what a course’s assessed work gives students the chance to show, not what any particular student learned. The assessment guide is a first step toward collecting evidence that objectives were met; that evidence is not yet fed back into the scores.
  • The AI’s readings can share blind spots. Published studies of AI-assisted curriculum mapping find agreement with experts that varies a good deal by skill (Zamecnik et al., 2024; Jayalath et al., 2025; see Background §7). This is why faculty confirmation is required and why an outside check is needed (below).
  • Coverage is partial. With 19 courses captured, program-level views are incomplete, and nothing yet tracks how the curriculum changes over time.

What comes next

The next design step is the career comparison (Q1). The proposal is to weight each career’s requirements by how important O*NET rates them for the occupation, to show a shortfall in an important skill on its own rather than averaging it away, and to flag skills that appear in one course and are never reinforced later. A later stage would account for skills fading when they are not used, once there is data to estimate that. This is designed, not built; details are in the Measurement Hypotheses document. The student advising view is specified but not yet built.

How we will check it

The tool cannot confirm on its own that its readings match how graduates actually fare. A Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study compares the competencies the tool emphasizes with where GC graduates from the past ten years actually work, using failure criteria set in advance so that the results can count against the framework as well as for it. Until it reports, the framework’s larger claims should be read as working hypotheses. Full design: Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study.
