**What the secondary survey claimed (per the task brief), checked claim-by-claim:**

1. "65 million workers across 280,000 firms, 2015–2025" — **CONFIRMED**, verbatim from the abstract (p. 1): "U.S. résumé data covering 65 million workers at more than 280,000 firms (2015–2025)." (Note: the body text gives slightly larger rounded figures — "roughly 66 million unique workers," "281,111 firms" (pp. 4, 19) — the abstract rounds down; not a discrepancy worth flagging beyond this note.)
2. "Firm AI adoption identified by text analysis of job postings ('GenAI integrators')" — **CONFIRMED**, and more specific than the brief implies: it is explicitly **LLM-classifier-derived**, not keyword-only. Method (p. 21): postings are first keyword-flagged (603,152 of 198.8M postings, 0.30%), then an LLM classifier (Section A.9.1: `llama-3.1-8b-instant` via Groq) decides whether each is a genuine "integrator" role versus a generic AI mention, yielding 131,845 (0.066%) classified postings and 10,433 adopting firms (3.71% of the 281,111-firm sample) by March 2025.
3. "After adoption junior employment declined 8–10% relative to non-adopters within ~2 years, senior employment unchanged" — **CONFIRMED**, with precision: DiD estimate ≈ **9% decline** after six quarters (Fig. 6, p. 34); triple-difference ≈ **9% decline** after six quarters (Fig. 7, p. 35); staggered event study ≈ **8% decline** after eight quarters (Fig. 9, p. 40). Senior employment: the abstract's "trends remain largely unchanged" is the paper's own framing — in the data, senior employment at adopting firms *continued rising* on its pre-existing upward trajectory with **no break around 2023**, rather than being flat in level. Worth stating precisely in §16 as "no inflection in senior employment's growth trend," not "senior employment stayed flat."
4. "Decline concentrated in most-exposed occupations and driven by slower hiring, not separations/promotions" — **CONFIRMED** on both counts. Occupational exposure (§5.3, Fig. 8, p. 38): junior employment in high-GenAI-exposure occupations contracts ~7 log points relative to low-exposure occupations at adopters between 2022Q4–2025Q1, while the senior series in the same occupations keeps rising. Flow decomposition (Table 5, p. 44): adopting firms hired **4.0 fewer juniors per quarter** post-2023Q1 relative to non-adopters (≈80% of the pre-period mean of 5.06/quarter), separations fell by only 1.1/quarter (partially offsetting, not driving, the decline), and promotion rates rose a statistically significant but economically trivial 0.033 pp.
5. **"Latest version finds the junior decline equally pronounced in non-teleworkable positions" — NOT IN PAPER.** I read the complete 105-page May 25, 2026 version — main text (§§1–6) and all 23 supplemental-appendix sections (A.1–A.23) — and **there is no teleworkability analysis anywhere in this paper**: no mention of "telework," "remote," or "work-from-home" as a variable, split, or robustness check. This claim does not describe this paper. It most likely describes a *different*, frequently co-cited study — the paper itself repeatedly cites Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2025a), "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab), as documenting a parallel age-based employment-decline pattern in U.S. payroll data, and that is a plausible candidate for where a teleworkability split actually lives. **Do not cite this paper for the remote-work-confound claim; verify against Brynjolfsson et al. (2025a) directly before using that claim anywhere.**

**What else it actually says, beyond the secondary brief:**

The paper's own framing is narrower and more careful than "junior jobs are disappearing": it is explicitly a test of whether GenAI constitutes *seniority-biased technological change* — a labor-demand *composition* effect within adopting firms, not a claim about aggregate labor-market-wide junior employment (the authors flag the "missing intercept problem," p. 8, explicitly: cross-sectional estimates like theirs do not identify the economy-wide effect without further assumptions). The conceptual framework (§3) decomposes the effect into three forces — *displacement* (automated junior tasks lost), *productivity* (fewer workers needed per surviving task), and *reallocation* (juniors absorbing senior tasks as GenAI augments them more) — and the paper's own task-level tests (§5.1.1, Table 2–3) find support for displacement (juniors: β ≈ −4 percentage points DHS contraction per SD of task exposure, p=0.024) but find **no evidence for reallocation** (all six coefficients indistinguishable from zero, wrong sign if anything) — i.e., juniors are not expanding into former senior tasks as the model's offsetting channel would require.

**Sample representativeness — "does LinkedIn over-represent professionals?" CONFIRMED as a real and acknowledged limitation**, addressed directly in Appendix A.5: the LinkedIn/Revelio sample is tilted toward "managerial, professional, and high-information occupations and industries" relative to BLS/QCEW/OEWS/QWI benchmarks (2024 occupation-level cross-sectional correlation with OEWS is only 0.37). The authors' defense is not that the tilt is absent but that it is **stable** over 2021Q1–2025Q1 with no break at the ChatGPT launch, so it is absorbed by firm/sector fixed effects and shouldn't bias the *differential* (DiD) estimate — a narrower and more defensible claim than "the data are representative."

**Creative/design/marketing occupations or small firms — no dedicated analysis; one important caveat for GC.** Table A.5 (p. A.42) lists the top-5 high- and low-exposure occupations by sector; "Writers and Authors," "News Analysts, Reporters, and Journalists," "Editors," and "Public Relations Specialists" appear among high-exposure occupations in Information, Professional Services, and Educational Services — adjacent to but not identical with Graphic Communications destinations, and Graphic/Visual Designers do not appear by name in any top-5 list shown. There is **no occupation-specific estimate for design, marketing, or creative-services roles**, and **no firm-size heterogeneity test** of the central employment result. The one firm-size fact the paper does establish (Table 1, p. 23) cuts the other way from what §16 would want: **adopters are systematically large firms** (mean 546 employees, heavily concentrated in Information (24%) and Professional Services (28%) sectors) **versus non-adopters (mean 108 employees)** — i.e., the treated population this paper's "junior decline" result describes skews toward larger, tech-/professional-services firms, not toward the small design studios, print shops, and in-house marketing teams that are GC's typical entry-level destinations. Extending the result to small creative-services employers is an extrapolation the paper does not itself test.

**Limits (author-stated, Conclusion, p. 45):** the adoption measure captures deliberate integrator-hiring and is "conservative" — likely to miss informal/"silent" adoption (misclassifying true adopters as controls), which the authors say should *attenuate* estimates toward zero, not inflate them; the panel covers a short post-ChatGPT window (2023–2025) so longer-run adjustment (training, task redesign, career-ladder restructuring) could attenuate or amplify the initial effect; and the identification is cross-sectional/differential, not a claim about aggregate net junior employment in the economy.

**Why it earns a place for §16:** it is a genuinely adoption-based (not exposure-based) replication of the junior-hiring-decline pattern, using an LLM-validated measure of actual firm-level GenAI integration rather than an occupation-level exposure score — a real methodological advance over studies (including Eloundou et al. and the exposure-based literature this paper itself critiques, p. 9–10) that infer effects from task-content similarity rather than observed firm behavior. The mechanism finding — hiring slowdown, not separations, and no support for the reallocation channel that would let juniors absorb displaced senior work — is a clean, well-identified complement to Autor & Thompson's (2025) expertise framework already cited in §16: where Autor & Thompson supply the *mechanism* for the thinning entry rung, this paper supplies adoption-based *confirmation* that the contraction operates through the hiring margin specifically. **However, the specific claim this paper was proposed to carry — that it "also answers the remote-work confound" — is not supported; drop that framing or source it elsewhere**, and flag explicitly in §16 that the adopter population here is larger and more professional-services/information-sector-skewed than GC's typical small-firm destinations, so the magnitude (not the direction) of the effect may not transfer.

**Verdict:** Mostly consistent with the secondary brief, with one claim **NOT IN PAPER** (teleworkability) that must not be attributed here, and one important scope caveat (adopters are large, tech-/professional-services-skewed firms, not small creative shops) that bears directly on how far §16 can stretch this citation toward GC's own graduate destinations. Safe to cite for: adoption-based (LLM-classified) junior-hiring decline, concentrated in high-exposure occupations, driven by hiring not separations, with no reallocation-channel offset. Not safe to cite for: teleworkability/remote-work robustness, or for any claim about small-firm or design/marketing-occupation-specific effects.
