**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper's central move is that conventional probabilistic prediction — representing knowledge as "a single probability distribution" — collapses two fundamentally different sources of uncertainty. **Aleatoric** (statistical) uncertainty "refers to the notion of randomness, that is, the variability in the outcome of an experiment which is due to inherently random effects." The prototype is coin-flipping: the process "has a stochastic component that cannot be reduced by any additional source of information (except Laplace's demon)," so "even the best model of this process will only be able to provide probabilities for the two possible outcomes, heads and tails, but no definite answer." **Epistemic** (systematic) uncertainty "refers to uncertainty caused by a lack of knowledge (about the best model)" and, unlike aleatoric uncertainty, "can in principle be reduced on the basis of additional information." The paper's own contrast example: asked whether the Swahili word "kichwa" means head or tail, one might feel equally uncertain as in a coin flip, "yet the nature of uncertainty is different, as one could easily get rid of it" with more information. The summarizing sentence, the one directly load-bearing for background.html's claim: "epistemic uncertainty refers to the *reducible* part of the (total) uncertainty, whereas aleatoric uncertainty refers to the *irreducible* part."

The authors are explicit that this split is relative to the model class and available information, not absolute — what looks irreducible under one hypothesis space may be reducible under a richer one. They also note the distinction can be set aside when a single forced decision is all that matters (a Bayesian objection), but argue it is essential whenever a decision can be deferred, a classifier can refuse ("reject option"), or further information-gathering (active learning) is possible — precisely the situation the curriculum tool is in when it must decide whether to trust, dispute, or defer a score rather than simply emit one.

**Limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) This is a machine-learning methods survey, not an education-measurement or psychometrics paper — it supplies the vocabulary and formal distinction, not an empirical finding about curriculum-coverage scoring specifically. (2) The aleatoric/epistemic split is explicitly relative to "the hypothesis space and the information available to the learner" (the authors' own caveat) — background.html's "genuine ambiguity in the construct" framing is a faithful application of this, but it should be read as "irreducible *given the current scoring setup*," not irreducible in any absolute sense. (3) The bulk of the paper (formal machinery: Bayesian ensembles, credal sets, evidential/possibilistic representations, second-order distributions) is not what background.html draws on; only the conceptual aleatoric/epistemic distinction is used.

**Why it earns a place here, for KUD+ and this tool:** it supplies the formal justification for one of the framework's more easily misunderstood design choices — that program-level depth is reported as a band, and that two close scores are a tie rather than a ranking. Without this distinction, a reader could reasonably ask why the tool doesn't just "get a better prompt" to resolve disagreement between judge passes; this source is the answer: some of that disagreement is aleatoric (genuine construct ambiguity on abstract competencies like "problem-solving" or "critical thinking"), not epistemic (fixable model ignorance), and no amount of prompt engineering removes an irreducible component.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; HW1, substantive).** The *prior* wording ("Much of the residual disagreement... is not reducible error but genuine ambiguity... aleatoric uncertainty that no better prompt removes") overclaimed in two ways the paper itself warns against. First, the paper's own immediate qualification of the aleatoric/epistemic dichotomy was dropped: "aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty should not be seen as absolute notions. Instead, they are context-dependent in the sense of depending on the setting (X, Y, H, P). Changing the context will also change the sources of uncertainty: aleatoric may turn into epistemic uncertainty and vice versa" (p. 9; see also p. 8). Second, "much of the residual... is... aleatoric" presented as a finding about *this* curriculum-scoring residual specifically, when the paper supplies only the general vocabulary — it makes no claim about how much of any particular model's error is aleatoric versus epistemic. The sibling `measurement-hypotheses-deep-dive.html` independently found, from the closest empirical study of this exact task (Xu et al., 2026), that LLM scoring error traces substantially to **reducible**, model-side causes (over-interpretation of instructional keywords; difficulty parsing long unstructured text) rather than to genuine construct ambiguity — the opposite emphasis from what the prior wording implied.

`background.html` §7 now reads "Some of the residual disagreement... is likely genuine ambiguity... How much of the residual is of that kind is an open question," restores the authors' own context-dependence caveat in the same sentence, and cross-references the measurement-hypotheses companion's finding that much of the error is in fact reducible.

**Verdict:** Consistent with the paper's core aleatoric/epistemic vocabulary and the "irreducible part of the uncertainty" quote (p. 8), which this card already states correctly. Not consistent with the *prior* framing that presented "much of the residual is aleatoric" as a settled, specific finding about this tool's scoring disagreement, and that dropped the authors' own context-dependence caveat — both corrected in the live doc as of 2026-10-07.
