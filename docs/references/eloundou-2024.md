**What the background doc claims it says:** The only sentence citing Eloundou et al. is in §16, in the Brynjolfsson paragraph's structural caveat: "while the employment outcomes are administrative payroll records, both of the study's primary AI-exposure measures are themselves LLM-derived — GPT-4 task-level exposure estimates (Eloundou et al., 2024, human-validated) and Claude conversation shares from the Anthropic Economic Index. Which occupations count as 'AI-exposed' therefore rests on model judgment, the same correlated-error concern §7 raises about LLM ensembles."

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper builds a rubric-based "exposure" measure over O*NET's 19,265 tasks and 2,087 Detailed Work Activities (DWAs) across 1,016 occupations. Exposure is explicitly non-causal and non-displacement: "we define exposure as a proxy for potential economic impact without distinguishing between labor-augmenting or labor-displacing effects" (p. 2). Operationally, a task is E1 ("direct exposure") if "using the described LLM via ChatGPT or the OpenAI playground can decrease the time required to complete the DWA or task by at least half (50%)" (p. 7); E2 ("LLM+") if complementary software could do so; E0 otherwise. Three summary measures are built from these labels: α (= E1, lower bound), β (= E1 + 0.5·E2, the paper's default), ζ (= E1 + E2, upper bound).

**Both humans and GPT-4 rated the same rubric.** "We obtained human annotations by applying the rubric to each O*NET Detailed Worker Activity (DWA)... The authors personally labeled a large sample of tasks and DWAs and enlisted experienced human annotators" (p. 8). Separately, "we administered a similar rubric to an early version of GPT-4... on all task/occupation pairs" (p. 8), with the rubric's wording "slightly" modified from the human version "to enhance agreement with a set of human labels." Critically, the paper itself states which is primary: **"In this analysis, we present results from human annotators as our primary results"** (p. 9, §3.4.2) — not the GPT-4 ratings.

**Agreement between GPT-4 and human ratings (Table 2, p. 8, "GPT-4, Rubric 1; Human"):**

| Measure | Weighting | Agreement | Pearson's r |
|---|---|---|---|
| α | E1 | 80.8% | 0.223 |
| β | E1 + .5·E2 | 65.6% | 0.591 |
| ζ | E1 + E2 | 82.1% | 0.654 |

The headline α agreement (80.8%) comes with a weak Pearson correlation (0.223) — that "agreement" rate is mostly agreement on the common non-exposed label, not correlated judgment. β, the paper's default measure, correlates more substantially (r = 0.591). The authors describe the occupation-level pattern as "a high degree of agreement" (p. 9) and note that "near the highest levels of exposure... GPT-4 ratings tend to be lower than Human ratings" (Fig. 2 caption, p. 10).

**Occupation-level summary statistics (Table 3, p. 12):** Human α 0.14 / β 0.30 / ζ 0.46; GPT-4 α 0.14 / β 0.34 / ζ 0.55. By worker share, the headline the abstract reports: "around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of LLMs, while approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted" (p. 1), based on β values (p. 11). The task-level headline: "about 15% of all worker tasks in the US could be completed significantly faster at the same level of quality. When incorporating software and tooling built on top of LLMs, this share increases to between 47 and 56% of all tasks" (p. 1).

**The authors' own limitation sections are extensive** (§3.4, pp. 9–10): annotators are "not occupationally diverse" and "mostly unaware of the specific occupations mapped to each DWA"; GPT-4 outcomes are "sensitive to alterations in the rubric's wording, the prompt's order and composition"; "none [annotation source] should be considered the definitive ground truth relative to the others"; the validity of the whole task-based framework is flagged as an open question ("if indeed, the task-based breakdown is not a valid representation of how most work in an occupation is performed, our exposure analysis would largely be invalidated," p. 9).

**Limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) The paper's own stated primary results are the **human** ratings, not GPT-4's (p. 9) — so calling the measure "GPT-4 task-level exposure estimates" without qualification inverts the paper's own emphasis, even though β-level agreement between the two is substantial (r = 0.591) and downstream users (Frank et al. 2026, Brynjolfsson et al.) do in practice take the GPT-4 column. (2) Agreement is strongest on β/ζ, not on α, where raw agreement (80.8%) masks weak correlation (r = 0.223) — "human-validated" should not be read as "tightly validated" across every exposure measure. (3) Exposure measures technical time-saving feasibility, explicitly not automation or job loss ("technical feasibility does not guarantee labor productivity or automation outcomes," elsewhere in the paper). (4) 2023-era GPT-4/GPT-3.5-era ChatGPT; US-only; excludes the self-employed. (5) The 50% threshold is, by the authors' own account, "somewhat arbitrary."

**Why it earns a place here, for KUD+ and this tool:** this is the measurement substrate background.html's §16 and §7 both lean on when warning that "AI-exposed" occupational classifications are themselves model-derived judgments, not independently observed automation events — precisely the correlated-error concern §7 raises about LLM ensembles (Kim et al. 2025; Kohli 2026). Eloundou et al. is the common ancestor: its exposure labels (or close derivatives of them) underlie Brynjolfsson et al., Frank et al., and Orr, Tucker & Warren, all cited elsewhere in §16's "wider evidence" paragraph — so a reader needs to know, once, that these are not independent confirmations of the same labor-market pattern but multiple studies built on a shared, LLM-derived exposure scaffold.

**Verdict:** Consistent, with one precision gap. Background.html's characterization — "GPT-4 task-level exposure estimates... human-validated" — is defensible as shorthand (GPT-4 ratings exist, and they were checked against human ratings with moderate-to-strong correlation on the paper's default β measure) but is imprecise in one respect: the paper's own primary/headline results are the **human** annotations, with GPT-4 as the secondary, human-validated measure, not the reverse. Given that most downstream papers (including those §16 cites as "wider evidence") in practice use the GPT-4-derived scores, the current wording is close enough not to mislead, but a careful editor should distinguish "the rubric has been validated against human judgment" from "the operative exposure numbers in the literature come from GPT-4, checked against humans" — the second is more precisely what downstream citations (Frank et al. 2026) actually use. No numeric claim in background.html's single sentence about this source is wrong; the issue is emphasis, not fact. Separately: Eloundou et al. has no `#ref-` anchor or §17 entry despite being named in the running text — adding one is a copy-editing decision for the doc owner, not something this card performs (per task scope).
