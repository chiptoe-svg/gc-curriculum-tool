**What it actually says (from the PDF):**
- Design: cross-sectional; "Retention of Spanish learned in school was tested over a 50-year period for 733 individuals" (p. 1). Of these, 587 had taken Spanish in high school or college (p. 2); the rest were controls and current students.
- Shape: "memory curves which decline exponentially for the first 3-6 years of the retention interval. After that retention remains unchanged for periods of up to 30 years before showing a final decline" (p. 1).
- Rehearsal: "The great majority of subjects rehearse so little that the data reveal no significant rehearsal effects" (p. 1).
- What decides permastore: "a function of the level of original training, the grades received in Spanish courses, and the method of testing (recall vs. recognition)" (p. 1). "This suggests a discrete transition into a permastore state during the extended period of original training" (p. 1).
- Figures (Spanish–English recall vocabulary, p. 23): "Individuals with a mean grade of A retain approximately 52%, 72%, and 80% … for Training Levels 1, 3, and 5 respectively; individuals with a mean grade of C retain only 0%, 53%, and 73%."
- Mechanism (p. 25): "extension of the reexposure to information over long time periods produces a cumulative effect which eventually gives permanence to responses"; the transition "occurs during acquisition and is discrete." The claim that "without repeated exposure only a small portion of the information acquired during the first exposure would endure" is cited to Bahrick (1979), not shown in this study.

**Verdict (audited 2026-10-07 against the held PDF):** The pages' core use holds: a decades-stable plateau whose share depends on how much training there was, entered through a discrete transition. Corrected on all three pages: "shallow learning decays toward zero" overgeneralized (0% applies to one-level, C-grade learners' recall vocabulary; A students at the same level kept 52%), and the † "not held" markers were removed. Not supported by this paper: "single-exposure learning decays toward zero" and any role for context variation (problem-solving had already dropped both). For the twin card: retention-interval rehearsal had no significant effect (so "rehearsed enough" is wrong), and the single-exposure point is Bahrick (1979), not this study.
