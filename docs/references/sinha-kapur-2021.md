**What the background doc claims it says:** The 2021 meta-analytic review found an overall effect favoring problem-solving followed by instruction for conceptual knowledge and transfer (g ≈ 0.36 across 53 studies, 166 comparisons), with no significant advantage for procedural knowledge. The evidence is strongest when the problem-solving and instruction phases are carefully designed around the same target concept.

**What it actually says (synthesis):** This systematic review and meta-analysis examined 53 studies and 166 comparisons that compared problem-solving followed by instruction (PS-I) designs with instruction followed by problem-solving (I-PS) designs across STEM and related disciplines. The central research question: does the sequence matter — specifically, does solving problems before receiving instruction produce better learning than the conventional order (instruction first, then problems)?

Key quantitative findings: The overall meta-analytic effect favored PS-I over I-PS with Hedges' g = 0.36 (95% CI: 0.20–0.51), a significant moderate effect. When restricted to studies implementing PS-I with high fidelity to the principles of Productive Failure specifically (not just any problem-first sequence), effect sizes were stronger (Hedges' g ranging from 0.37 to 0.58). This distinction matters: not every PS-I design produces equivalent effects; the design must embody the specific PF principles (generating multiple solutions, activation of prior knowledge, consolidation phase using student failures).

Boundary conditions identified: The PS-I advantage was stronger for older students (middle school and above; younger students in grades 2–5 showed the reverse pattern favoring I-PS), for conceptual knowledge and transfer outcomes (but NOT for procedural knowledge — no significant advantage), and when studies used well-designed problems and genuine consolidation phases.

The "no significant advantage for procedural knowledge" finding is important and is correctly cited in the background doc. The productive failure effect is specifically a conceptual and transfer effect, not a route to faster procedural acquisition.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; SK10, SK8, S1/SK5, substantive/minor).** Three overstatements in the prior framing, confirmed against the held PDF and corrected in both `background.html` and `problem-solving-deep-dive.html`:

1. **Domain-general skills did not just show "no advantage" — they showed a significant *negative* effect for PS-I (SK10).** Table 5 (p. 779) reports "Domain-general skills (−0.17 [−1.11, −0.02])" — the only subgroup in the entire moderator analysis with a confidence interval that excludes zero on the *negative* side, i.e. favoring instruction-first (I-PS) over PS-I. The review's own gloss: "except for domain general-skills, most STEM and non-STEM learning domains had moderate effect sizes in favor of PS-I" (p. 787). The domain-general subgroup is small (n = 8 comparisons, Table 1: control-of-variables strategy, water jug problems, Rubik's cube) and the authors offer a plausible mechanism (Chase & Klahr, 2017: goal-ambiguity in domain-general problem solving undercuts implicit feedback during the PS phase, p. 787) rather than treating it as disqualifying — but the finding is a direct empirical data point *against*, not neutral on, applying PS-I to domain-general targets. The framework's extension of productive failure to domain-general dispositions (e.g., Agency, Curiosity) remains an untested hypothesis of this framework's own, and the nearest direct evidence in this review points the other way.
2. **"Strong" / fit framing overstates the evidence base (SK8).** "Nearly 75% of all included comparisons (see Table 1) targeted learning concepts of math and physics" (p. 787–788), and the authors caution: "more studies are also needed in other STEM domains as well as in non-STEM domains (e.g., psychology, arts, history) before we can generalize the effectiveness of PS-I, even with high PF fidelity" (p. 788). A label like "Strong — conceptual knowledge & transfer" or "substantially closer to the evidence base" for a non-math/physics technical domain overstates what the review itself claims generalizes. "Closest (evidence mostly from math and physics)" is the more faithful framing.
3. **The driver of larger effects is PF design fidelity, not "the problem-solving and instruction phases... designed around the same target concept" (S1/SK5).** The sentence above was a loose paraphrase — every PS-I design shares a target concept across phases by definition, so that framing doesn't distinguish anything. The paper's actual, tested driver is **design fidelity to Kapur's productive-failure criteria**, and specifically: "instruction building on student solutions, group work as the participation structure in the problem-solving phase, evidence for multiple RSM generation in the article, and dialogue-dominant social surround facilitation in the instruction phase" as "the top four most important predictors" from stepwise regression (p. 781, Table 6). "Instruction building on student solutions" is the predictor most directly tied to Kapur's own consolidation-phase design principle.

**Verdict:** Consistent on the headline numbers: g ≈ 0.36 across 53 studies, 166 comparisons (95% CI 0.20–0.51), and the "no significant advantage for procedural knowledge" finding. Not consistent, prior to this pass, on (a) domain-general skills showing merely "no advantage" rather than a significant negative effect (g = −0.17), and (b) the claim that effects are strongest when phases share a target concept, which the paper does not test — the paper's own tested driver is PF design fidelity, especially instruction building on student solutions. Both corrected above.
