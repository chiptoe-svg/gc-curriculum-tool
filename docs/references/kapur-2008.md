**What the background doc claims it says:** The original productive-failure paradigm. Students who struggle with ill-structured problems before instruction outperform direct-instruction controls on transfer.

**What it actually says (synthesis):** The 2008 paper in *Cognition and Instruction* provides the original "existence proof" for productive failure as a learning design paradigm. The study involved eleventh-grade science students (in Singapore) in a computer-supported collaborative learning environment working on Newtonian kinematics problems.

Two conditions, both collaborative problem-solving — **neither condition receives direct/explicit instruction**: in the productive-failure condition (PF/IS), students first worked collaboratively in small groups on ill-structured, complex problems — problems without a clearly specified solution method, requiring students to use their existing knowledge in new ways. In the comparison condition (WS), students first solved well-structured problems in small groups. Both groups then moved to individual well-structured problems, i.e. the design (Table 1, p. 386/PDF p. 8) is pretest → group work (IS or WS) → individual post-tests, with no instructional phase between the group work and the post-tests. The two conditions differ only in problem *structuredness*, not in whether instruction occurred.

Key findings: Groups in the PF condition struggled — they produced poor-quality solutions to the ill-structured problems. Yet despite this failure, these students significantly outperformed the DI-first students on both near and far transfer measures taken individually afterward. The students who struggled productively in their initial group phase outperformed students who had been scaffolded toward successful solutions.

Kapur's explanation involves what he calls "activation and awareness of prior knowledge" — students in the PF condition, by attempting the problems with their existing schemas, activated and made explicit the limits and gaps in their knowledge. This activation created an "affordance for learning" in the subsequent instruction phase, which students in the DI-first condition lacked (they were scaffolded past the struggle without activating these affordances). The failure modes themselves became informative scaffolding for the canonical approach.

The paper identifies the two-phase generate-then-consolidate structure as the design unit: students first generate multiple (often wrong) solutions, then an expert consolidation phase uses those attempts as scaffolding. This is not "discovery learning" (which lacks the consolidation phase); it is a specific two-phase design.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; K82, substantive).** The original framing here and in `background.html`'s reference-list annotation — "students who struggle with ill-structured problems **before instruction** outperform **direct-instruction controls** on transfer" — overstates the design on two points: (1) there is no direct-instruction control condition anywhere in this paper; both conditions are collaborative problem-solving, differing only in problem structuredness; (2) there is no instructional phase at all between the group-work struggle and the individual post-tests — "before instruction" implies a later teaching phase that the design does not have. The paper's own description of its two conditions is the one quoted above (Table 1). `background.html`'s reference-list entry for this source should be checked against this correction if it still uses "direct-instruction control" language.

**Verdict:** Consistent on the core empirical result (the PF/ill-structured condition outperforms the WS/well-structured condition on individual post-test transfer measures) and on the two-phase generate-then-consolidate framing drawn from the companion 2016 paper. Not consistent on "direct-instruction controls" or "before instruction," which the 2008 design does not contain — corrected above.
