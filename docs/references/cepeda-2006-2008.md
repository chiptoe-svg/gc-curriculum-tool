**What the doc claims it says:** §2: "across the studies Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesized, spaced presentation averaged 47.3% recall against 36.7% for massed (Table 1), and 'there is no hint that massed presentation was preferable to spaced, whether retention interval was very short (less than 1 min) or very long (over 30 days)' (p. 359). **What it actually says (synthesis):**

**2006 meta-analysis.** Synthesizes 839 assessments from 317 experiments across 184 articles on distributed-practice effects in verbal recall. It separates the spacing effect (spaced beats massed) from the lag effect (more-spaced beats less-spaced), and its central methodological contribution is that the optimal inter-study interval (ISI) and the retention interval (RI) must be considered jointly — there is no single best gap; the best gap depends on how long retention needs to last. Table 1 reports spaced recall averaging 47.3% against 36.7% for massed. On the direction of the effect across the whole range tested, the paper is unambiguous: "there is no hint that massed presentation was preferable to spaced, whether retention interval was very short (less than 1 min) or very long (over 30 days)" (p. 359) — i.e., this paper does **not** support any claim that massed practice produces better short-term/in-the-moment performance than spaced practice; it finds the opposite holds across the full range it tested, including very short retention intervals. (A short-term massed-practice advantage, where it exists, is a separate finding from **Bjork (1994)**, not from either Cepeda paper — see the correction note below.) Neither Cepeda paper discusses "variety" of practice as a factor; that claim, where it appears, should be sourced to Bjork (1994) instead.

**2008 study.** A large web-based experiment (1,354 subjects) learning 32 trivia facts, with a second study session after a gap of up to 3.5 months and a final test after a retention interval of up to one year (26 gap-by-RI conditions). Key result: at every retention interval, increasing the gap first increases then gradually reduces final-test performance (an inverted-U), and the optimal *absolute* gap grows as the retention interval grows. The load-bearing nuance for proportional framing: expressed as a **share of the retention interval**, the optimal gap *declines* as the interval lengthens — the abstract's own figures (p. 1095) give "the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay." So "grew in absolute size... but shrank as a share of that delay" is the paper's own framing, not a reviewer's inference.

**Correction note (citation audit, 2026-10-07; CE1/AD1/RK1).** An earlier draft of `measurement-hypotheses-deep-dive.html` attributed a compound claim — "spaced, varied practice beats massed... even when massed produces higher in-the-moment performance... variety matters, not just count" — jointly to Cepeda (2006), Adesope et al. (2017), and Roediger & Karpicke (2006). That attribution did not survive a full-text check: Cepeda 2006 (p. 359, quoted above) says the *opposite* of the in-the-moment-massed-advantage claim; Adesope et al. (2017) is a practice-*testing* meta-analysis that never mentions massed practice (it supports only the retrieval-practice-beats-restudying claim, g = 0.51 vs. restudying, g = 0.93 vs. no activity, Table 1 p. 672); and Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found extra studying helps more on a 5-minute test but testing helps more on delayed tests (pp. 253–254) — a result about testing vs. restudying, not about "variety." The only held source that supports **both** the short-term/long-term reversal and the role of variety is **Bjork (1994)**: "massing practice... produces better short-term performance or recall... but markedly inferior long-term performance or recall" (p. 190), and "introducing variation and/or unpredictability in the training environment causes difficulty for the learner but enhances long-term performance" (p. 189). `measurement-hypotheses-deep-dive.html` §2 now splits this into three separately-sourced claims: spacing beats massing (Cepeda 2006/2008, as above), retrieval practice beats restudying (Adesope; Roediger & Karpicke), and the reversal-plus-variety claim (Bjork 1994 only).

**Verdict:** Consistent with the specific figures this card states (Table 1's 47.3%/36.7%; the p. 359 quote; the 2008 abstract's 20–40%→5–10% proportional-gap figures). The compound "massed is better in-the-moment, but variety matters" claim that a prior draft attached to these two papers is not supported by either and has been corrected (reattributed to Bjork 1994, or to Adesope/Roediger & Karpicke for the narrower testing-effect claim) in the live doc.
