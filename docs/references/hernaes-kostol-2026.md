**What the doc would claim (per the task brief):** Employment in the most AI-exposed occupations grew 0.1% since October 2022 vs 0.3% in least-exposed (insignificant gap); young software developers fell ~18%; young workers in the most-exposed quintile did not fall behind the least-exposed, including within firms; for 22–25-year-olds a gap opened in spring 2025; parallel pre-trends rejected in every age group, so neither the null nor the late gap is well identified.

**What it actually says (verbatim, with page refs):**

- **Headline 0.1%/0.3% gap — CONFIRMED.** Abstract (p. 1): "Since October 2022, the month before ChatGPT's release, employment in the most exposed occupations has grown by 0.1 percent, against 0.3 percent in the least exposed occupations." Conclusion (p. 31) adds the significance call directly: "a gap that is small and not statistically significant." The formal test (Table 8, p. 29) reports the Q5−Q1 double difference as −0.21 percentage points with SE 2.38 — far from significant.

- **Young software developers fell ~18% — CONFIRMED, with the exact figure being a *per-capita* number from the cross-country section, not the headline design.** P. 22: "The Norwegian per-capita decline of about 18 percent for young software developers is between the US (−20%, Brynjolfsson et al., 2025) and the Swedish raw figure (−44%, Lodefalk et al., 2026)." This is a single-occupation case-study figure (software developers, STYRK 2512–2514/2519, ages 21–30), not the full-distribution quintile result — the two should not be conflated when cited.

- **Young workers in the most-exposed quintile did not fall behind, including within firms — CONFIRMED.** Cell-level: "the Q5 series tracks Q1 after the ChatGPT release" and "the broader ranking does not reproduce the decline seen in the selected occupations" (p. 12); Table 4 (p. 20) Q5×Post for ages 21–30 = +0.0213 (SE 0.0220, n.s.). Within-firm: Table 6 (p. 26) Q5×Post for ages 21–30 = +0.0126 (SE 0.0210, n.s.); Figure 7/text (p. 23): "the Q5 path is noisy and ends slightly positive, so the within-firm comparison, like the cell-level one, does not show the most-exposed quintile among the young falling behind." Conclusion (p. 31): "Grouping all occupations into exposure quintiles, the most exposed quintile did not fall behind the least exposed for workers aged 21 to 30."

- **22–25-year-old gap opening spring 2025 — CONFIRMED, and this is a *narrow replication*, not the paper's primary 21–30 result.** P. 24–25, replicating the Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) age-band design: "the most- and least-exposed quintiles track each other through early 2025, and from spring 2025 the most-exposed quintile falls below, with the gap widening to about 35 log points by February 2026. The recent gap exceeds the 15 log points Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) report for the United States." The pooled post-period average for this 22–25 group is "small, about 3 log points" and it is only the *late* sub-period that shows separation.

- **Parallel pre-trends rejected in every age group — CONFIRMED verbatim, but the "neither result is well identified" framing in the brief OVERSTATES what the paper concludes for the main 21–30 null — CORRECTED.** P. 20–21: "joint tests reject parallel pre-trends in every age group in both the cell and firm-FE specifications." However, the paper does not treat this as leaving the 21–30 null unidentified: it runs a Rambachan & Roth (2023) HonestDiD sensitivity analysis specifically because of the rejected pre-trends (Table 5, p. 22), and finds the breakdown value M̄ = 0.00 — meaning even the *conventional, non-robust* 95% CI already includes zero, so "the 21–30 exposure gradient is not a fragile negative estimate; it is essentially absent under the paper's preferred design... a relatively precise null... rather than a negative effect whose robustness is in question" (p. 22). By contrast, the paper flags the 22–25 *late* divergence as the one genuinely compromised by the pre-trend problem: "the narrow 22–25 replication diverges only late and **on rejected pre-trends**" (Section 3.8, p. 22). So the correct reading is: pre-trends are rejected everywhere, but the authors explicitly defend the main 21–30 null as robust to this, while explicitly caveating the 22–25 late-spring-2025 gap as sitting on the same rejected pre-trends. The brief's claim should be split into these two different epistemic statuses, not merged.

**Creative/design/media occupations — NOT IN PAPER.** The paper's case studies are software developers, customer service agents, electricians, and home health aides (Figure 2); Table 2's five largest occupations per quintile (health care assistants, cleaners, child care workers, shop assistants, teachers, managers, policy administrators, office clerks, sales reps, systems analysts, software developers, etc.) contain no design, creative, arts, or media occupation. There is no exposure-quintile or case-study result for this occupational family — any §16 use must not imply one exists here.

**Exposure measure:** Primary measure is Eloundou et al. (2024) GPT-4 exposure score, β = E₁ + 0.5×E₂ — a theoretical/expert-plus-GPT-4 task-exposure score computed from US SOC codes in early 2023, crosswalked to Norwegian STYRK-08 occupation codes via BLS SOC→ISCO tables (p. 7–9; 38.8% of crosswalk rows are partial many-to-one/one-to-many matches). Secondary measures: Handa et al. (2025) Anthropic Economic Index (revealed Claude.ai usage, split into automation vs. augmentation shares) and, in Table 3 only, Felten et al. (2021) AIOE and a 2026 Anthropic job-exposure index (not used in the main results). **All exposure scores are US-occupation-derived and US-LLM-usage-derived**, mapped onto Norwegian occupation codes by a filtered code match the authors themselves caveat is "not a claim that the two classifications are exactly the same" (p. 9, fn. 4).

**Limits, including transfer to US graduates:**
- Not peer-reviewed (discussion paper; disclaimer explicit).
- **Norway's labour market is a poor proxy for the US entry-level market the background doc's §16 argument is about.** Centralized sector-level wage bargaining compresses wage dispersion and strong employment protection channels any AI-related adjustment toward the quantity (headcount) margin rather than wages (p. 15, p. 23–24: "A limited wage response is consistent with Norway's two-tier bargaining system... channelling any AI-related adjustment toward the quantity margin"). A US labor market with weaker employment protection and more wage flexibility could show the opposite split — more wage effect, less headcount effect — making a Norwegian "no employment gap" finding not informative about whether a US employment gap exists.
- **The exposure scores themselves are imported wholesale from US task/occupation structures** (Eloundou GPT-4 score and Handa Claude-usage score), then force-mapped onto Norwegian STYRK codes through an imperfect BLS crosswalk with ~39% partial-match rows. Occupational task content, job title boundaries, and AI adoption patterns may differ enough between the US and Norway that the exposure ranking itself is noisy for Norway, independent of the labour-market institutional difference.
- Confounders the authors themselves flag and cannot rule out: post-COVID digital-demand normalization and 2022–2024 Norges Bank monetary tightening (policy rate 0%→4.5%, p. 7) could both move the same AI-exposed occupations for non-AI reasons; supply-side reallocation (young workers avoiding occupations they perceive as AI-exposed) would produce the same observed pattern through worker choice rather than employer substitution (p. 21).
- Microdata.no cell data suppress cells with <5 observations, coded as zero in the balanced panel (p. 3) — a small-cell approximation whose main robustness check is against individual-level data, not an independent data source.
- The paper is explicitly a tracking instrument ("the answer will keep changing," p. 31) rather than a definitive test — its own framing discourages treating any single vintage's null as final.

**Why it earns a place:** This is the largest-scale, most current (through February 2026), population-wide test available of whether AI has widened early-career employment gaps outside the US, using the universe of Norwegian private-sector payroll records rather than a sample. It is the strongest available *null* counterweight to Brynjolfsson et al. (2025)'s US payroll finding for §16's "entry rung" argument — but it is a null with identification caveats that must travel with it: non-parallel pre-trends everywhere (defended via HonestDiD for the main 21–30 result, not defended for the 22–25 late-2025 divergence), and an occupation-exposure measure and labour-market institutional setting both substantially different from the US context the vision doc is actually about. It also supplies a useful second case-study data point (software developers, −18% per-capita for young workers) that is directionally consistent with Brynjolfsson et al. rather than contradicting it, which complicates any simple "Norway says no effect" framing — the contradiction is specifically at the full-distribution/quintile level, not at the single-occupation level.

**Verdict:** Use with care. CONFIRMED on all five secondary claims as stated facts, but two require sharper framing before §16 can use them: (1) the ~18% software-developer decline is a single-occupation case study that *agrees* with Brynjolfsson et al., so citing it in the same breath as the "null" risks implying the paper found a contradiction where it found a split between the aggregate and the case study; (2) "pre-trends rejected everywhere" must not be flattened into "neither finding is well identified" — the authors explicitly argue the 21–30 null *survives* a formal sensitivity bound that the 22–25 late-2025 gap does not get the same defense for. The §16 sentence should present this as "the best available population-wide null, in a labor market whose institutions and whose (US-derived) exposure measure both limit how far the finding transfers, and whose own authors flag the null as more secure than the later, narrower, pre-trend-compromised 22–25 divergence."
