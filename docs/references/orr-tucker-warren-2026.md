**What the doc would claim it says:** Proposed for a §16 entry-rung paragraph: the closest published analogue to our own graduate-outcome study of ~1,000 Clemson GC graduates' first jobs — a large-sample, administrative-data study of how new bachelor's graduates' early employment and earnings outcomes vary by college major's AI exposure, usable to calibrate expectations for GC-adjacent majors (design, communications, marketing) against the computer-science-dominated top decile the headline numbers describe.

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

**Sample and design.** 6,665,500 bachelor's graduate records (2016–2024 graduation cohorts, ~29% of all U.S. bachelor's degrees in that window) from the Census Bureau's Post-Secondary Employment Outcomes (PSEO) data — 27 data partners, 24 states, 350+ institutions, skewed toward large public doctoral-granting universities — linked to Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) wage and employment records. This is a **difference-in-differences / event-study design** (Eq. 1, p. 14), not merely descriptive: institution×major, institution×quarter, and major×quarter-of-year fixed effects, with the four 2022 quarters as the omitted reference period and effects estimated by exposure decile relative to the pooled bottom six deciles. The authors are explicit that this recovers an *average treatment effect on the treated* under a parallel-trends assumption anchored to 2022, not necessarily the full causal effect of AI (the bottom six deciles may themselves be affected post-2022) — and they devote pp. 15–17 to stating and probing the identifying assumptions (parallel trends, no anticipation, SUTVA/general-equilibrium spillovers).

**How major-level exposure is built — confirms the LLM-derived-exposure risk the project flags.** Exposure is constructed in two steps (§2.2–2.3, pp. 7–10): (1) occupation-level AI exposure scores from **Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2024)**, the "GPT-4 Beta" measure — built by *asking GPT-4 itself* to judge, for each O*NET detailed work activity, whether GPT-4 "could reduce the time it takes to complete the task by at least half," with core tasks weighted double; this is the paper's **primary/baseline** measure and is unambiguously LLM-derived (self-assessed by the model being studied). (2) These occupation scores are mapped to college majors using the graduate-weighted mean of occupations actually held by 21–29-year-old bachelor's degree holders in that major in the 2018–2022 ACS. The authors **do** test robustness against two non-GPT-4-self-report alternatives: Eisfeldt, Schubert & Zhang (2023)'s "Core" task-exposure index (Spearman ρ=0.93 with GPT-4 Beta at the major level) and Handa et al. (2025)'s "Usage" measure, built from an automatic classifier applied to actual Claude.ai conversation transcripts — a real-usage, not task-capability, measure. Appendix B (pp. 64–67) reports **general agreement through the 80th exposure percentile across all three measures**, but a divergence at the top: Eloundou/Eisfeldt (both capability-based) rank computer-science-adjacent majors as most-harmed, while Handa's usage-based measure reorders business majors (Accounting, Finance) above some computer majors and shows a *rebound* rather than a cliff at the 90th percentile — the authors attribute this to the capability-based measures ranking Accounting/Finance as less exposed than CS, which the usage data contradicts. **This is a genuine, stated limitation of generalizing from the single headline measure**, not fully resolved by the robustness check.

**The five secondary claims, checked against the paper:**

1. **"Most-exposed tenth of majors (mostly computer science) were 5 percentage points less likely to be employed right after graduation."** — **CONFIRMED.** Abstract and p. 3: "the most AI-exposed decile of college majors saw their likelihood of initial employment decline by five percentage points" in the quarter after graduation, in regression-adjusted estimates relative to the pooled bottom six deciles. P. 18 gives the precise decile-10 figure: an average −5 percentage points from 2024 onward (deciles 7/8 average −2pp, decile 9 ambiguous — "might reflect reversion to long-run trends rather than a negative effect of AI"). Table A3 and Figure 10 (p. 35) confirm decile 10 is "predominantly composed of computer science and adjacent majors" (Computer Science, Computer Engineering, Computer Programming, Info. Sciences, Computer and Info. Systems-General, Mgmt. Info. Systems, Computer Admin. Mgmt. and Security — plus, notably, **Journalism**, discussed below).

2. **"Initial earnings fell ~13%, similar to graduating into a recession."** — **CONFIRMED, with scope caveat.** Abstract: "full-quarter initial earnings declined by thirteen percent" for the top decile relative to the least-exposed deciles' 2022 change (two quarters post-graduation), "comparable in magnitude to the earnings losses associated with graduating into a large recession" — the paper cites the recession literature's 9–10% initial annual losses (Altonji et al. 2016; Oreopoulos et al. 2012) and treats 13% (full-quarter, not annual) as consistent in magnitude (p. 45, footnote 31 explains the measures aren't directly comparable but the authors judge them consistent). This effect **attenuates over time**: to ~8.1 log points at one year and ~3.3 log points at two years post-graduation relative to long-run pre-pandemic trend (p. 28) — so "13%" is specifically the *initial* (2-quarter) shock, not a persistent level.

3. **"Half the earnings loss came from starting in lower-paying sectors (retail, restaurants)."** — **CONFIRMED, close to verbatim.** P. 23/25: "since 2022 approximately half of the overall earnings decline for the top decile of AI-exposed new graduates is explained by their movement into lower-paying sectors" (the "across-industry composition effect" in Figure 6's decomposition). Figure 5 (p. 24) shows the top-exposure decile shifted >2pp more into Retail Trade and Accommodation & Food Services while shrinking share in Information (−3pp) and Professional/Scientific/Technical Services (−6pp) — the two highest-paying, most AI-exposed sectors per the paper's own citation of Tucker (2026). Exact numbers: without the sectoral shift, 2024 earnings for the top decile would still be down ~8% from 2019 levels; with it, they are down 15%, "placing real earnings at levels last seen before 2016" (p. 25).

4. **"Controlling for remote work barely changed results."** — **CONFIRMED.** §7.1 (pp. 40–41, 68–74): work-from-home at the major level correlates 0.88 with AI exposure (higher than Lambert & Schindler's 0.77 at the occupation level), a genuine collinearity concern the authors flag directly rather than minimize. Adding a time-varying WFH control leaves post-period coefficients "nearly identical to those of our baseline model" for employment and preserves the long-run-relative decline for earnings (p. 41). A second specification replicating Lambert & Schindler (2026)'s design — which found *that* paper's AI effects load almost entirely onto remote work — explicitly **fails to reproduce that pattern** here: "we do not replicate the patterns of Lambert and Schindler" (p. 74), and Appendix Figure A13 shows WFH rates for the most-exposed decile actually *declining* through 2022–2024, the opposite direction needed to explain worsening outcomes via a remote-work channel.

5. **"Rising self-employment and graduate-school enrollment could explain half the employment drop."** — **CONFIRMED, near-verbatim.** §7.3 (p. 42): "changes in self-employment and graduate school enrollment may explain up to half of the five percentage point decline in initial employment of the most exposed graduates that we observe" — self-employment among top-decile 21–24-year-olds rose from 1.01% to 1.49% (2022→2024) vs. +0.06pp for the bottom six deciles; grad-school enrollment rose 1.66pp for the top decile vs. −0.50pp for the bottom six. The paper explicitly does **not** read this as good news: self-employed top-decile graduates earn "less than a third" of their traditionally-employed peers' earnings (p. 43), consistent with self-employment being a weak-demand response rather than an opportunity pull.

**What this shows for GC — and what it does not.** Commercial Art and Graphic Design, the closest proxy to GC's design side, sits in exposure decile 6 — above the median, but inside the paper's less-exposed comparison group (deciles 1–6) yet shows one of the **largest earnings declines in the whole creative/communications cluster** (−0.070 log points relative to the average major, 2022→2024), with a borderline-negative employment coefficient. The surrounding cluster GC alumni also enter — Communications, Mass Media, Advertising & PR, Marketing, Journalism — shows consistent negative earnings coefficients of −0.04 to −0.07, and Journalism sits in the top exposure decile alongside computer science. Two readings follow. First, **the GPT-4 exposure index does not predict where the earnings pressure on design graduates landed**: graphic design is "low exposure" by the index and among the hardest hit on earnings, so for GC the index is a weak guide and observed outcomes are the better evidence. Second, these are per-major descriptive contrasts, not causal AI effects — the paper's identified estimates are for exposure deciles, and the per-major rows cannot separate AI from anything else that moved design and media pay in 2022–2024 (the post-2022 tech and advertising downturn is the obvious candidate). The production-technology proxy (2503) moving the *other* way (+0.026 earnings) is suggestive for GC's print/production side but is too indirect a proxy to carry weight.

**Limits.**
- **Not peer-reviewed**, Census working paper, explicit non-endorsement disclaimer on the cover page.
- **Institution sample skews public, large, urban, doctoral-research** (p. 7, Table A2) — the authors state results "may only be representative of this subset of institutions." No indication whether Clemson or similar mid-size land-grant universities are in the 24-state PSEO partner list (Table A1 does not include South Carolina).
- **Short post-treatment window.** ChatGPT-3.5 released November 2022; the latest LEHD data run through Q3 2025, so the longest observable horizon is ~2 years post-graduation for the earliest post-ChatGPT cohorts, and the "2 years post-grad" cells for the most recent cohorts are necessarily thin. The authors repeatedly flag that long-run persistence is unknown.
- **Exposure is a fixed pre-period (2018–2022 ACS) assignment, not current.** AI capability has evolved substantially since GPT-4; the authors explicitly caveat (p. 8) that "AI adoption patterns continue to evolve, and the measures used in this paper predate many model innovations as well as the widespread diffusion of agentic platforms."
- **LLM-derived primary exposure measure** (GPT-4 self-assessing its own task capability) is the project's own flagged correlated-error risk category; robustness checks only partially mitigate it (agreement breaks down above the 80th percentile, exactly where the headline claims live).
- **Mechanism is not identified.** The paper is candid (§7, §8) that it cannot distinguish direct AI task-substitution from AI-driven erosion of educational signaling value (grade inflation masking true skill — citing Chirikov 2026, Hausman et al. 2026), training-channel effects, or other contemporaneous shocks; it rules out work-from-home, graduate oversupply, and monetary-policy shocks as *primary* drivers but does not claim to isolate AI's causal share precisely.
- **SUTVA/general-equilibrium spillovers** are explicitly unaddressed — the "control" group (bottom six deciles) may itself be affected by AI-driven labor reallocation post-2022, which would bias the headline deltas toward understating total AI impact.

**Why it earns a place here:** it is the first large-sample (6.6M graduates), administrative-wage-record study to measure AI-era labor market entry outcomes *by college major* rather than by occupation or industry — exactly the unit our own Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study needs to benchmark against. It supplies a rare quantitative anchor for a question §16 currently can only address qualitatively: whether GC's design-adjacent destinations behave more like the unharmed Commercial Art/Graphic Design cell or the harmed Communications/Marketing/Journalism cluster. It is also useful as a **methodological model** — the paper's own candor about exposure-measure disagreement at the tail, non-causal caveats, and the self-employment/grad-school confound is a template for how our own by-major or by-destination claims should be hedged.

**Verdict:** Consistent — all five secondary claims CONFIRMED against the paper, with the scope/denominator caveats above (the 5pp and 13% figures are regression-adjusted deltas relative to the pooled bottom six exposure deciles in 2022, not absolute population rates, and 13% is the initial 2-quarter shock that attenuates to ~3% by two years). For §16, the citable sentence should be narrow and cite the *decile*, not "AI majors" generally: **"In U.S. administrative wage-record data covering 6.6 million bachelor's graduates, majors in the single most AI-exposed decile (led by computer science) saw a 5-point lower initial employment rate and ~13% lower initial earnings after ChatGPT's release, roughly half attributable to a shift into lower-paying sectors (Orr, Tucker & Warren 2026); GC's closest analogue, Commercial Art and Graphic Design, sits in exposure decile 6, inside the less-exposed comparison group, yet shows one of the largest relative earnings declines in the creative/communications cluster (−0.070 log points, 2022→2024, descriptive), alongside negative earnings coefficients across Communications, Marketing, Advertising and Journalism — so for GC the exposure index is a weak guide to where the pressure landed."** *(Sentence corrected 2026-10-05: the first draft said graphic design showed "no detectable effect", from a misread table cell.)* Caveats that must travel with any citation: not peer-reviewed; primary exposure measure is LLM-self-assessed (GPT-4 judging GPT-4's own capability) and its ranking diverges from a real-usage measure above the 80th percentile; short post-ChatGPT window with unknown long-run persistence; institution sample skews large public doctoral universities and may not include South Carolina; mechanism (direct substitution vs. signal erosion vs. other) is not identified.
