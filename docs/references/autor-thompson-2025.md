# Autor & Thompson (2025) — *Expertise* (NBER WP 33941)

**Full citation:** Autor, D. H., & Thompson, N. (2025). *Expertise*. NBER Working Paper No. 33941 (version read: 18 June 2025). Originally the Joseph A. Schumpeter Lecture to the European Economic Association, 29 August 2024, under the title "Does Automation Replace Experts or Complement Expertise? The Answer is Yes."

**Accessibility:** Clean born-digital PDF. Abstract + §1 Introduction (pp. 1–3, read here) carry the full conceptual argument; the formal model is §2, the novel expertise measure and four-decade empirics follow.

**What the doc claims it says:** Per §16, that the *direction* of AI's effect on an occupation depends on **which** tasks it removes — automating an occupation's less-expert tasks raises the expertise the remaining job demands (wages up, employment down), while automating its expert tasks lowers those requirements (wages down, employment up, less-expert workers able to enter) — and that this is the mechanism behind the thinning execution-apprenticeship rung §16 already describes.

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper's target is the assumption, shared by human-capital, task, and "exposure" models alike, that occupations with similar automation exposure should be similarly affected. Autor & Thompson argue the opposite: "Since the same task may be relatively expert in one occupation and inexpert in another, automation can simultaneously replace experts in some occupations while augmenting expertise in others."

The framework rests on two pillars the canonical task models background: **expertise** — "a worker's capability... to perform specific tasks," hierarchical, so that "workers of a given expertise level can perform all tasks at or below their expertise level, but cannot perform tasks above" — and **task bundling within occupations**: "All tasks bundled in an occupation must be performed by each worker in that occupation, but expertise requirements differ among the tasks bundled." Crucially, "automation in the expertise model is not merely labor-saving, it is also expertise-displacing. Once a task is automated, the relevant task expertise is no longer needed anywhere in the economy."

The central prediction is a pair of countervailing effects: "automation that decreases expertise requirements reduces wages but permits the entry of less expert workers; automation that raises requirements raises wages but reduces the set of qualified workers." Their worked contrast is accounting clerks (automation removed *inexpert* bookkeeping, leaving expert problem-solving → wages rise, employment falls) versus inventory clerks (automation removed the *expert* tasks, leaving counting and stocking → wages fall, employment rises). They characterise the mechanism as a labour-supply shift: "task automation that reduce[s] occupational expertise requirements expands the effective supply of qualified labor; task automation that raise[s] occupational expertise requirements reduces the effective supply."

Empirically, over four decades, they "document that automation has raised wages and reduced employment in occupations where it eliminated inexpert tasks, but lowered wages and increased employment in occupations where it eliminated expert tasks," using "a novel, content-agnostic method for measuring job task expertise." They claim this "resolves the puzzle of why routine task automation has lowered employment but often raised wages in routine task-intensive occupations."

**Two limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) The empirics are about **automation over the past four decades**, not about generative AI. Applying the framework to AI is an *extrapolation of a mechanism*, which the authors invite (the keywords include Artificial Intelligence, and the lecture title is explicitly about the question) but do not themselves test on AI-era data. (2) The model is deliberately stylised — unidimensional expertise, exogenous task bundling, elasticity of occupational demand assumed to exceed one; the authors flag an alternative interpretation in a footnote, that rising productivity could saturate demand rather than expertise supply constraining employment.

**Why it earns a place here:** it supplies the *mechanism* for a claim §16 previously carried only as an empirical association (Brynjolfsson et al.'s early-career employment declines) plus a plausibility argument (Dell'Acqua et al.'s "AI substitutes for the guidance an experienced colleague provides"). It also connects the AI section to the framework's core thesis: if AI removes a creative occupation's *inexpert* tasks — production execution, resizing, routine comping — the surviving job demands **more** evaluative judgment from fewer people, which is exactly the capability KUD+ is built to measure and the reason §16 argues the program must now supply what the first job used to build. It is the strongest available support for that argument, and stronger than the scenario-modelling literature, because it is an identified empirical pattern rather than a projection.

**Verdict:** Consistent, with the extrapolation flagged. The §16 sentence must attribute the *empirical* finding to four decades of automation and present the AI application as an inference about mechanism, not a demonstrated AI result. Do not cite it as evidence about generative AI specifically.
