# Chin & Martin Richmond (2026) — *Work at the Frontier* (OpenAI Economic Research)

**Full citation:** Chin, C., & Martin Richmond, A. (2026). *Work at the Frontier: How AI is Expanding What People Do at Work*. OpenAI Economic Research, July 2026.

**Reference ID in background.html:** `ref-chin-richmond-2026`; cited in §16 (AI-era implications), and load-bearing for the §11 argument that a SOC-anchored destination taxonomy under-counts what graduates actually enter.

**Where it lives:**
- Published by OpenAI as an economic-research report. Not peer-reviewed.
- Open-access status: open. `https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/work-at-the-frontier-report.pdf`
- **Local copy:** `docs/references/_pdfs/openai-2026-work-at-the-frontier.pdf` — archived 2026-09-09. **This citation ran from August to September 2026 with no archived copy**, i.e. a load-bearing §16 claim depended on a live vendor URL. Card written retroactively at the same time.

**Accessibility:** Clean born-digital PDF. Key takeaways (p. 1), method (p. 2), Fig. 1–2 with the full classification split (p. 3), task-crossover discussion (p. 4), Fig. 3 per-role shares (p. 6), Table 2 recurring tasks (p. 7).

**What the doc claims it says:** Per §16, that ~44% of *occupation-specific* AI use concerns tasks historically belonging to another occupation; that design draws most heavily on cross-occupation tasks while marketing and engineering tasks travel furthest into other roles; and that this supports the §11 verdict that anchoring the destination side in a federal occupational taxonomy under-counts the cross-boundary task bundles graduates enter.

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

Method: a random sample of work-related messages from US ChatGPT users whose occupations are known from ChatGPT Business account role information, each classified to the single O*NET **detailed work activity (DWA)** that best matches its task content, then compared against the DWAs traditionally associated with the sender's own occupation. Eight occupation groups: customer experience, design, engineering, finance, HR, legal, marketing, sales. Messages are never read by humans; classification is done by model.

**The number that matters most, and its exact scope.** The classification is three-way, and both figures the report headlines are true of different denominators:

> "16.8 percent of messages are classified as Cross-occupation... 21.8 percent are classified as Within occupation... and 61.5 percent are classified as Generic."
> "Among non-generic messages, 43.5 percent are cross-occupation."

So **43.5% is the share of *occupation-specific* messages; 16.8% is the share of *all* work-related messages.** The report is transparent about the rebasing (Fig. 2 shows it explicitly), but the qualifier is load-bearing: "nearly half of AI use crosses occupational boundaries" would overstate it by a factor of ~2.6, while "nearly half of the AI use that is occupation-specific" is exact. §16 carries the absolute figure alongside the rebased one specifically so the qualifier cannot be dropped in a later edit without the sentence becoming visibly self-contradictory. *(Distinction flagged by the GC Alumni session, 2026-09-09.)*

**Per-role shares (Fig. 3), cross-occupation as a share of that role's occupation-specific messages:** customer experience 77%, **design 75%**, HR 69%, legal 56%, marketing 53%, sales 40%, finance 40%, engineering 28%. Across the eight groups, "between 11 and 30 percent of messages involve task crossover" in absolute terms.

**Direction — travellers vs borrowers.** The headline percentages hide a flow direction the report states plainly: "Marketing and engineering work appears widely in the AI use of workers in other fields. Meanwhile, workers in fields like design, sales, and human resources are more likely to use AI for tasks outside of their typical roles." So design is a heavy *borrower*, not a source; marketing/engineering tasks are the ones that *travel*. GC sits on both sides of that split, which is a sharper claim than any single percentage.

**Firm size.** "Task crossover is more common in smaller workplaces... cross-occupation use is 18.9 percent in 2–5 seat workspaces and 16.3 percent in workspaces with 101 or more seats among users in the middle 50 percent by message volume; the analogous pattern is not monotonic among top-quartile users." Directly relevant to GC destinations, much of which is small studios and in-house teams.

**The framing that justifies the §11 SOC argument.** The authors position usage as an early-warning signal rather than a measurement of change already realised: "Analysis of Generative AI usage offers a window into an earlier stage of the job evolution process, as workers can begin testing and recombining tasks before firms revise job descriptions or invent new job titles altogether." This is a *better* justification for the crosswalk lag than "the taxonomy is wrong" — the classification is not incorrect, it is downstream. *(Reframing suggested by the GC Alumni session, 2026-09-09; adopted in §16.)*

Also useful: Table 2's recurring cross-occupation tasks, where "Create marketing materials" has **design as its top borrowing group (13.6%)** — the single most direct datum for the claim that creative roles absorb marketing work.

**Limits.** Not peer-reviewed; single-vendor usage data from self-selected ChatGPT Business users; occupation is self-reported at signup; one DWA per message forces multi-task messages into a single bucket; and "generic" is doing a lot of work in the denominator — the 61.5% share is itself a modelling choice about what counts as occupation-specific.

**Verdict:** Consistent, with one qualifier that must never be dropped. §16's "roughly 44% of *occupation-specific* AI use" is exact as written; the absolute 16.8% is now stated beside it as structural protection. Do not paraphrase to "nearly half of AI use." The travellers-vs-borrowers direction and the leading-indicator framing are both the report's own and both strengthen the §11 argument rather than complicating it.
