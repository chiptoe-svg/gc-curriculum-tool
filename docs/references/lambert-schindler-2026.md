**What the doc would claim:** Per the brief, that remote work (WFH) correlates strongly with AI exposure; that in a joint specification the WFH coefficient barely changes while the AI-exposure coefficient drops to zero or flips positive; that this holds across WFH-exposure and actual-WFH-adoption treatments, measurement-error simulations, and several AI-exposure measures; and that the data are LinkedIn/job-postings based.

**What it actually says (short verbatim quotes, with page refs):**

Correlation of exposures: "the WFH exposure measure of Hansen et al. (2023) and the GenAI exposure index of Eloundou et al. (2024) have a Spearman rank correlation of 0.77" (p. 4) — **CONFIRMED**, "strongly" is a fair characterization of 0.77.

Joint-specification attenuation: "In single-treatment difference-in-differences specifications, both WFH and GenAI exposure predict sizable declines in early-career job opportunities in 2023–2025... Our first main result comes from a joint-treatment specification in which both exposures enter simultaneously. The WFH effects are robust... By contrast, the GenAI effects attenuate heavily and often become statistically indistinguishable from zero" (p. 4). The abstract states it more precisely: "Estimated jointly, the WFH effect remains, while the GenAI coefficient attenuates sharply and is often statistically indistinguishable from zero" (p. 2) — **CONFIRMED** for "barely changes" (WFH) and "drops to zero" (GenAI). The "flips positive" part of the brief is also directly supported: the robustness summary states the GenAI coefficient "attenuates, flips sign, or becomes statistically insignificant under most of the same exercises" (p. 22) — **CONFIRMED**, though "flips positive" is one of three listed outcomes, not the single or dominant one.

Robustness scope: "Several robustness exercises... calibrated Monte Carlo simulations of classical measurement error all preserve the WFH-dominant ranking" (p. 4); "we also analyze the effects of actual WFH adoption during 2021–2022... the WFH-channel remains a robust predictor" (pp. 17, 22); "Robustness across alternative WFH and GenAI exposure measures. The WFH coefficient..." (p. 22) — **CONFIRMED** across WFH-exposure, actual-WFH-adoption, measurement-error simulation, and multiple exposure measures, exactly as claimed.

Data source: "Data on new hires comes from Revelio Labs, which provides monthly matched employer–employee records assembled from résumés (predominantly, but not exclusively, from LinkedIn)" (p. 11); the second panel is "407 million online job vacancy postings" (p. 3) — **CONFIRMED**, "LinkedIn/job postings" is accurate, with the caveat that the hires panel is résumé-assembled and only *predominantly* (not exclusively) LinkedIn-sourced.

**Limits.** Working paper, not peer-reviewed, dated one version only (May 2026) as of this archive — no evidence it has been revised since. The "junior" outcome in the hires panel is coded from Revelio's own role-level taxonomy ("Entry-Level," "Junior," "Associate," etc., p. 11), not from worker age — this matters directly for reconciling against Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2026), who use age and get the opposite joint-specification result (see `brynjolfsson-2026-response.md`). The paper's own robustness section is extensive but entirely within its own two data sources (Revelio hires + job postings); it does not test against a payroll-administrative dataset, which is the gap the Stanford response fills.

**Why it earns a place here:** §16 currently treats the AI-driven decline in early-career/entry-rung hiring as settled background. This paper is the sharpest documented challenge to that premise — not a dismissal of an AI effect, but a direct omitted-variable claim that would, if it held up, substantially discount how much of the observed decline is actually AI-attributable versus a remote-work supervision/learning story. Whether or not the challenge survives (see the Brynjolfsson reconciliation), the §16 paragraph on the entry rung should name the confound and the live disagreement rather than treating the AI attribution as uncontested.

**Verdict:** All four secondary claims in the brief are CONFIRMED against the text, with the minor caveat that "flips positive" is one of three stated outcomes (attenuate / flip sign / become insignificant) rather than the headline result, and "LinkedIn" slightly overstates how exclusively the hires panel is LinkedIn-sourced (Revelio's résumé data is "predominantly, but not exclusively" LinkedIn). The paper does not, on its own, resolve the debate — see the companion card for the direct rebuttal using payroll data.
