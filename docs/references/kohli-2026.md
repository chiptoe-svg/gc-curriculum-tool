**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper tests whether LLM-judge panels — ensembles of diverse models voting on evaluation items, used to mitigate single-model bias — actually deliver the independence the Condorcet Jury Theorem requires for majority voting to help. The setup: "a panel of 9 frontier LLMs from 7 model families" (GPT-4o/4o-mini, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Llama 4 Maverick/Scout, Qwen3-32B, Mistral Large 3, DeepSeek-V3) evaluated on three natural-language-inference benchmarks (ChaosNLI-MNLI, -SNLI, -AlphaNLI), each with 100 human annotator labels per item for ground truth.

**Headline result.** "The 9 judges effectively provide only about 2 independent votes' worth of information. Roughly three-quarters of the panel's nominal independence is lost because the models make the same mistakes on the same items" (abstract). Using the Kish effective sample size (n_eff = k / [1 + (k−1)·φ̄]), the paper reports n_eff ≈ 2.0–2.5 across the three NLI datasets, with the text elsewhere stating the deficit means "no weighting scheme can extract a third independent vote from ∼2.2 effective votes of information" — the specific figure (≈2.2) background.html's §17 entry quotes.

**Consequences.** "The panel's actual accuracy falls 8–22 percentage points short of what independent voting would achieve, and the best single judge matches or outperforms the full panel across all conditions" (abstract). This is demonstrated concretely in the paper's own worked example: on MNLI, the panel (72.0%) barely edges its best single judge, Qwen3-32B (71.8%) — within noise; on SNLI, the best judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5, 84.2%) *dominates* the full panel (77.7%); on AlphaNLI the pattern persists (91.2% best judge vs. 88.7% panel). Critically, the deficit is not an aggregation-algorithm problem: "established aggregation methods — Dawid-Skene EM and accuracy-weighted voting — close at most 11% of the Condorcet gap across all four datasets [the three NLI sets plus RewardBench], even with oracle access to gold labels." The paper's stated bottleneck: "The bottleneck is correlated judges, not the aggregation algorithm, implying that scaling up panels cannot substitute for genuinely independent evaluation." The deficit is "stable across tasks, prompts, and temperatures" — n_eff stays in the 2.0–2.5 range across three NLI datasets, three prompt variants, two temperature settings, and a fourth task (RewardBench, pairwise preference, n_eff = 2.0), and "the scaling curve shows that adding judges beyond 5 yields negligible benefit."

**Limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) Not peer-reviewed (arXiv v1 only as of this card). (2) The panel is evaluated only on natural-language-inference classification tasks (entailment/neutral/contradiction, abductive reasoning) and one pairwise-preference benchmark — not on open-ended generative scoring of the kind the curriculum tool's own KUD+ auditor performs, so the exact n_eff figure is an illustrative order of magnitude for this project's use, not a direct measurement of this tool's judge setup. (3) The specific n_eff (≈2.0–2.5, with ≈2.2 appearing in the paper's own summary prose) is for this 9-judge, 7-family panel on these three datasets; a different panel composition or task would likely yield a different number — the paper itself frames the deficit's *stability* (not its exact magnitude) as the robust finding.

**Why it earns a place here, for KUD+ and this tool:** this is the direct empirical justification for §7's strongest claim — that panel/ensemble LLM judging cannot certify its own reliability because the panel's nominal diversity collapses to a small handful of truly independent opinions. It is the paper the text leans on to argue that "the only check on that error class is comparison against a signal the model did not generate" (i.e., assessed student artifacts and the external graduate-outcome study) — converting that external validation from a nice-to-have into a structural requirement, since an internal multi-judge loop cannot substitute for it.
