**What the doc would claim (per the two candidate uses):**
(a) §16 entry-rung paragraph — a degree-level analogue of Brynjolfsson et al.: junior/entry-level job opportunities are shrinking specifically within the destination occupations of more AI-exposed degrees, mirroring the early-career employment decline §16 already documents at the occupation level.
(b) A supply-side point for GC recruiting/enrollment — students are moving *toward* AI-exposed subjects rather than away from them (contra human-capital theory), which would be read as reassuring or alarming depending on interpretation for a program whose graduates sit ~60% in "grows with AI" occupations.

**What it actually says (verbatim, with page refs):**

*Data and design.* Revelio Labs LinkedIn profiles (UK workers, "full work history alongside their education records," p. 4) are used to build a time-invariant, degree-level (university × subject) AI exposure index from the 2002–2022 graduation cohorts' occupational destinations, scored against Eloundou et al. (2024)'s GPT-4-derived task-exposure measure. Critically: "We only use labor market information from before the release of ChatGPT in constructing the index to avoid contamination from LLMs" (p. 5) — so the exposure index is *not* computed from post-period LinkedIn data, addressing one obvious reverse-causality concern directly. This is merged with UCAS domestic-applicant microdata, 2020–2025 cycles, "1,090 university-subject pairs across six application cycles, yielding a total of 6,540 observations" (p. 6). The design is a standard two-way-fixed-effects DiD around the November 2022 ChatGPT release (Eq. 1, p. 7), with provider×subject and year fixed effects, standard errors two-way clustered by university and subject.

*Main estimate.* Table 1: "AI exposure x post" = **0.0596*** (SE 0.0132)** with provider×subject + year FE; falls to **0.0354** (SE 0.0144)** when subject×year FE are added (identification then comes only from within-subject, cross-university variation). Text, p. 9: "roughly six percent, or about 60 additional applications per cycle for the median degree." Event study (Fig. 2, p. 9): "In 2023, applications increase by four percent for each additional standard deviation of AI exposure. By 2024, this effect grows to eight percent. The effect levels off by 2025."

*Heterogeneity (pp. 11–12).* Positive in STEM and non-STEM (non-STEM imprecise, difference not significant); roughly twice as large at Russell Group universities (0.0917) as elsewhere (0.0471), difference not significant; **negative and insignificant at small universities**, effect "concentrated in medium-sized and large institutions"; positive for both genders and all five ethnic groups in the public subject-level data, none significantly different from each other.

*Labor-market side (pp. 12–13, the (a)-relevant result).* "Averaged over the post-treatment period, postings fall by 0.8 percent and the share of junior employment declines by 0.25 percentage points per standard deviation of exposure, **although the former is only statistically significant in some specifications** (Table A5)." Table A5 confirms this asymmetry exactly: postings coefficient is **-0.0078 (SE 0.0049), not significant** with university×subject+month FE, and only becomes significant (**-0.0122**, SE 0.0050**) once university×month FE are added; the junior-share coefficient is significant in *both* specifications (**-0.0025***, -0.0023***, SE ≈0.0002). So "job opportunities for juniors have deteriorated" (p. 3, abstract and intro) is carried almost entirely by the junior-share result, which is robust; the postings decline is explicitly hedged by the authors themselves as "only some evidence" — this is a real asymmetry worth preserving if the paper is cited, since the abstract's flatter language ("the entry-level jobs they will likely apply to are disappearing," p. 3) reads more confident than the body text's own caveat.

*Robustness (pp. 10–11).* Alternative exposure measures (α index, ISCO-2025 index, first-job-only variant): "qualitatively and quantitatively similar." CAH3-level re-estimation (87 of 166 subjects, 3,237 degrees): "all conclusions also remain the same." Raw-count outcome: 134 additional applications (SE 32.44, ***). Weighted by 2022 application volume: estimate rises to 0.0832 (stronger among large degrees). Leave-one-subject-out: range 0.0457–0.0653, all significant; "the lower bound comes from excluding computing, and even there the effect is more than three quarters of our headline magnitude."

*Limitations, in the authors' own words (p. 14).* "While the timing of the effects narrows the set of alternative explanations, **we cannot rule out that some shocks that arrived at the same time as ChatGPT are correlated with exposure**, and our estimates may partly reflect such shocks rather than the technology itself." On the exposure index: "It captures the share of tasks that today's models could substantially speed up, but not the value of those tasks within a job, how occupations will change over a graduate's career, or whether exposure leads to actual automation." And on supply vs. demand: "applications measure demand from students, not supply from universities. If universities increased capacity or intensified recruitment in exposed subjects over the same period, part of the response we attribute to students may reflect these institutional choices instead."

**Critical limit that governs use (b): GC's own field is excluded from the dataset.** Appendix A1 (p. 19–20, Table A1 notes) states plainly that "the nine unmatched groups, among them psychology, the creative arts, and languages, account for the remaining 26.5 percent and are excluded," and the table note spells it out further: "the remaining 26.5% falls in the 9 CAH1 groups with no Revelio field at all (**e.g. psychology, design and creative arts, language and area studies**)." Marketing survives only as one of four fields pooled into "Business and management" (CAH17, alongside accounting, business, finance) and is never reported separately. **This means the paper contains zero direct evidence — positive or negative — about application trends, AI exposure, or junior-job prospects for design, creative arts, or communications degrees.** Any claim that "students are moving toward GC-adjacent exposed subjects" would be an extrapolation from the paper's STEM/business/social-science sample to a subject area the authors explicitly could not measure and excluded for want of a defensible Revelio counterpart.

**Other identification threats, assessed:**
- *Pre-trends / COVID overlap.* The three pre-treatment cycles (2020–2022) span the most disrupted years in recent UK admissions history — 2020 first-lockdown cycle, 2021 Teacher Assessed Grades (record grade inflation, atypical subject-level demand swings). The authors assert parallel pre-trends from Figure 2 but do not discuss COVID-era admissions policy as a potential confound anywhere in the text; this is a real gap, not just an omitted robustness check, since 2021 TAG grade inflation is well documented to have shifted competitive subject access differentially.
- *UK fee/policy confound.* Tuition fees have been frozen in nominal terms since 2017, eroding in real terms — plausibly pushing students toward subjects perceived as safer career bets (which correlates with STEM-ness, which correlates with the exposure index: computing 0.59, engineering 0.55 vs. education 0.40, nursing-adjacent 0.38 per Table A3, p. 23). The heterogeneity check (STEM vs. non-STEM both positive) partially addresses this but the non-STEM estimate is imprecise, so it is suggestive rather than dispositive.
- *Exposure index is LLM-derived, but not from post-period data.* The underlying Eloundou et al. (2024) task-exposure scores come from GPT-4 self-assessing which O*NET tasks it can perform — a construct-validity concern (the technology grading its own reach) distinct from, and not addressed by, the authors' (correct) point that the labor-market inputs predate ChatGPT.
- *Overstatement check.* The abstract/intro state flatly that "the job opportunities for juniors have deteriorated for more exposed degrees" and "entry-level job opportunities are declining" — true of the junior-share result, which is robust. But stated without the hedge the body text itself applies to the companion postings result ("only... significant in some specifications"). Not a fabrication, but a rhetorical smoothing-over worth flagging if quoted from the abstract alone rather than the results section.

**Why it earns a place here — honest evaluation:**
For use (a), it is a reasonable *degree-level* companion to the occupation-level early-career decline literature §16 already cites (Brynjolfsson et al.), with its own robust junior-share estimate (Table A5, cols 3–4) surviving a university×month fixed-effects tightening. But it is a six-week-old, non-peer-reviewed program output, its postings result is explicitly weak by the authors' own admission, and its COVID-era pre-trend window is unexamined — it should be cited as corroborating/supplementary, not as a load-bearing pillar, and flagged as a working paper every time.

For use (b), the paper's headline finding (students moving toward exposed subjects) is well-identified and the strongest part of the paper — multiple robustness checks, consistent across demographics, concentrated where you'd expect (large, prestigious institutions). But it **cannot be used to say anything about design, creative arts, or communications enrollment specifically** — that subject area is named in the paper's own appendix as excluded for lack of data. Using this paper to argue "GC-adjacent fields are seeing a similar supply-side shift" would be citing a paper against its own stated scope. The honest, defensible use for (b) is only the macro point — that the broader UK applicant pool is not retreating from AI-exposed subjects in general, which is relevant context for competitive admissions pressure — not a claim about GC's specific subject area.

**Verdict:** Cite for **(a) only**, and even there as a secondary/corroborating source, explicitly labeled a non-peer-reviewed SPAR working paper (August 2026), with the postings-vs-junior-share asymmetry preserved rather than smoothed into "labor market prospects deteriorated." **Do not use for (b)** as currently scoped — the paper's own appendix excludes design/creative arts/communications from its sample, so it supports no claim about GC's specific subject area; at most it supports a one-sentence macro-context remark ("UK-wide, students are not retreating from AI-exposed subjects generally") clearly scoped as *not* about design/creative fields. If a §16 sentence is written from this paper, it should read something like: "A UK degree-level difference-in-differences study (Vossos et al., 2026, non-peer-reviewed working paper) finds the junior share of employment in AI-exposed degrees' destination occupations fell by 0.25 percentage points per standard deviation of exposure after ChatGPT's release — a degree-level echo of the occupation-level early-career decline documented elsewhere in this section — while a companion decline in job postings for the same degrees was only inconsistently significant."
