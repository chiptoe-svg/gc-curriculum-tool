# Frank et al. (2026) — *AI-exposed jobs deteriorated before ChatGPT* (arXiv:2601.02554)

**Full citation:** Frank, M. R., Javadian Sabet, A., Simon, L., Bana, S. H., & Yu, R. (2026). *AI-exposed jobs deteriorated before ChatGPT*. arXiv:2601.02554v1 [econ.GN]. Submitted 5 January 2026. **What the doc would claim:** Proposed use in §16's entry-rung paragraph — as timing evidence that AI-exposed occupations' labor-market weakening predates ChatGPT (so the entry-rung thinning §16 already documents should not be attributed to ChatGPT as a clean break), and as evidence that curriculum exposure to AI-relevant tasks is associated with better, not worse, first-job outcomes for graduates after ChatGPT — i.e. that abandoning "AI-exposed" skills in the GC curriculum would run against the paper's own evidence.

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper triangulates three large administrative/web datasets to separate pre-existing labor-market weakening from LLM diffusion: (1) monthly U.S. unemployment-insurance claims (ETA 203) by six-digit SOC occupation and state, 2015–2024; (2) 10,584,980 LinkedIn profiles (via Revelio Labs) tracking graduate cohorts' first jobs; (3) 3 million U.S. higher-education syllabi from the Open Syllabus Project (via the authors' own Course-Skill Atlas, Javadian Sabet et al. 2024, *Scientific Data*). "AI exposure" throughout is **not newly measured here** — it is imported wholesale from Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock, "GPTs are GPTs" (*Science* 2024, ref. 2 in the paper), whose underlying task-exposure labels were generated by **GPT-4 rating individual O*NET tasks for automatability** ("Rubric 1 β"). Frank et al. then build a syllabus-level "education exposure" score (`edu`) as the dot product of (a) a semantic-similarity vector between each course's learning objectives and O*NET tasks, and (b) that GPT-4-derived task-exposure vector (SI p. S7: "Let $\vec{e} \in [0,1]^{|T|}$ be the task-level exposure vector (2) under GPT-4, Rubric 1 β... We define the syllabus-level education exposure as the dot product $edu_s = \vec{t}_s \cdot \vec{e}$"). **This exposure measure is LLM-derived at its root** — both the occupation/task exposure labels and (transitively) the curriculum exposure score depend on an earlier GPT-4 rating exercise, not on direct observation of automation events.

**Finding 1 (unemployment risk, Fig. 1, pp. 3–5).** Using occupation-by-state unemployment-insurance claims, "unemployment risk in the most exposed quintiles begins rising after [an] early-2022 trough—well before ChatGPT's November 2022 launch—and then stabilizes rather than accelerating in the quarters following the launch" (p. 5). Computer/math occupations (SOC 15) show the largest 2022–2024 rise, beginning before ChatGPT and flattening afterward. A small number of states (CA, WA, AK) show post-launch increases in computer/math unemployment risk, but the authors caution these are state-specific and "timing alone cannot rule out a contribution from LLM diffusion" there (p. 5).

**Finding 2 (graduate cohorts, Fig. 2, pp. 5–8).** Using Revelio/LinkedIn profiles of graduates' first post-degree jobs, "graduate cohorts from 2021 onward entered LLM exposed jobs at lower rates than earlier cohorts with these gaps opening prior to late 2022" (abstract). The body text is explicit about timing: "this low performance for 2021 and 2022 graduates starts months before the launch of ChatGPT (note 2023 graduates entered the workforce only after ChatGPT's launch)" (p. 8). A salary-based placebo comparison (graduates whose first job was high-salary rather than high-exposure) shows a similar post-2022 trend, which the authors flag as "a broader early-career tightening that is not specific to LLM-exposed workers" (p. 7) — i.e., the graduate-cohort result is consistent with, but does not cleanly isolate, an AI-specific mechanism.

**Finding 3 (curriculum exposure and outcomes, Table 1, p. 10; text p. 9).** In regressions on 1,350,482 graduate job-starts with extensive fixed effects (state, sector, degree type, graduation year, CIP field, university), "educational LLM exposure (`edu`) is not meaningfully associated with first-job salary or time to first job for pre-ChatGPT job starts... In contrast, for post-ChatGPT job starts, higher `edu` predicts higher salaries and shorter job searches" (p. 9). Jobs with higher *occupational* LLM exposure (`llm`) are associated with *weaker* post-ChatGPT outcomes (negative `gpt×llm` interaction on salary), but the positive three-way interaction (`edu×llm×gpt`) indicates curriculum exposure "partially offsets these post-ChatGPT penalties in more-exposed jobs" (p. 9). The authors state plainly: "While not causal, they suggest that LLM-relevant preparation is at least compatible with better early-career outcomes in the post-ChatGPT period" (p. 10) — this is an explicit, authorial non-causal framing, not a reviewer inference. The `edu` measure itself is built from **pre-2020 syllabi only** ("our educational exposure measure is derived from syllabi that predate 2020 and therefore captures baseline differences across programs rather than curricular responses to ChatGPT," p. 12) — most recent syllabi in the Open Syllabus data are from 2017, and post-2017 graduates are matched to 2014–2017 syllabi by field/university as a *status quo* proxy. A robustness check reweighting the LinkedIn sample to match NSF's National Survey of College Graduates field-of-study distribution (SI pp. S11–S12, Table S4) finds the same pattern, "perhaps... stronger."

**Limits that bound citation.** (1) Not peer-reviewed (arXiv v1 only, submitted 5 Jan 2026 — check for updates/journal placement before final citation). (2) The authors explicitly disclaim causal identification throughout: "We do not identify the causal effect of LLMs on labor-market outcomes" (p. 12). (3) The exposure measure for both occupations and curricula is LLM-derived (GPT-4 task ratings), not an independently validated ground truth of automation — a correlated-error risk this project flags for any LLM-derived exposure measure. (4) The `edu` curriculum measure is a static, pre-2020 baseline proxy, not a measure of what any given cohort was actually taught near graduation, so it cannot speak to whether a program *updated* its curriculum post-ChatGPT — only to whether having had more AI-relevant content historically correlates with better post-ChatGPT outcomes. (5) The salary variable is a Revelio proprietary ML estimate, not verified against actual wages (p. 7: "We are not able to directly verify the estimated salaries against workers' actual salaries"). (6) Time-to-first-job analyses exclude graduates with no observed job within three years, which may understate delays for the most disadvantaged graduates (p. 12).

**Why it earns a place here:** GC's curriculum and its graduates' first jobs are exactly the two objects this paper studies at the macro scale — occupational AI exposure and curricular AI exposure, measured against real labor-market outcomes. It gives §16 a timing correction (the entry-rung thinning predates ChatGPT, so attributing it to ChatGPT specifically is empirically contestable) and a complementary, non-causal data point that curricular engagement with AI-exposed tasks tracks with *better*, not worse, graduate outcomes post-ChatGPT — directly relevant to whether GC should scale back or lean into AI-exposed skills in its curriculum. The explicit authorial "not causal" framing is a model for how §16 should hedge its own citation of this result.

**Verdict:** Consistent with the three secondary claims, with required corrections to scope and causal framing (see below). Cite for timing and for the directional, non-causal curriculum-outcome association; do not cite the curriculum-pay finding as causal, and flag the LLM-derived exposure measure wherever the citation lands.

### Adversarial check of the three secondary claims

1. **"Unemployment risk in AI-exposed occupations rose from early 2022, before ChatGPT."** **CONFIRMED.** Fig. 1 (p. 4) and text pp. 3–5: unemployment risk in the most LLM-exposed occupation quintiles "begins rising after [the] early-2022 trough—well before ChatGPT's November 2022 launch—and then stabilizes rather than accelerating in the quarters following the launch." Computer/math occupations show the clearest pattern (p. 5).

2. **"2021+ graduate cohorts entered AI-exposed jobs at lower rates, with gaps opening before late 2022."** **CONFIRMED.** Fig. 2C and text p. 8: cohorts 2021 and 2022 show lower `jobGap` for the most LLM-exposed job decile in their first few months and again around one year post-graduation, and "this low performance... starts months before the launch of ChatGPT." Caveat worth carrying: a salary-based placebo (p. 7) shows a similar post-2022 trend for high-salary (not just high-exposure) first jobs, so the paper itself notes this may reflect a "broader early-career tightening," not an AI-specific effect alone.

3. **"After ChatGPT, graduates who took more AI-exposed curricula had higher first-job pay and shorter job searches."** **CONFIRMED, with a load-bearing correction: the paper calls this explicitly non-causal and descriptive, not causal.** Table 1 (p. 10) and text p. 9: higher education exposure (`edu`) predicts higher post-ChatGPT salaries and shorter job searches, with the authors stating "While not causal, they suggest that LLM-relevant preparation is at least compatible with better early-career outcomes" (p. 10). Also worth noting: the benefit of `edu` is a partial *offset* against a separately negative effect of job-level LLM exposure (`llm`) on post-ChatGPT salary, not a simple "more exposure, more pay" story — and `edu` is built from **pre-2020 syllabi**, so it measures baseline curricular differences across programs, not any post-ChatGPT curricular response.

**Exposure-measurement flag:** the occupation/task exposure labels underlying both the occupational exposure (`llm`) and the curriculum exposure (`edu`) are **GPT-4-derived** (from Eloundou et al. 2024, "GPTs are GPTs," *Science*), not directly observed automation outcomes. Any §16 citation of this paper should carry the project's standard correlated-error caveat for LLM-derived exposure measures.
