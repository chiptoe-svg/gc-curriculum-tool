**What the background doc claims it says:** The 2013 meta-analysis of after-action reviews in professional contexts quantifies the effect: structured debriefs improve subsequent performance by approximately 25%, with effect sizes consistent across teams and individuals, simulated and real settings. The structure that works is not elaborate — what happened, why, what to do differently — but it must be specific to the concrete failure, timely, and outcome-oriented.

**What it actually says (synthesis):** The meta-analysis examined debrief effects on performance in team and individual settings, drawing on **46 independent samples (N = 2,136) from 31 studies (29 published, 2 unpublished)** — not "46 studies" (correction below, TC-count). Debriefs (also called after-action reviews) are structured retrospective discussions of recent performance, widely used in military, medical, aviation, and other high-stakes professional contexts.

**Overall effect:** On average, teams and individuals that conducted debriefs outperformed their non-debrief counterparts by approximately 25% (after excluding three extreme outliers, the average was still 21%). The effect was consistent: even "misaligned" debriefs (those not meeting all the best-practice criteria) produced positive effects, suggesting debrief efficacy is robust rather than fragile.

**Team vs. individual:** Both team and individual debriefs produced significant positive effects. Team debriefs specifically improved group performance by up to 38% in the most effective conditions.

**Simulated vs. real settings:** Effect sizes were similar across simulated (training environment) and real operational settings, suggesting that debrief benefits are not artifacts of controlled training contexts.

**Structural factors:** Structured debriefs outperformed unstructured ones. The use of multimedia aids showed no meaningful improvement. Average debrief duration in the studies was approximately 18 minutes — suggesting that brief, focused debriefs are effective, not just lengthy elaborate reviews. The strongest effects occurred when debriefs were aligned in purpose, participant, and focus.

**What makes debriefs work — correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; TC4, substantive).** The earlier "what happened / why / what to do differently... timely, and outcome-oriented" recipe does **not** match this paper's own stated structure, and a full-text check (abstract and Table 1 definitions) found no support for it as the paper's finding. The paper's own four "essential elements" of a debrief (Table 1, p. 233) are: **active (vs. passive) self-learning, developmental (vs. administrative) intent, specific (vs. general) events, and multiple (vs. single) information sources** — a different structure than "what happened / why / what to do differently." Two further precision points: (1) **timeliness is listed as a direction for future research, not a tested or established moderator** — "Additional moderators that were not adequately covered in prior research... include temporal proximity of the review event" (p. 242); (2) **generic reflection prompts were not tested against outcome-focused ones** in this meta-analysis — the "generic prompts are less effective" contrast is this project's own extrapolation, not a finding reported here. `background.html` §4 has been corrected to state the paper's own four essential elements and its timing caveat, rather than the invented recipe.

**Second correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; TC2, TC3, TC5, minor/substantive).** Three further precision points, confirmed against the held PDF:

1. **Count (TC2).** The paper's own unit is "46 samples" drawn from "31 studies (29 published, 2 unpublished)" with "a total N of 2,136" (p. 235) — "46 studies" conflates the sample count with the study count. Fixed in the synthesis above.
2. **Population moderator (TC3).** The paper's own tested population split is **medical vs. nonmedical** ("medical, k = 28, d = .66; nonmedical, k = 18, d = .69," p. 239) — the text never uses "military" or "civilian" as a coded moderator (checked full text; "military" appears only in background/origin framing, e.g. "Debriefs... have become a common tool... in military settings," p. 231).
3. **"Stable across moderators" overstates the paper (TC5).** The paper's own robustness language — "the efficacy of debriefs appears quite robust. Effect sizes averaged in the range of 20% to 26%, showing similar results across teams and individuals, across simulated and real settings... and for medical and nonmedical participants" (p. 240) — covers only those methodological factors. Two of the paper's own *substantive* moderators move effect size by large amounts in the opposite direction from "stable": **facilitation** roughly triples effectiveness ("facilitated debriefs (d = .75, or 27%) were about 3 times as effective as nonfacilitated debriefs (d = .25, or 10%)," p. 239, confirming Hypothesis 3), and **level-alignment** more than doubles it at the team level ("observed effectiveness was greater when team performance was measured (d = 1.20, or 38%) than when individual performance was measured (d = 0.41, or 16%)," p. 239, confirming Hypothesis 2). A blanket "stable across moderators" claim is therefore not supported — the paper's own headline moderators (facilitation, alignment) are exactly where effect size varies most.

**Verdict:** Consistent on the ~25% overall effect (21% after excluding outliers) and on effect sizes holding across teams/individuals, simulated/real settings, and medical/nonmedical samples (not "military/civilian," which the paper never codes). Not consistent on the specific "what happened / why / what to do differently, timely, outcome-oriented" debrief structure, which is not this paper's finding — corrected above to the paper's own four essential elements, with timeliness flagged as future research rather than an established factor. Also not consistent on any claim that effects are "stable across moderators" generally — facilitation (~3×) and level-alignment (~2.4× at team level) are the paper's own largest moderators and move effect size substantially.
