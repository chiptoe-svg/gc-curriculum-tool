The §17 entry: "Meta-analysis of skill decay: cognitive/accuracy skills can lose on the order of a standard deviation of performance after a year of non-use — the order-of-magnitude anchor for the depth-map durability caveat (§4)."

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

A meta-analysis of 189 independent data points from 53 articles on loss of trained skills/knowledge over periods of non-use, reporting a standardized effect size *d* (end-of-training performance vs. performance after a retention interval). The abstract states: "Results indicate that there is substantial skill loss with nonpractice or nonuse, with the amount of skill loss ranging from an effect size (d) of −0.01 immediately after training to a d of −1.4 after more than 365 days of nonuse" (p. 57).

**Table 3 (p. 77, "Results of Overall Meta-Analysis for the Relationship Between Retention Interval and Skill Retention")** breaks this into 8 retention-interval bins (Table 2, p. 76): (1) <1 day, (2) 1–7 days, (3) 8–14 days, (4) 15–28 days, (5) 29–90 days, (6) 91–180 days, (7) 181–365 days, (8) >365 days. The corrected mean effect (δ) by bin: bin 1 δ=0.02; bin 2 δ=−1.01; bin 3 δ=−1.52; bin 4 δ=−0.93; bin 5 δ=−0.65; bin 6 δ=−1.39; bin 7 δ=−1.03; **bin 8 (>365 days) δ=−1.27** (mean d=−1.28; min d=−2.48, max d=−0.92; n=3 data points, total sample 275). The correlation between retention-interval category and corrected mean d across the 8 bins is −0.51.

**A precision gap worth flagging.** The abstract's headline "−1.4 after more than 365 days" does not exactly match Table 3's own >365-day row, which gives δ=−1.27 (mean d=−1.28). The bin closest to −1.4 is actually bin 6 (91–180 days, δ=−1.39), not bin 8. The discrepancy is modest (−1.27 vs. −1.4) and both numbers are "on the order of" one SD, but a precise quote of "−1.4" as *the* >365-day figure is not what Table 3 itself reports for that bin.

**Moderator pattern (Table 4, p. 78, "collapsed across all retention intervals," so not year-specific):** cognitive tasks decay faster than physical tasks (cognitive δ=−1.15 vs. physical δ=−0.75); accuracy-based tasks decay faster than speed-based tasks (accuracy δ=−1.00 vs. speed δ=−0.32); artificial tasks decay faster than natural tasks (artificial δ=−0.98 vs. natural δ=−0.93, a much smaller gap); and — a genuine surprise the authors flag themselves — closed-looped (fixed-sequence, e.g., preflight checks) tasks actually decayed *less* than open-looped (continuous, e.g., tracking, problem-solving) tasks (closed δ=−0.71 vs. open δ=−1.04). The authors are explicit that this is the reverse of their own hypothesis, which (following prior qualitative reviews) predicted open-looped tasks would retain better: "a higher overall level of retention being reported for closed-looped (−0.71) instead of open-looped tasks (−1.04) — a finding that is in contrast to the study hypothesis" (p. 80). This matters for any future citation of this source: open-looped, continuous, problem-solving-type tasks — arguably closer to what GC coursework in ideation, critique, and design problem-solving resembles — are the *faster*-decaying category in this meta-analysis, not the slower one a naive reading of "closed-loop = rote = fragile" might assume.

**Limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) The effect sizes are in standard-deviation (*d*) units of task performance on the specific skills/tasks in the 53 meta-analyzed studies (largely military, industrial, and laboratory psychomotor/cognitive tasks) — not in KUD+ depth-scale units. Any mapping from "~1 SD of task performance" to "~1 KUD depth level" is an extrapolation layered on top of this source, not something the meta-analysis measured. (2) The >365-day bin has only 3 data points (n=275 total sample) — the thinnest-evidenced bin in Table 3, and its confidence interval (−2.90 to 1.03, using the uncorrected bound) is wide. (3) Table 4's moderator breakdowns are "collapsed across all retention intervals" (the authors' own words, p. 76) — i.e., the cognitive/accuracy-decay-faster figures are not specific to a one-year interval; they are pooled across all 8 bins from <1 day to >365 days. (4) The retention-interval bins are overlapping-category, rational groupings chosen by the authors to "reflect an exponential increase," not uniform calendar periods, so "per year" is a loose gloss on "the open-ended >365-day bucket," not a rate measured per 365-day unit.

**Why it earns a place here, for KUD+ and this tool:** it is the strongest available empirical anchor for the depth-map's single most important caveat — that a depth score records what a course develops *at the moment it is taught*, not what survives to graduation. Both §4 and §6 use it identically: as an order-of-magnitude existence proof that unreinforced cognitive skill decays substantially (on the order of, though not precisely, one SD) within about a year, which is the empirical grounding for insisting the opportunity map be read as "at the moment of the course, reinforced or not."

**Verdict:** Partially inconsistent on a precise figure, consistent on the substantive claim. Background.html's own wording is careful — it says "on the order of a standard deviation per year of non-use," hedged language that survives the gap between the abstract's quoted "−1.4" and Table 3's actual >365-day row (δ=−1.27, close to but not equal to −1.4; the −1.4-ish value, −1.39, actually belongs to the 91–180-day bin). Because background.html never quotes "−1.4" itself and only claims the order-of-magnitude ("on the order of a standard deviation"), this imprecision in the source's own abstract does not propagate into a false claim in background.html. It would become a problem only if a future edit tried to cite a specific number (e.g., "a 1.4 SD loss after one year") rather than the current hedged phrasing — flagging this now so that specificity is not added without checking Table 3 first. **Two corrections to the twin card.** (1) `ai_career_impact`'s arthur-1998.md states "d = −0.01 (immediate) → d = −1.4 (>365 days)" as if −1.4 were Table 3's >365-day figure; per Table 3 (PDF p. 23 of the local copy, printed p. 77), the >365-day row is δ=−1.27 (mean d=−1.28), and −1.4-ish (δ=−1.39) belongs to the 91–180-day bin instead. (2) **More consequential:** the twin card's moderator-pattern bullet states "closed-loop (fixed-sequence, e.g., preflight checks) tasks decayed *faster* than open-loop (continuous, e.g., tracking, problem solving) tasks, which are better retained even over months/years." This is the **reverse** of what Table 4 reports and what the paper's own prose states: closed-looped δ=−0.71 (less decay), open-looped δ=−1.04 (more decay), with the authors explicitly noting this "is in contrast to the study hypothesis" (which had predicted the direction the twin card states). Both corrections should be applied to the twin card and are being announced to that repo.
