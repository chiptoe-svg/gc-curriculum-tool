**What it actually says (synthesis):** The 1993 paper in *Psychological Review* introduced the deliberate practice framework and is frequently cited as the most influential paper on expertise in the second half of the twentieth century. Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer conducted a study of student violinists at the Music Academy of Berlin, comparing three groups ranked by their teacher assessments: "best violinists" (those expected to become international soloists), "good violinists" (expected to have professional orchestral careers), and "music teachers" (expected to teach rather than perform professionally). A fourth group of professional pianists provided additional data.

Central finding: Differences in retrospective estimates of accumulated deliberate practice (practice alone, working on specific technique, highly focused) corresponded closely to each skill level. By age 18, best young violinists had accumulated an average of **7,410 hr** of practice alone, reliably more than the **5,301 hr** accumulated by the good violinists, F(1, 27) = 4.59, p < .05; both groups had reliably more than the **3,420 hr** accumulated by the music teachers, F(1, 27) = 11.86, p < .01 (p. 379). A fourth group, professional middle-aged violinists, had accumulated **7,336 hr** by age 18 — "so close to the average of 7,410 hr for the best young violinists... that the difference is not statistically significant" (p. 380). The authors concluded that individual differences in performance could be largely explained by accumulated deliberate practice, and that talent (innate ability) played a much smaller role than popular belief held.

The paper introduced the specific definition of "deliberate practice" that distinguishes it from mere repetition: it must be (1) designed to improve specific aspects of performance, (2) highly effortful (at the limits of current ability), (3) informed by immediate feedback, and (4) accompanied by full attention. Playing pieces you already know fluently does not qualify; working through difficult passages with immediate correction does.

The paper's own generalized aggregate figure, stated in the General Discussion rather than tied to a specific age or group, is "over 10,000 h of deliberate practice extended over more than a decade" (pp. 393–394, sentence spans the page break) — this is the paper's own headline phrase for the timescale of expert performance, not a claim that violinists specifically reach 10,000 hr "by age 20." This is also the source of the popular "10,000-hour rule" framing, though Ericsson et al. themselves state the figure as a floor reached over a minimum of a decade, not a fixed target age.

The paper's claims were significantly challenged by subsequent meta-analyses, most notably Macnamara et al. (2014), which found that deliberate practice explained substantially less of the variance in performance than Ericsson et al. implied.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; ER3, substantive).** This card previously stated round, approximate figures — "approximately 10,000 hours... by age 20; good violinists approximately 7,500 hours; music teachers approximately 5,000 hours" — that do not match the paper's own reported numbers. The actual age-18 figures (7,410 / 5,301 / 3,420 hr for best/good/teachers, pp. 379–380) are lower than the card previously stated for every group, and the paper does not report an age-20 figure for any group in the text (Figure 9 plots accumulated hours through age 20 but gives no table of exact values at that age). Separately, `problem-solving-deep-dive.html` and `background.html` previously described the timescale as "tens of thousands of hours," which overstates the paper's own aggregate figure of "over 10,000 h... extended over more than a decade" (pp. 393–394) by roughly 2–9× relative to the paper's own Study 1 data. Both documents have been corrected to use the paper's own phrasing.

**Verdict:** Consistent on deliberate practice's four defining criteria and on the broad shape of the finding (accumulated deliberate practice tracks skill level). Not consistent, prior to this pass, on the specific hour figures by group and age, and on "tens of thousands of hours" as the paper's timescale claim — corrected above to the paper's own numbers ("over 10,000 h... extended over more than a decade," pp. 393–394) and Study 1's age-18 figures (pp. 379–380). The background doc's citation of this reference as a theoretical anchor for the deliberate-practice component of the framework's extrapolation, immediately followed by the Macnamara et al. (2014) tempering caveat, remains appropriate.
