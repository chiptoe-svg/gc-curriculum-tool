# Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg (2025) — *Correlated Errors in Large Language Models*

**Full citation:** Kim, E., Garg, A., Peng, K., & Garg, N. (2025). Correlated Errors in Large Language Models. *Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML)*, PMLR 267. arXiv:2506.07962 [cs.CL], 9 Jun 2025. Cornell University (Kim, Peng, Garg); Avi Garg independent.

**What the background doc claims it says:** §7: "LLM judges share correlated errors from common training — a nine-model panel behaves like roughly two independent votes (Kohli, 2026), and larger, more capable models are more correlated, not less (Kim et al., 2025) — so no self-consistency pass, judge panel, or multi-faculty vote can cancel a bias the judges hold in common." The §17 entry: "Across >350 models, LLMs share correlated errors from common training — and larger, higher-accuracy models are more correlated, not less — so ensembling LLM judges cannot cancel shared bias."

**What it actually says (synthesis):**

The paper directly tests an assumption: "Diversity in training data, architecture, and providers is assumed to mitigate homogeneity in LLMs. However, we lack empirical evidence on whether different LLMs differ meaningfully." The authors run "a large-scale empirical evaluation on over 350 LLMs overall, using two popular leaderboards [HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard, Stanford HELM] and a resume-screening task" — 349 models on 12,032 MMLU-style questions (HuggingFace), 71 models on 14,042 questions (HELM), and 20 models on 1,800 resume-job description pairs.

**Headline correlation finding.** "We find substantial correlation in model errors — on one leaderboard dataset, models agree 60% of the time when both models err" (abstract). The body specifies this is the HELM dataset: "pairs of models agree on average about 60% of the time when both models are incorrect (choosing between incorrect answers uniformly at random would lead to an agreement rate of 1/3)" (p. 2). Figure 1 (p. 2) visualizes pairwise error-agreement rates for both leaderboards, sorted by model accuracy, and shows nearly every model pair agreeing on errors well above the chance baseline.

**Headline scaling finding — the one background.html quotes.** Through regression analysis on model pairs, the authors find three predictors of correlated errors: shared provider, shared base architecture, and shared size — but crucially, correlation persists beyond shared components: "even after conditioning on these factors, pairs of models that are *more accurate individually* also have more correlated errors. This means that newer models that differ on the surface may be converging in their outputs" (p. 2). This is stated without qualification as a property of individually-more-accurate models, independent of architecture or provider — exactly the "larger, more capable models are more correlated, not less" claim background.html attributes to it.

**Downstream applications.** The paper traces two consequences: (1) LLM-as-judge evaluation, where "model correlations substantially affect LLM model evaluations when another model is used to proxy ground truth labels: judges overinflate the accuracy of models that are less accurate than it — especially for models of the same provider or architecture"; (2) hiring, where correlated errors across firms using similar models reproduce "algorithmic monoculture" and "systemic exclusion" concerns from prior theoretical literature (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021; Bommasani et al., 2022; Peng & Garg, 2024a).

**Limits that bound how far this may be cited.** (1) The 60% figure is dataset-specific (stated for HELM; the HuggingFace figure is not quoted at the same precision in the material read here) — treat "60%" as an illustrative, not universal, number. (2) "Error agreement" and "accuracy" are measured on standard multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU-style) and one resume-screening task; neither is an LLM-as-judge-of-open-ended-text setting of the kind the curriculum tool's own auditor runs, so the application to KUD+ scoring is an analogy, not a direct replication. (3) The paper quantifies *that* correlation rises with individual accuracy and shared components; it does not itself quantify an "effective sample size" for a judge panel the way Kohli (2026) does — that conversion is the companion paper's contribution, not this one's.

**Why it earns a place here, for KUD+ and this tool:** this is the empirical backbone of §7's single most self-critical passage — the admission that "the tool's own auditor is an LLM, so it is exposed to exactly the... inference" bias Xu documents, and that "no self-consistency pass, judge panel, or multi-faculty vote can cancel a bias the judges hold in common." Without this paper (and Kohli 2026), that claim would be an assertion; with it, it is an empirical finding from >350 models across three datasets, directly on point for why KUD+'s evidence-above-zero rule and external graduate-outcome validation are structurally necessary rather than a nice-to-have.

**Verdict:** Consistent. Both figures §7 attributes to this source — the ≈60% error-agreement rate and the "larger/more accurate models are more correlated, not less" scaling result — are stated by the paper substantially as background.html represents them, including the counterintuitive direction (more capable models are *more* correlated, which runs against a naive "better models, more independent, more trustworthy ensemble" intuition). No correction needed.
