# Kapur (2016) — Examining Productive Failure, Productive Success, Unproductive Failure, and Unproductive Success in Learning

**What the background doc claims it says:** This conceptual paper introduces the productive/unproductive distinction and identifies the two-phase generate-then-consolidate structure as the design unit. Kapur (2016) explicitly distinguishes productive from *unproductive* failure — failure without expert consolidation, without structured debrief, produces no learning advantage and can damage motivation and self-efficacy. The mechanism is not failure as such; it is failure followed by sense-making.

**What it actually says (synthesis):** The 2016 paper in *Educational Psychologist* is a theoretical and conceptual contribution to the productive failure literature. Rather than reporting an experiment, it provides a 2×2 framework for analyzing instructional conditions along two dimensions: (1) whether initial activity performance is high or low, and (2) whether learning outcomes (retention, transfer) are high or low. This creates four quadrants:

**Productive Failure:** Initial performance is poor (students fail at the task), but long-term learning outcomes are strong. This is the signature condition Kapur's research has identified — struggle before consolidation.

**Productive Success:** Initial performance is high and learning outcomes are also high. Possible in principle, but the paper argues that highly scaffolded instructional designs that produce immediate success often do so by giving students the answers (removing the effortful processing that produces durable learning), making them unproductive success instead.

**Unproductive Failure:** Initial performance is poor and learning outcomes are also poor. Failure that is not followed by expert consolidation produces no learning advantage. This is the crucial distinction the background doc references — unscaffolded discovery learning, poorly designed problems, or failure without structured debriefing all fall here.

**Unproductive Success:** Initial performance is high but learning outcomes are weak — students succeed at correctly executing procedures during instruction (or at imitating the worked examples) but cannot transfer or retain the knowledge.

The paper's key design principle: the goal of instruction should be to maximize productive learning outcomes, even at the expense of immediate performance during instruction. A learning environment that looks like it is going well (students succeeding on practice tasks) may be producing unproductive success; one that looks difficult (students failing) may be producing productive failure.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; K62/K63, substantive).** Two overstatements in the prior framing, both now fixed in `background.html`:

1. **"Can damage motivation and self-efficacy" (K62) has no support anywhere in this paper.** The words "self-efficacy" and "debrief" never appear in the text (checked all 12 pages). What *is* supported: unguided problem solving or discovery learning without subsequent consolidation "often leads to dismal learning outcomes" (p. 290) — i.e., the "produces no learning advantage" half of the claim is fine; the self-efficacy/motivation-damage half is this framework's own extrapolation and has been dropped.
2. **"Only failure followed by consolidation reliably builds transferable depth" (K63) is contradicted by the paper's own hedge.** Kapur explicitly declines to rank productive failure above productive success: "Even though both productive failure and productive success can lead to productive learning outcomes, it is not clear if one is better than the other" (p. 294). The four-way productive/unproductive × failure/success structure itself is a fair characterization of the paper's framework (it does describe all four cells), but "only failure... reliably builds" overclaims a ranking the paper refuses to make. Also worth noting: Kapur never uses the word "quadrant," and the article contains no figure of its own — any quadrant diagram attached to this citation is the framework's own illustration, not the paper's.

`background.html`'s Figure 1 caption and unproductive-failure-cell description have been corrected to (a) drop "can damage motivation and self-efficacy" in favor of "no learning gain," and (b) note productive success as an equally viable second route, with the grid/examples labeled as the framework's own.

**Third correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; K62, substantive).** Two further overstatements, both now corrected above and in the live doc:

1. **Productive success's worked example is Kapur's own PBL/guided-inquiry example, not direct instruction.** "An established and well-researched example of productive success is PBL... Through PBL, students learn the targeted domain knowledge... Teachers model behavior, guide student problem solving by asking questions, and directing attention to critical features" (p. 293). Productive success is explicitly *guided problem-solving that succeeds*, not instruction that happens to be well-matched to readiness — the "Unproductive Success" section above has been trimmed to remove the direct-instruction framing it previously carried.
2. **Direct instruction as unproductive success is Kapur's own explicit open question, hedged, not a settled claim.** Section heading: "COULD DIRECT INSTRUCTION BE A FORM OF UNPRODUCTIVE SUCCESS?" (p. 295). His answer is two-sided: "given direct instruction's consistently superior performance against discovery learning, it would be rather harsh to categorize it as an unproductive success. On the other hand, as the preceding analyses showed, direct instruction was found wanting against productive failure and productive success" (p. 295), and later: "it is not as harsh to categorize direct instruction as an unproductive success relative to productive failure and productive success. It is important to emphasize the relative nature of the categorization" (p. 296). Kapur never flatly states DI "is often unproductive success" — he poses it as a question, answers it with an explicit hedge ("rather harsh"), and frames the categorization as relative, not absolute. The card's "Unproductive Success" description above has been trimmed to the paper's own abstract definition of the quadrant, without attributing the DI-is-unproductive-success claim to Kapur as settled fact.

**Verdict:** Consistent on the productive/unproductive distinction and the generate-then-consolidate design unit; not consistent on the self-efficacy/motivation claim (unsupported, dropped), on failure being the *only* reliable route to transferable depth (the paper calls this an open question), on productive success's example being direct instruction (Kapur's own examples are PBL/guided inquiry), or on direct-instruction-as-unproductive-success being a settled conclusion (Kapur poses it as a hedged open question) — all corrected above.
