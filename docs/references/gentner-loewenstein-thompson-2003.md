# Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003) — Analogical Encoding

**Full citation:** Gentner, D., Loewenstein, J., & Thompson, L. (2003). Learning and Transfer: A General Role for Analogical Encoding. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 95(2), 393–408. Demonstrates that comparing two cases during learning ("analogical encoding") abstracts a transferable schema and produces markedly better cross-context transfer than studying the same cases separately — shown with novices learning negotiation strategies, including a live face-to-face negotiation.

**What the doc claims it says (corrected 2026-10-07):** Case comparison (analogical encoding) produces cross-context transfer **among novices learning negotiation strategies** (undergraduates, not professional trainees). *Previously* the doc described this as "replicated in professional training via case comparison" and this card described the participants as "MBA-type"; both have been corrected — see below.

**What it actually says (synthesis):** The paper's premise is that case-based teaching (the mainstay of business, law, and medicine) varies widely in effectiveness because learners tend to encode cases in surface-bound terms and then fail to retrieve them when a structurally similar but superficially different problem appears (the authors note that "over 80% of people's remindings were based on surface similarity"). Their proposed fix is *analogical encoding*: having learners explicitly *compare two examples* and articulate what they share. The act of comparison foregrounds the common relational structure, yielding a more abstract, portable problem schema. **All three experiments reported in this paper use undergraduate students, not professional trainees:** "A total of 48 undergraduate students participated" (Exp. 1, printed p. 397); "A total of 128 undergraduate students participated" (Exp. 2, printed p. 399); "A total of 158 undergraduate students participated" (Exp. 3, printed p. 402); participants were recruited "to fulfill a course requirement" (printed pp. 397, 399, 402). The paper's own professional-population evidence is cited as *prior work by other authors*, not this paper's data: "several groups of advanced learners (e.g., accountants, consultants, managers, and executives)" refers to Thompson et al. (2000), a different, earlier paper discussed in this one's introduction as background — this 2003 paper's own three experiments are undergraduate studies. The paper itself frames its contribution accordingly: "The present studies demonstrate that analogical encoding can help *novices* to learn and transfer negotiation strategies" (p. 403). They test *contingent contracts* (deals indexed to uncertain future outcomes) and *trade-offs* (logrolling across issues of differing importance).

**Experiment 1 — comparison vs. no case study.** Participants who drew a guided analogy across cases were far more likely to produce the target negotiation solution than baseline participants who had no case study: roughly 13% of analogy-trained participants applied the principle versus about 6% of baseline, with the comparison process also reducing reliance on naive compromise solutions. This establishes that case comparison helps relative to nothing, but the headline contrast is Experiment 2.

**Experiment 2 — comparison vs. studying the same two cases separately.** This is the key manipulation, because it holds the case content constant and varies only whether learners compared the two cases or studied them one at a time. "Participants in the comparison condition were over twice as likely (48% of participants) to transfer the principle to the test case as participants in the separate-cases condition (19%)," χ²(1, N=128)=11.85, p<.01. The advantage held for both principle types: for contingent contracts, 38% (comparison) vs 16% (separate); for trade-offs, 59% vs 22%. Comparison also reduced naive "split-the-difference" compromise solutions (61% comparison vs 83% separate). Schema quality mediated transfer: participants who, when describing the two study cases, articulated the underlying principle in full were the ones who transferred. So the same two cases yield roughly *double* the transfer when compared rather than read separately — exactly the analogical-encoding claim.

**Experiment 3 — live, face-to-face transfer.** The most ecologically demanding test. Participants trained by various methods then conducted an actual face-to-face negotiation in which a contingent contract was the efficient solution. The proportion of negotiating *pairs* who actually formed a contingent contract rose monotonically with the amount of comparison support: baseline .37 (7/19), separate-cases .55 (11/20), comparison .70 (14/20), and guided-analogy (the most comparison support) .90 (18/20). The two comparison-drawing groups formed reliably more contingency contracts than the non-comparison groups (χ²(1, N=79)=9.74, p<.01), with a reliable linear trend across levels of comparison support. Participants who had compared cases were also more likely to regard a contingency as the *ideal* settlement (≈68–73% vs ≈47–48%), indicating the schema shaped their judgment, not just their behavior under a cooperative partner. This experiment is what licenses the doc's phrase "cross-context transfer in professional training": the schema abstracted from written cases transferred to a live, dynamic, interactive negotiation — a genuinely different context.

**Correction (citation audit, 2026-10-07; GE1/GE2, substantive).** The prior framing — "replicated in professional training via case comparison" / "produces cross-context transfer in professional training, e.g. negotiation" — misstated the population of this specific paper. The ~2× transfer finding is real and correctly attributed to this 2003 paper (Experiment 2: "the comparison group showed a two-fold transfer advantage over the separate-cases group," printed p. 400), but the three experiments reported in *this* paper all recruited undergraduates, not professional trainees (see populations above). `background.html` §4 and its reference-list entry (§17) have been corrected to say "undergraduates" with a "two-fold transfer advantage" quote, and to note the earlier experienced-learner studies (MBA students, accountants, consultants, managers, executives) belong to Thompson et al. (2000), a different paper cited within this one's introduction as background, not to this paper's own data.

**Verdict:** Consistent on the core analogical-encoding result (case comparison roughly doubles transfer over studying the same cases separately: 48% vs. 19% in Experiment 2; the advantage carries into a live face-to-face negotiation in Experiment 3, .70/.90 vs. .55/.37). Not consistent on the population — corrected above from "professional training" to undergraduates.
