Backpropagation and the Curriculum

How a neural network learns from its mistakes, and whether the same idea could trace a shortfall in a program’s stated objectives back to the course where a change would close it.

Date 2026-11-25 Status Hypothesis; first test being set up Audience Faculty, curriculum committee
01

Why this document exists

The curriculum tool records how deeply each course develops each competency, on the Know, Understand and Do scales from 0 to 5. It also records which courses are prerequisites for which. A career target, such as Production & Operations, can state how deep each competency needs to be by graduation. When the courses on a student’s path do not reach that depth, the program has a shortfall.

Finding a shortfall is the easy part. The harder question is where to fix it. The last course on the path is the obvious place to look, but it may be short because students arrive underprepared from an earlier course. Neural networks face the same problem. When a network gives a wrong answer, which of its many internal settings should change? The answer, an algorithm called backpropagation, is one of the central ideas of modern machine learning.

This page explains that idea from the ground up for readers with no machine-learning background, then asks how far it carries over to a curriculum map. Part I covers how a network learns. Part II sets out the analogy and shows, on a small made-up example, how a shortfall could be traced back. Part III is an honest accounting of what the method needs, where the analogy breaks, and how the department plans to test it. The idea is listed as an open hypothesis in the KUD+ Background, §15. It is not part of the tool.

How sources are handled

Every cited claim comes from a source whose full text we hold, with the quoted passage and page recorded in the page’s citation ledger. A work we know of but do not hold in full carries a dagger (†). Worked examples and statements about the tool’s own data are ours and are marked as such.

Part I — How a neural network learns

02

A network is a chain of small steps

A neural network is built from simple units, often called neurons. Each one takes some numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds the results together with a constant called a bias, and passes the total through a simple function that decides what it sends on. Nielsen (2015) describes a unit that “has weights for each input” and “an overall bias,” whose output is a function of the weighted inputs plus the bias. The weights say how much each input matters. They are the network’s adjustable settings, and learning means changing them.

Units are arranged in layers. The first layer reads the input, such as the pixels of a photo, and each later layer reads the outputs of the one before. Layers in between are called hidden because they are neither the input nor the answer. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016, p. 164) explain that these networks “are called networks because they are typically represented by composing together many diﬀerent functions,” and that “these chain structures are the most commonly used structures of neural networks.” A network, in other words, is a long chain of small calculations, each feeding the next.

03

Measuring the error

To learn, a network needs examples where the right answer is known, and a way to score how wrong it is on each. That score is called the cost, loss or objective. LeCun, Bengio and Hinton (2015, p. 436) put it this way: “We compute an objective function that measures the error (or distance) between the output scores and the desired pattern of scores. The machine then modifies its internal adjustable parameters to reduce this error.” Nielsen’s version is a cost that “becomes small” precisely when the network’s outputs match the desired ones for all the training examples (Nielsen, 2015).

Learning therefore has a precise meaning: find the weights that make the cost as small as possible.

04

Stepping downhill

A network can have millions of weights, so trying combinations at random is hopeless. The standard approach, gradient descent, treats the cost as a landscape. Each weight is a direction you can move in, and the height is the error. From where you stand, you work out which way is downhill and take a small step that way, then repeat. Nielsen describes the procedure as repeatedly computing the slope “and then to move in the opposite direction, ‘falling down’ the slope of the valley,” so that repeated steps “roll down the hill” and “hopefully find a minimum of the cost function” (Nielsen, 2015).

The slope in every direction at once is called the gradient. LeCun and colleagues give the most useful plain definition: for each weight, the gradient “indicates by what amount the error would increase or decrease if the weight were increased by a tiny amount” (LeCun et al., 2015, p. 436). In other words, the gradient is a list of sensitivities, one per weight, saying how much the error responds to that weight.

05

Backpropagation: assigning the blame

Knowing the sensitivity of the error to a weight deep inside the network is the hard part. A weight in the first layer affects the answer only through every later layer. This is an old problem. Writing in 1961, Marvin Minsky called it the difficulty of “distributing credit for success of a complex strategy among the many decisions that were involved” (Minsky, 1961, p. 17), and named it the credit-assignment problem in a section heading (p. 20).

Backpropagation solves it for networks. A 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams describes a procedure that “repeatedly adjusts the weights of the connections in the network so as to minimize a measure of the difference between the actual output vector of the net and the desired output vector,” and in doing so the hidden units “come to represent important features of the task domain” (Rumelhart et al., 1986, p. 533; our held copy is a scan, and we have corrected its character-recognition errors in these quotations). The computation has a forward pass, which produces the answer, and a backward pass, “which propagates derivatives from the top layer back to the bottom one” (p. 534).

The backward pass rests on the chain rule from calculus. If the error depends on a unit’s output, and that output depends on a weight, the sensitivity of the error to the weight is the product of the two sensitivities along the way. Backpropagation applies this rule one layer at a time, starting from the error and working back. As LeCun and colleagues put it, the method “is nothing more than a practical application of the chain rule for derivatives” (LeCun et al., 2015, p. 438). Goodfellow and colleagues add that it is “an algorithm that computes the chain rule, with a speciﬁc order of operations that is highly eﬃcient” (Goodfellow et al., 2016, p. 201).

That efficiency is the point. One backward sweep yields the sensitivity of the error to every weight at once, however many there are. Researchers in automatic differentiation describe backpropagation as “only a special case” of a general technique called reverse-mode differentiation, which “propagates derivatives backward from a given output” and is much cheaper than the forward-mode alternative when there are many inputs and one output to explain (Baydin et al., 2018, pp. 12, 17).

One common confusion is worth heading off. Backpropagation does not do the learning by itself. It only computes the sensitivities. Another procedure, such as gradient descent, uses them to change the weights. Goodfellow and colleagues are explicit: “back-propagation refers only to the method for computing the gradient, while another algorithm, such as stochastic gradient descent, is used to perform learning using this gradient” (p. 200). This distinction matters in Part II, because the curriculum version keeps the first idea (tracing sensitivity backward) and drops the second (automatic learning).

06

A worked example

Here is the whole cycle on the smallest possible network: one input, one hidden unit and one output. The numbers are ours, chosen to keep the arithmetic easy.

ForwardInput x = 1. Hidden unit: weight w1 = 2, so it outputs h = 2 (it passes positive values unchanged). Output: weight w2 = 3, so y = 6.
ErrorThe right answer is 4. Using half the squared difference as the cost: ½ (6 − 4)² = 2.
BackwardError’s sensitivity to y: 6 − 4 = 2. To w2: 2 × h = 4. To h: 2 × w2 = 6. To w1: 6 × x = 6.
UpdateStep each weight against its sensitivity by 0.05 of it: w2 = 3 − 0.2 = 2.8, w1 = 2 − 0.3 = 1.7. Now y = 4.76 and the cost falls from 2 to about 0.29.

Notice what the backward pass did. It started from a single number, how wrong the output was, and handed each weight its share of the blame, including w1, which never touches the output directly. Its share came through w2, by the chain rule. That is the whole idea. Real networks repeat it over millions of weights and many thousands of examples.

Part II — The curriculum analogy

07

Matching the parts

A curriculum has a similar shape. Students move through a chain of courses. Each course takes what students bring in and sends them on with more. At the end, the program’s stated objectives say where graduates should be. The table matches the parts. The right-hand column is the hypothesis, not established practice.

Neural networkCurriculum map in this tool
Units and layersCourses, in prerequisite order
Connections between unitsPrerequisite links, tagged with the competency they carry
A unit’s outputThe depth (0–5 on Know, Understand, Do) students leave a course with
Desired outputThe career target’s required depth for each competency
ErrorThe shortfall: required depth minus the depth the path reaches
Gradient (sensitivity per weight)For each course, how much a one-level change would shrink the shortfall
BackpropagationTracing the shortfall back through the prerequisite chain
Gradient descent (automatic learning)No counterpart. Faculty decide what to change, informed by the trace.

The last row is the important one. In a network the trace drives an automatic update. In a curriculum it can only point. Changing a course is a decision for the people who teach it.

08

Curricula are already designed backward

Working backward from outcomes is not new in curriculum design. Wiggins and McTighe’s backward design asks planners to identify the desired results first, then decide what evidence would show them, and only then plan instruction. They “call this three-stage approach to planning ‘backward design’” and urge teachers to “first ‘think like an assessor’ before designing specific units and lessons” (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005, pp. 17–18). Biggs’s constructive alignment makes the related demand that teaching and assessment line up with the intended outcomes, which “are thus used to systematically align the teaching methods and the assessment” (Biggs, 1996, p. 347). The guidelines of a recent course-design method go further in the same direction: “start with the final learning outcomes,” then keep splitting skills into prerequisite skills until reaching skills “that the students should have already mastered” (Bijl, 2025, p. 3).

Treating a curriculum as a network is not new either. Aldrich (2014, p. 1) treats “the curriculum as a complex system with nodes representing courses and links between nodes the course prerequisites.” Heileman and colleagues’ curricular analytics measures that network’s shape. A course’s delay factor is “the number of vertices in the longest path” through it, and its blocking factor reflects that a gateway course, if failed, blocks students “from attempting many of the other courses” (Heileman et al., 2018, pp. 9, 11). They keep this structural complexity separate from instructional complexity and treat the two as “independent components” (p. 7). In other words, these measures describe the wiring of a program, not how much skill flows along it.

What the backpropagation idea would add is the combination: a backward trace that runs over the prerequisite network and carries skill depth, not just structure. A literature search for this project in November 2026 found no published work that does this. That is our own search, not a cited finding, and absence of evidence in a search is weak evidence.

09

Tracing a shortfall: a small example

The example below is made up, to show the mechanics. Three courses form a chain, A → B → C, and the career target requires students to graduate at Understand 4 on one competency. Each course’s profile says the depth students leave with, and each later course expects students to arrive at a certain depth.

The plain view finds no problem. The tool’s builder combines depths along a path by taking the highest level any course reaches, and competency levels do not fade. Course C reaches U4, so the requirement looks met.

The chain view does. Course A leaves students at U1, but Course B expects them at U2. If students who arrive underprepared get less out of a course, B delivers less than its profile shows, and so in turn does C. How much less is an assumption. This page calls it the gate, and the test tries three versions:

Gate assumptionWhat B deliversWhat C deliversReachedShortfall
No effect: courses deliver their profile regardlessU3U4U4none
One for one: one level less per level short on arrivalU2 (one short)U3 (one short)U31
Total: nothing above the arrival level when shortU1U1U13

Tracing it back. Now ask the backpropagation question: which one-level change to a course’s depth closes the gap? Under the one-for-one gate, raising any of the three courses by one level works. Raising A to U2 lets B and C deliver in full. Raising B or C makes up the lost level directly. Under the total gate, only raising A works, because B and C still receive underprepared students. So the one fix that holds under both assumptions is in Course A, the course with the lowest depth on the path. A view that looks only at which course reaches the highest level would never point there.

The example also shows the method’s weak spot. The answer depends on the gate, and under the first assumption there is nothing to fix. That is why the planned test asks faculty to say what each course expects students to arrive with, and counts a recommendation only if it survives more than one gate assumption.

10

Why what-ifs, not gradients

Backpropagation needs smooth slopes: a tiny change to a weight must produce a tiny, measurable change in the error. A curriculum map has neither. Depths are whole levels from 0 to 5, so there is no “tiny amount” to change. And the builder combines a path by taking the highest level any course reaches. With the highest-level rule, raising any course that is not already the highest changes nothing, so its slope is zero. Only the top course would ever get credit. That is our reading of the rule, illustrated by the plain view in §9, where Course A’s weakness is invisible.

The way around this is simpler than calculus. A curriculum is small compared with a neural network, so a computer can try every one-level change to each course’s depth, for every competency and dimension on a path, recompute the result, and record which changes close which shortfall. It can then list every smallest set of changes that closes a shortfall, rather than naming a single winner. This keeps the backward question that backpropagation answers (which upstream setting is responsible?) but answers it exactly, by trying each change, instead of with slopes.

Two checks guard against false confidence. Each proposed fix is rerun under all three gate assumptions. And each course’s depth is moved up or down one level, one at a time, to see whether the recommendation survives scoring noise. A fix that flips under these small changes is reported as fragile, not as an answer.

12

Where the analogy falls short

There is nothing to learn from

A neural network learns its weights from examples where the right answer is known, usually a great many. LeCun and colleagues note that a typical deep-learning system may have “hundreds of millions of labelled examples with which to train the machine” (LeCun et al., 2015, p. 436). Education models that do learn from student data use large datasets too. Deep knowledge tracing, which applies “flexible recurrent neural networks” to students’ exercise histories, was demonstrated on several datasets, the largest of them “1.4 million exercises completed by 47,495 students” (Piech et al., 2015, pp. 1, 6). A department has a few dozen courses and no measured record of each graduate’s depth per competency. The curriculum version is therefore a what-if calculator built on faculty judgments and capture scores, not a model that learns from outcomes.

Learned models do not reliably reveal separate skills

Even where enough data exists, a more complex learned model is not automatically better. When Bayesian knowledge tracing, a much simpler model, was given extra flexibility, it “achieves a level of performance indistinguishable from that of DKT” (Khajah et al., 2016, p. 1). Nor do a learned model’s internals necessarily mean what one hopes. One study concluded that “instead of tracking each skill through time, DKT is more likely to learn an ‘ability’ model” (Ding & Larson, 2021, p. 1). A curriculum trace that claims to say which course builds which skill should be wary of borrowing a learned model for that job.

Links may carry less than the map assumes

The trace assumes that what a student takes out of one course is available in the next. The research on transfer of learning warns that “very often the kinds of transfer hoped for do not occur” (Perkins & Salomon, 1992, p. 4 of 11), and distinguishes near transfer, “between very similar contexts,” from far transfer, between contexts that “seem remote and alien to one another.” Skills also fade. A meta-analysis of skill retention found “substantial skill loss with nonpractice or nonuse”; its abstract summarizes effect sizes ranging “from an effect size (d) of -0.01 immediately after training to a d of -1.4 after more than 365 days of nonuse” (Arthur et al., 1998, p. 57). The tool’s builder currently treats competency levels as never fading, a deliberate department choice. The first test keeps that choice, and an optional second run can apply a fading estimate.

Grade data measures effects on grades, not skill transfer

One could try to measure links from transcripts instead of asking faculty. Kaur, Polyzou and Karypis ask “If a student takes course X, will it cause them to do well in a subsequent course Y?” and estimate the answer with matching and regression on grades. They validated their results against “the existing prerequisite structure” and also “identify additional courses that do not appear in the prerequisite structure” (Kaur et al., 2019, p. 1). That is useful evidence about which courses matter, but it measures effects on grades, not the depth of a specific skill, which is what the trace needs.

The inputs are soft

Capture depths are scored from course materials and instructor interviews with AI help, and a one-level difference can be noise. Required depths and expected arrival depths will be faculty judgments. The robustness checks in §10 exist for this reason. A trace built on soft numbers should be read as “look here first,” never as a verdict.

Structure is not quality

A trace can say where a chain is weakest. It cannot say why, or whether the fix is worth its cost in time, staffing or other content. Curricular analytics draws the same line between a program’s structure and its instruction (Heileman et al., 2018, p. 7). Any change remains a curriculum decision.

13

How it will be tested

The department is setting up a small, honest test before building anything into the tool. It uses one career target, Production & Operations, seven competencies, and the courses on that target’s prerequisite path.

  1. Sealed predictions. Two faculty who teach on the path each write down, on their own and before seeing any results, which competencies they expect to fall short at graduation and which course they would change first and second.
  2. Worksheet. Each supplies the required depths and, for each prerequisite link, whether it really carries that competency and at what depth students should arrive. The two are kept separate, not averaged. Nothing they enter changes the live tool.
  3. Trace. A read-only script runs the what-ifs in §10 under all three gate assumptions and the one-level robustness checks.
  4. Compare. The faculty open their sealed predictions next to the results.

It counts as a success if the traced shortfalls largely match what faculty predicted, the leading fix holds across gate assumptions and small score changes, the gate changes the answer at least once compared with the plain highest-level view, and faculty find at least one result they did not predict but judge plausible. It counts as a failure if recommendations flip under small changes, if most shortfalls trace to uncaptured courses, or if faculty cannot tell the output from what the builder already shows. Either way, the result will be recorded and reported.

Two courses on the test path do not yet have a confirmed capture. Results that trace to them will be marked unverified.
