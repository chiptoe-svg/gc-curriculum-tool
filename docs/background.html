The KUD+ Framework

A theoretical and practical account of how the GC Curriculum Tool measures what a course is evidenced to require, assess, and support — scored on Know, Understand, and Do depth scales — and how those measurements feed program-level analysis and career-path alignment.

§ 1The problem this addresses

Curriculum review at most programs operates on long cycles — several years between accreditation reviews, longer for systemic redesign — and on coarse evidence: catalog descriptions, faculty self-report, and the occasional advisory-board conversation. Between reviews, a program's understanding of itself drifts. Courses evolve, faculty change, employer expectations shift, and the gap between what the curriculum claims to deliver and what its assignments actually produce widens without anyone tracking it.

The trouble turns concrete the moment you ask what a claim actually rests on. A syllabus bullet and an evidence-backed depth score answer very different questions:

Ordinary claim → what KUD+ asks for
Ordinary curriculum claimWhat KUD+ requires as evidence
“Covers color management”Do students actually diagnose or correct color under assessed, novel conditions? A Do score needs the graded artifact, not the topic in a list.
“Students understand branding”Where do students explain tradeoffs, defend choices, or revise from critique? An Understand score needs student-produced reasoning.
“Develops problem-solving”Where do upper-depth domain work, productive struggle, reflection, and transfer scaffolding appear across the sequence? (See §4.)

This is the framework's evidence-above-zero rule, and it is the heart of the approach: a course does not earn credit for a competency because the syllabus names it — it earns credit when assignments, rubrics, student-produced reasoning, or instructor-confirmed work show students actually engage it, and any depth above the lowest rung must point at that evidence. Everything that follows — the depth scales, the program views, the career alignment — exists to make that one rule usable at scale.

The GC Curriculum Tool proposes a continuous, evidence-backed alternative: each course described in commensurable terms, derived from its actual materials and confirmed by its instructor, available year-round to inform advising, accreditation, and revision. The unit of analysis is not what the syllabus claims a course will teach, but what the assignments and rubrics actually demand of students.

For that analysis to be useful, it must produce results that:

  • distinguish meaningfully between courses that touch a topic and courses that develop it,
  • survive review by other faculty without collapsing into subjective interpretation,
  • aggregate cleanly into program-level views (coverage matrices, scaffolding analysis, prerequisite checks), and
  • remain comprehensible to instructors who do not have time to learn a new technical vocabulary.

The framework described here, designated KUD+, is the working answer to those constraints. It begins with a well-established three-category outcome model (Know, Understand, Do) and extends each category with a 0–5 depth scale anchored to observable student attainment. The remainder of this document explains the theoretical basis for those choices, their practical implementation, and the limitations the approach acknowledges.

The choice of these three categories — rather than Bloom's six cognitive levels, the more familiar outcome vocabulary — is deliberate and load-bearing, and the reason is fit to purpose. Bloom's taxonomy classifies the cognitive demand of learning objectives, and its authors intend it for aligning objectives, activities and assessments within a unit, course or curriculum (Krathwohl, 2002). What it lacks for this tool's job, mapping a whole program against the careers it feeds, is a performance axis, and its levels (Remember, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) do not line up with how occupations are actually described. Careers are specified by the knowledge a role requires and the work it performs — which is exactly Know and Do, with Understand the transferable reasoning that carries either into an unfamiliar case. Because curriculum and career are then written on the same axes, comparing them becomes a same-axis comparison rather than a lossy translation — and, unlike Bloom, KUD carries the performance (Do) dimension where career readiness and habitual problem-solving are evidenced. The full argument, including where Bloom remains the better tool, is in §5.

Expressing courses, prerequisites, program coverage, and career demand all on the same K/U/D depth axes gives the framework a common metric — a single measurement currency in which those otherwise-incommensurable quantities become directly comparable. This commensurability is the load-bearing property the program-level and career-path analyses (§11–§12) depend on: it lets a prerequisite gap, a coverage roll-up, and a career-readiness claim be a same-axis comparison rather than a translation between mismatched vocabularies. The conversion is exact within the curriculum, which shares one taxonomy, and explicitly partial into external occupational frameworks, where the alignment is many-to-many and match-quality-stamped (§11).

§ 2Theoretical grounding

The framework is consistent with several established traditions in curriculum design. None of them prescribe the specific depth scales used here; the language below describes how the framework is consistent with each tradition, not derived from any of them. The specific design choices — 0–5 grain, student-side anchors, the evidence-typed scoring rule, the null-vs-zero treatment of foundationals — are framework commitments that go beyond what the cited traditions individually establish, and are presented as such throughout this document.

Backward Design (Wiggins & McTighe)

Understanding by Design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005, pp. 17–18) defines a three-stage planning sequence: identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, plan learning experiences and instruction. The KUD categories give outcomes a typed structure that fits Stage 1's "desired results"; the framework's evidence rule — depth values above zero require excerpts of student-produced work — shares Stage 2's structural commitment that outcome claims must be backed by demonstrable evidence. The student-side anchoring of the depth scales is consistent with that commitment. Wiggins and McTighe do not themselves prescribe a 0–5 numerical depth scale on each of K, U, D; that operationalization is this framework's, taken in a direction the original Stage 2 is hospitable to but does not require.

Constructive Alignment (Biggs)

Biggs's principle of constructive alignment (Biggs, 1996) requires that intended outcomes, teaching and learning activities, and assessment all support one another. The framework's per-course audit conversation is shaped by this principle: mismatches between stated objectives and material evidence become first-class findings rather than silent inconsistencies. The audit conversation is one specific way to apply Biggs's principle, not the only one.

Bloom's Taxonomy (in support, not in place)

The revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)† remains the right vocabulary for analyzing the cognitive demand of an assignment. The framework does not replace Bloom's verbs; it employs them inside the audit conversation, where the auditor asks an instructor which Bloom level a high-stakes assignment most closely matches (remembering vs. applying vs. creating). Bloom's authors intend it more broadly too, as a means of checking “the congruence of educational objectives, activities, and assessments in a unit, course, or curriculum” (Krathwohl, 2002, p. 212). The discriminator between Bloom and KUD is therefore not scope but axes: both of Bloom's dimensions (knowledge and cognitive process) are cognitive, while KUD adds a performance dimension and is written in the vocabulary careers use. The revised Bloom's also includes a Knowledge Dimension (Factual / Conceptual / Procedural / Metacognitive) that partially overlaps with K and U; the framework chooses its three-category structure over the Knowledge Dimension because the K/U/D distinction is more legible to non-specialist faculty audiences and because the Do dimension has no counterpart in A&K's Knowledge Dimension — and that gap is not incidental: the Do axis is where both career performance (work activities and tasks) and the development of habitual problem-solving are evidenced, so it is precisely the dimension a curriculum-to-career tool cannot do without.

Competency-Based Education

Competency-based frameworks define learning as the demonstrated mastery of specified competencies and often tie those competencies to external (employer) expectations. The accreditation-policy formulation is summarized in (C-RAC, 2015); for a fuller theoretical account of the CBE tradition see Spady (1988, 1994)†. KUD+ shares the mastery orientation in its depth scales — the top step on Do (creative judgment, ability to guide others) describes a near-professional standard — and shares the external-alignment orientation through the planned career-path layer described in §11. The framework departs from strict CBE in two ways: it does not propose self-paced credit (it maps the existing time-based curriculum), and it treats program-level mapping as the primary deliverable rather than individual transcripts of demonstrated competencies. Less-strict CBE models that operate inside conventional credit-hour structures share more design choices with this framework than strict CBE does.

Integrative Learning

AAC&U's Integrative Learning VALUE Rubric (AAC&U) describes a progression from connecting prior knowledge across contexts to synthesizing learning in complex, real-world performances. The KUD+ Do scale's D=4 anchor (adapts to new constraints) is consistent with the rubric's Capstone descriptor for transfer: “Adapts and applies, independently, skills, abilities, theories, or methodologies gained in one situation to new situations to solve difficult problems or explore complex issues in original ways” (p. 2). D=5's guiding of others goes beyond the rubric, whose top level is Capstone; it is the framework's own addition. The framework borrows the integrative-learning orientation rather than the rubric's structure: the VALUE Rubric is designed for assessing student work via portfolios across multiple dimensions, while the Do scale describes one dimension of what a course is designed to develop.

Knowledge spaces: the formal cousin of the “skill tree”

A recurring reaction from people seeing the tool for the first time is that it looks like a video-game skill tree. The reaction is apt, and worth reframing. The structure the tool maintains — a directed graph of skill-tagged prerequisite edges (§12) in which each competency carries a graduated 0–5 depth — is the kind of object Knowledge Space Theory formalizes (Doignon & Falmagne, 1985; Falmagne & Doignon, 2011†; Doignon & Falmagne, 2015): a domain of items linked by a surmise (prerequisite-like) relation, where a learner's “knowledge state” is the set of items they have mastered and the outer fringe of that state is the set of items they are now ready to learn (Doignon & Falmagne, 2015, Definition 20, p. 15: the outer fringe “tells us exactly what the student is ready to learn,” p. 16). This is not academic exotica — it is what the ALEKS adaptive-learning system deploys to millions of students (Falmagne, Cosyn, Doignon & Thiéry, 2006†; Doignon & Falmagne, 2015, p. 1), and the same prerequisite-graph structure recurs in K–12 learning-map systems such as the Dynamic Learning Maps project (Kingston & Broaddus, 2017), in the learning-progressions literature, in the mastery-tracking models of intelligent tutoring systems, and in the game-design “skill tree” itself. The framework was not built from any of it; it arrived at a knowledge-space-shaped structure from the two driving questions and the evidence rule alone — mild corroboration that the structure is a natural one. Two refinements the literature lends: the depth scale is what separates this from a binary unlock map (a competency is developed to a degree, not simply owned), and KST's outer fringe — the set of competencies a learner is now ready for — is precisely the computation a future advising view requires.

The metaphor is a good servant and a bad master, and three differences are load-bearing. First, a game skill tree is deterministic, closed, and fully authored — the designer decrees that node B unlocks when node A is bought. Curricular competence is none of these: it is probabilistic, contested, and known only through evidence. The tool's whole discipline — the evidence-above-zero rule (§7), the basis stamp that marks a prerequisite edge as measured, intended, or no-data (§12), the refusal to score an unaudited node zero — exists precisely to refuse to draw a crisp edge where there is no evidence. It builds the fuzzy, evidential knowledge space, not the crisp game one. Second, unlocking a node is not the same as having the skill: passing a prerequisite course does not deposit the competence in the student, which is exactly why attainment is scored from assessed evidence rather than from enrollment — the completion-is-not-acquisition distinction the knowledge-tracing literature exists to measure. Third, and most important, problem-solving is not a node: the framework treats it as a program-level emergent property of depth × productive failure × reflection (§4), the opposite of a discrete unlockable. Anyone taking the skill-tree metaphor literally would add a “problem-solving” node and gate it — the precise error §4 is written to prevent. The situated-learning critique that the 3-Act discussion (§9) already engages cuts the same way: competence is bound to its context of use, not a portable token that travels up the tree intact.

§ 3Why not other frameworks

Several alternative frameworks deserve specific mention, both for completeness and because the choice not to use them clarifies what KUD+ is trying to accomplish.

The framework's design choice — three outcome categories with depth scales — is therefore not a rejection of these traditions but a selection among them, with explicit attention to which one is well-suited for the program-mapping task and which one is well-suited for the assessment-design task.

§ 4Problem-solving as a program-level emergent property

Problem-solving is among the attributes employers most consistently value when reviewing graduates' resumes — repeatedly ranked near the top of NACE's annual Job Outlook resume-attribute surveys, alongside teamwork and written communication (NACE, 2023)†. The natural curricular response is to add a "Problem-Solving" course, or to list problem-solving as a generic transferable skill the program develops. Both responses tend to underperform, in characteristic ways the cognitive-science research below explains, and the framework is deliberately structured to surface why and to support the alternative.

What problem-solving is, in this framework — stated before the critique

Problem-solving is real, the framework tracks it, and it is not removed from the curriculum map — it is relocated. Rather than a generic course or a sixth foundational checkbox, the framework treats it as a program-level emergent property: the signal appears in the pattern across courses, not in any single course's label. What the tool actually looks for is a combination of conditions:

Problem-solving opportunity = upper-depth domain work (U5 / D4+) + productive failure + structured reflection + abstraction/bridging across varied cases — read at the program level, not the course level.

This is what the framework looks for, not a computed score. “Emergent” does not mean invisible: it means the tool measures the conditions under which problem-solving forms — enough domain depth to attempt meaningful solutions, struggle that exposes gaps, reflection that converts failure into understanding, and repeated chances to abstract principles across varied cases. The critique that follows — why a generic “Problem-Solving 101” underperforms — therefore lands on a reader who already holds the replacement.

One distinction up front dissolves most of the apparent contradiction below. “Problem-solving,” as employers use the word, blends at least three things: technical-diagnostic troubleshooting (“why is this plate scumming?”), adaptive transfer to unfamiliar constraints, and professional problem-framing (clarifying goals, stakeholders, tradeoffs). The transfer literature is pessimistic about the second as a trained generic skill, but not about the first in-domain or the third as a disposition — which is how “far transfer is rare” and “deep domains build problem-solvers” are both true. The framework is strongest on the first two and keeps the third visible through the foundational dispositions (§8); the full treatment is in the companion problem-solving deep dive.

Why generic problem-solving instruction doesn't transfer

Four decades of cognitive science converge on the position that problem-solving competence is not a free-floating general skill that can be taught independently of subject matter. Willingham's widely-cited synthesis is unambiguous: "the processes of thinking are intertwined with the content of thought" (Willingham, 2007). He shows that even expert scientists fail at critical thinking when reasoning outside their familiar territory, and concludes that "it also makes no sense to try to teach critical thinking devoid of factual content." McPeck made the strong-specificity case earlier and more sharply: "there are no general thinking skills, since thinking is always thinking about some subject-matter" (McPeck, 1981)†. His later reply to Ennis is less absolute than that line suggests: “I do not assume that there are no general thinking skills as Ennis asserts—I argue the point. For what it is worth, I believe there are, in fact, some very limited general thinking skills” (McPeck, 1990, p. 12) — while adding that “these skills offer little to get excited about.” A large meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction (341 effect sizes on generic critical-thinking skills) is consistent with this position without settling it: critical-thinking instruction does produce reliable gains, content-specific CT outcomes are roughly twice as large as generic ones (g+ ≈ 0.57 vs. 0.30), and the promising instructional ingredients it identifies are discussion, authentic situations, and mentorship (Abrami et al., 2015). It does not show that standalone instruction fails: the four course types it compared (general, infusion, immersion, and mixed) all produced positive effects and did not differ significantly from one another (p = .25).

The empirical bedrock is the expert–novice literature, beginning with Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser's landmark 1981 study of physics problem-solving (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981). Experts and novices were given the same set of physics problems and asked to sort them. Novices sorted by surface features (the problem mentions a pulley, an inclined plane); experts sorted by underlying principles (conservation of energy, Newton's second law). Larkin and colleagues reported related findings a year earlier: in their models, skilled solvers work forward from the known quantities (“knowledge development”), while “many novice solvers” work backward from the quantity to be found. Some novices also worked forward in places, and the authors tie the difference to novices having “little knowledge of physics, but much more of algebra” (Larkin, McDermott, Simon, & Simon, 1980). The implication is structural: problem-solving skill in physics is not separable from physics knowledge — it is restructured domain knowledge organized around principles rather than surface features.

Sweller's cognitive load theory provides the mechanistic account (Sweller, 1988). Conventional problem-solving (working backward from a goal by means-ends search) imposes a cognitive load that may “leave little for schema acquisition, even if the problem is solved” — and schema acquisition, he argues, is “possibly the most important component of problem solving expertise.” Sweller's own remedy was to replace much conventional problem-solving practice with worked examples and nonspecific-goal problems during learning, and to reserve conventional problems “for tests and perhaps as a motivational device.” This framework draws a sequencing conclusion of its own that Sweller does not state: worked examples and direct instruction early, when there is no schema base to activate, and open-ended problems later, once enough domain knowledge exists for the struggle to produce learning (the productive-failure research below qualifies that order). Treating "Problem-Solving" as a generic course unit, decoupled from a specific domain in which schemas have been built, gives students the cognitive load without the schema acquisition.

The actual mechanism: depth → productive failure → reflection

The research converges on a specific developmental sequence. Problem-solving competence develops through repeated cycles of three things in a specific domain: building enough domain depth to make solution attempts meaningful, attempting solutions that fail in informative ways, and structured reflection that converts the failure into transferable understanding.

Productive failure — Manu Kapur's program of research — has identified a well-evidenced design pattern for this (Kapur, 2008) (Kapur, 2016). The unit of instruction is two-phase: students work on an ill-structured problem with their current knowledge, fail in various characteristic ways, and the consolidation phase uses those failure modes as scaffolding for the canonical approach. The 2021 meta-analytic review found an overall effect favoring problem-solving followed by instruction for conceptual knowledge and transfer (g ≈ 0.36 across 53 studies, 166 comparisons), with no significant advantage for procedural knowledge (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). The effect is larger when designs follow productive-failure principles closely: the review's overall fidelity score predicted effect size, and instruction that builds on the students' own solutions was among its strongest single predictors. Kapur (2016) explicitly distinguishes productive from unproductive failure: unguided problem-solving with no later consolidation is the design that, in his words, can “maximize neither performance nor learning,” and he cites agreement across camps that unguided discovery “often leads to dismal learning outcomes.” The mechanism is not failure as such; it is failure followed by sense-making.

Desirable difficulties — Robert Bjork's framework — generalizes the finding to a broader principle: conditions that suppress short-term performance often improve long-term retention and transfer (Bjork, 1994). Spaced practice, interleaving, retrieval testing, reduced feedback — all degrade in-the-moment performance and all dramatically improve learning that lasts. This is the empirical basis for the framework's commitment to evidence-of-attainment over evidence-of-coverage: a course that produces smooth performance during instruction but cannot demonstrate retention or transfer is producing the wrong thing.

Reflection — Donald Schön's reflective-practitioner work — established the theoretical case that professional expertise develops through cycles of reflection-in-action (adjusting during practice) and reflection-on-action (retrospective analysis) (Schön, 1983)†. Kolb's experiential learning cycle made the operational version explicit: concrete experience → reflective observation → abstract conceptualization → active experimentation (Kolb, 1984)†. The 2013 meta-analysis of after-action reviews in professional contexts quantifies the effect: structured debriefs improve subsequent performance by approximately 25%, with effect sizes consistent across teams and individuals, simulated and real settings (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli, 2013). What the meta-analysis treats as defining a debrief is specific: active self-learning rather than passive receipt of feedback, a developmental rather than judgmental intent, reflection on specific events rather than general strengths and weaknesses, and input from more than one source (their Table 1). How soon the review follows the event is, in their words, a moderator “not adequately covered in prior research.”

How the KUD+ framework encodes this

The framework's depth scales were designed in part to make problem-solving development legible at the curriculum-mapping level. Look at the upper-range anchors:

  • Do — 4: Adapts performance to new conditions or constraints.
  • Do — 5: Performs creatively with critical judgment; can guide others.
  • Understand — 4: Reasons through novel cases not previously seen.
  • Understand — 5: Critiques the principle, identifies limits, extends to new domains.

These are not abstract scale-tops; they are explicit definitions of problem-solving facets within a domain. D-4 names transfer to novel conditions — exactly the capacity Perkins & Salomon's transfer literature identifies as the hard problem of education (Perkins & Salomon, 1992). D-5 names creative judgment and the ability to guide others — this framework's own marker for the top of the scale, not Chi's. Chi's markers of principle-level rather than surface-level understanding are different: sorting problems by the underlying principle, and solution knowledge stored with “explicit conditions for applicability.” U-4 and U-5 name reasoning under novelty and the ability to critique principles, which together describe the metacognitive habits that the moderate-generalist position in the critical-thinking literature treats as the most cross-domain-portable component of problem-solving.

Treated this way, the framework already encodes problem-solving competence — but at the right scale (depth within a domain) rather than the wrong one (a generic transferable skill). When a curriculum analysis asks "does this program produce problem-solvers in graphic communications?", the right empirical question becomes: does the program develop technical competencies to D=4 and D=5 in the domains the career destinations require, with productive-failure and reflection scaffolding in the courses that get students there? The framework's data shape is built to surface exactly those conditions; whether they translate into stronger graduate problem-solving is the validation question, not a settled output of the tool.

What actually transfers, and how

A common and reasonable faculty intuition is that developing deep problem-solving in one area — and the habit of applying it — lets students problem-solve elsewhere. This is not in tension with the framework; it is the framework's position, with the conditions made explicit. The transfer literature sharpens it three ways.

What transfers is readiness to learn, not cold skill. Bransford and Schwartz's "preparation for future learning" reframes the usual transfer test: rather than dropping a person into a new domain to see whether prior skill fires unaided, measure how fast they learn the new domain (Bransford & Schwartz, 1999) (Schwartz & Martin, 2004). Deep prior problem-solving shows up clearly on that test: prepared learners extract more from each new learning opportunity. The honest mechanism behind the faculty intuition is usually not "I already knew the new thing" but "I learned it faster, because I knew what understanding-through-struggle feels like and what to look for."

The carrying mechanism is abstraction across varied cases plus deliberate bridging — and one rich domain can supply it. What travels is an abstract schema, and schemas form when learners compare multiple surface-different cases and articulate the shared structure: transfer from a single case is poor — about a 10% base rate, rising only to ~20–30% when learners must spontaneously notice the analogy unaided (an explicit hint lifts single-case transfer far higher, to ~75–90%) — but comparing two surface-different cases to extract the shared principle roughly doubles spontaneous transfer, to ~45–50% (Gick & Holyoak, 1980, 1983). The comparison effect was replicated with undergraduates learning negotiation strategies, where comparing two cases gave a “two-fold transfer advantage” (Gentner, Loewenstein, & Thompson, 2003); the same authors report earlier studies with experienced learners such as MBA students, consultants and managers. Perkins and Salomon distinguish low-road transfer (surface similarity, lots of varied practice) from high-road transfer (mindful abstraction of the principle, deliberate bridging to new contexts), and caution that transfer does not follow on its own: a teacher “uninformed about the difficulties of transfer” “might presume that students would spontaneously make some illuminating connections and might be puzzled when, for the most part, they do not” (Salomon & Perkins, 1989). The curriculum consequence: a single deep domain produces transferable problem-solving only when it is taught as a stream of surface-varied instances of one deep structure, with that structure explicitly abstracted and bridged outward — GC's diagnostic troubleshooting (many faults sharing one diagnostic logic) is exactly such a stream; narrow drilling on one instance type is not.

Skill transfers poorly; disposition and readiness transfer better. The claim the evidence rejects most firmly is that cognitive skill itself generalizes across distant domains — a skepticism that runs from Thorndike and Woodworth's founding identical-elements experiments (Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901) to the modern verdict that broad spontaneous transfer is largely an artifact of how experiments are designed (Detterman, 1993). Meta-analyses of chess, music, and working-memory training find far transfer near zero after bias correction (Sala & Gobet, 2017) (Sala et al., 2019), and a far-transfer taxonomy explains why distant transfer is rare (Barnett & Ceci, 2002). What the framework claims transfers is the narrower, better-supported thing: the disposition and readiness a well-designed cycle of productive failure and reflection builds, plus the adaptive (rather than routine) form of expertise that understanding-why and varied practice produce (Hatano & Inagaki, 1986). One implication for the audit is now built in: the active transfer ingredient — abstracting across varied cases and bridging to a new context — is a CourseCapture probe, the sixth Area-7 condition (abstraction-and-bridging), evidence-gated like the K/U/D scores (a rating above "absent" requires a graded artifact showing it); on legacy snapshots a missing value means "not assessed," not "absent."

Why this is not a foundational competency

The framework treats five dispositions as foundational program-wide threads — Agency, Attention to Detail, Resilience, Curiosity, Communication — and the natural question is whether Problem-Solving should join the list. The literature reviewed above is the reason it does not. Foundational competencies are scored on Do only because they are dispositions demonstrated through behavior; a student exhibits Resilience or doesn't, and the question per course is whether the conditions to develop the disposition were present. Problem-solving is structurally different: it is not a disposition that lives in every domain in the same form, and it cannot be evidenced by behavior in the absence of domain content. A student who "exhibits problem-solving" in a course where they have K=1, U=0 is exhibiting flailing — not problem-solving. The disposition framework would mis-encode the construct, and the program-level signal would be polluted by every-course D=1-or-2 inflations that do not in fact contribute to problem-solving development.

The alternative — and the position this framework takes — is that problem-solving is a program-level emergent property. No single course produces a problem-solver. The program produces problem-solvers when its sequence of courses develops technical competencies to upper-range depths in the right domains and creates productive-failure conditions in the courses that get students there and builds reflection habits across the sequence. The right scale to evaluate problem-solving is the program, not the course.

What the framework does about it

Two operational decisions follow.

First, the CourseCapture audit explicitly probes for productive-failure and reflection conditions in every course. Live Audit Area 7 of the capture chat surfaces, per course: whether any assignment requires students to attempt solutions before being taught the canonical method (Kapur's generate-then-consolidate structure); whether the course assigns open-ended ill-structured problems; whether revision cycles respond to specific identified failures or are merely point-recovery iterations; whether structured post-mortems tie reflection to concrete failure modes; and the maximum domain depth the course reaches in its strongest technical competencies. These are graded findings — present, partial, or absent for the qualitative conditions, and a numeric depth for the supporting depth — surfaced into the snapshot's audit notes alongside prerequisite gaps and objective misalignments. They feed program-level analyses without distorting the K/U/D scoring of competencies.

Second, the program-level views read these findings together with the depth distribution to produce a problem-solving opportunity/risk read — the conditions for formation, not a validated measure that it occurred. The Program Coverage Matrix Live (faculty trial) surfaces where the curriculum reaches D-4 and D-5 against each career target's sub-competencies — the depth surface where problem-solving lives. The Scaffolding Analysis Live (Stage 1) reads the sequence of productive-failure conditions across courses, asking whether the curriculum's pedagogical scaffolding matches the depth scaffolding: are productive-failure experiences placed in courses where prior coursework has built enough K/U/D base for the failure to be productive, and is reflection scaffolding present in the upper-division courses where the cumulative pattern should consolidate?

One caveat these maps carry — and a roadmap it opens. A depth cell records the depth a course reaches at the moment it is taught, not the durable depth a student carries to graduation. Skill decays without practice: a meta-analysis summarizes the loss in its abstract as ranging from d = −0.01 immediately after training to d = −1.4 after more than a year of non-use, across all task types, with cognitive tasks decaying more than physical ones (Arthur et al., 1998). In a 50-year study of school Spanish, retention fell for the first 3–6 years and then held steady for up to 30 years; how much reached this stable “permastore” depended on the level of training and the grades earned, through what the author calls “a discrete transition” during training (Bahrick, 1984, pp. 1, 23–25). Learners with the least training and lowest grades kept almost none of their recall vocabulary in permastore (0% for one-level, C-grade learners), while more training and higher grades put a growing share there (p. 23). So a D-5 reached freshman year and never revisited is not a D-5 at graduation, and the “unproductive success” quadrant of Figure 1 is doubly cautionary: depth from massed, single-context drilling is both brittle to novelty and the fastest-decaying kind. The opportunity map should be read as opportunity at the moment of the course, reinforced or not by the rest of the sequence. The same repeated-snapshot design that builds the map is, in principle, what could eventually estimate that decay — turning the caveat into a measurable durability model rather than a standing asterisk.

Deeper treatment

This section summarizes the framework's position. The full research synthesis — including the Kapur 2×2 productive/unproductive framework, the Sinha & Kapur 2021 finding that the productive-failure advantage is outcome-dependent, with no significant advantage for procedural knowledge, and the framework's caution about applying the paradigm to domain-general dispositions or design judgment, the operational chain from cognitive science to audit probe to program-level opportunity read, and the framework's commitment to function as its own research instrument — is in the companion document Problem-Solving as a Program-Level Emergent Property: A Research Deep Dive.

Caveats and where the research is honestly thin

Five honest acknowledgments. First, the literature does not provide a clean threshold for "how much domain depth before productive failure becomes productive rather than unproductive." The working assumption is that students need enough prior knowledge to generate meaningful attempts; no study we hold names a specific depth. In one pair of studies, students from a high- and a medium-achieving school both learned from productive failure, and the variety of student-generated solutions (inventive production) was a stronger predictor of learning than prior math achievement (Kapur, Saba, & Roll, 2023). The two schools had similar topic-specific prerequisite knowledge, so the study does not show what happens without it, and the authors caution that the findings “may not extend to the lower end of the spectrum.” The framework accordingly treats this as degrees-not-thresholds: lower-depth cycles contribute less per cycle than higher-depth ones; the scaffolding analysis weights contributions by depth rather than gating them at a threshold.

Second, longitudinal effects across a four-year curriculum are essentially unstudied. Most productive-failure research measures outcomes weeks or months out, not years. The framework's claim that cumulative productive-failure exposure across the degree produces better problem-solvers four years later is theoretically coherent and consistent with the deliberate-practice literature (Ericsson, Krampe, & Tesch-Römer, 1993) — but it is an extrapolation, not an empirically settled finding. Macnamara and colleagues' critiques further note that practice time alone explains only a fraction of expertise variance (Macnamara, Hambrick, & Oswald, 2014), which is why the framework requires the productive-failure and reflection conditions, not raw cycle counts.

Third, the productive-failure evidence base is concentrated in mathematics and physics: nearly 75% of the comparisons in the 2021 meta-analysis, whose authors say more studies are needed in other STEM and non-STEM domains “before we can generalize the effectiveness of PS-I” (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). The GC curriculum's core is the part of the program closest to that evidence: press operation, ink, plates, prepress and file diagnostics, color management, finishing — operations with canonical correct solutions and concrete, measurable failure modes. Productive failure is a plausible analog for these canonical technical modules — strongest where assignments target conceptual troubleshooting rather than procedural fluency, since the PS-I evidence is for conceptual knowledge and transfer, not procedural skill (Sinha & Kapur, 2021). A smaller portion of the program reaches design-judgment territory where multiple defensible solutions coexist (typography, layout, brand identity); for those modules productive struggle — critique, iteration, revision, reflective practice in the Schön tradition — is the more defensible framing. Program-level cumulative claims about how repeated cycles build dispositional habits remain framework hypotheses awaiting the longitudinal evidence the graduate-outcome study is designed to produce.

Fourth, the metacognition / dispositional question is better described today as a qualified yes under conditions than as fully contested. Metacognitive- and self-regulated-learning-strategy instruction shows reliable cross-domain effects in meta-analysis (Donker et al., 2014) — though in mathematics, Schoenfeld’s synthesis reports Lester and colleagues’ conclusion that metacognition instruction “is most effective when it takes place in a domain specific context” (Schoenfeld, 1992), and whether metacognitive skill is general or domain-specific is still unresolved (studies across tasks and domains “yielded inconclusive results”); most students “spontaneously pick up” some of it, and explicit instruction, best embedded in the content matter, is of particular value to those who do not (Veenman, Van Hout-Wolters, & Afflerbach, 2006) — and the preparation-for-future-learning and adaptive-expertise constructs give the cross-domain readiness claim a real evidence base. The load-bearing caveat is that none of this transfer is automatic — it appears only when the habit is explicitly cultivated (varied cases, abstraction, deliberate bridging, reflective control) and largely fails to appear from depth or content mastery alone. The framework's moderate-generalist position: it structures the data shape around domain-bounded K/U/D and treats the cross-domain habit as emergent from the cumulative pattern given those abstraction-and-bridging conditions — and it would tell us if it were wrong, since a program that satisfies the structural conditions and still produces weak problem-solvers is evidence the account is incomplete.

Fifth, the framework's founding intuition is itself partly grounded in personal experience of transfer, and personal experience is a trustworthy guide to the phenomenon but an unreliable guide to the mechanism. Introspective reports of one's own reasoning are reconstructed after the fact, not read off directly (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977), so "I used a general problem-solving skill" can be the feeling of deploying domain knowledge one cannot observe oneself using; selection confounds the inference (people who master one domain and succeed elsewhere are disproportionately high in general ability and conscientiousness, which independently predict both); and survivorship hides those who went deep and did not transfer. None of this makes the intuition wrong — it means the design must specify the conditions that reproduce the effect in a median student, which is why the framework locates the transferable element in cultivated disposition, readiness, and abstraction-and-bridging rather than in depth as such.

The position the framework takes, stated plainly: problem-solving competence is real, valued by employers, and consistently named among the attributes employers most want. The literature does not support treating it as a free-floating skill that transfers reliably without domain knowledge and explicit transfer scaffolding: far transfer is difficult, domain knowledge is required, and generic problem-solving instruction is weak. Problem-solving is the cumulative product of depth + productive struggle or failure + reflection, and the curriculum-mapping tool that wants to make it visible must surface each of those components at the right scale — the depths at the cell level, the conditions at the course level, the cumulative pattern at the program level. The framework is built to do that.

§ 5The KUD model

The framework adopts the three-category Know / Understand / Do structure as a planning vocabulary; Tomlinson's work on differentiated instruction (Tomlinson, 1999, pp. 40, 42–43) uses this same structure as a categorical pre-unit planning aid (“During planning, a teacher should generate specific lists of what students should know (facts), understand (concepts and principles), and be able to do (skills) by the time the unit ends”), and Wiggins & McTighe's Understanding by Design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005, p. 22, Figure 1.2) uses a structurally similar separation of desired understandings, knowledge, and skills in Stage 1 of backward design (“Students will understand that . . .” / “Students will know . . .” / “Students will be able to . . .”). Neither Tomlinson nor Wiggins/McTighe attach numerical depth scales to the categories; the depth extension introduced in §6 is this framework's contribution. The three categories are intended to separate what is often conflated in outcome statements:

  • Know. Facts, terms, conventions, vocabulary, procedural steps — content the student can recall, recognize, or reproduce. The cognitive operation is retrieval.
  • Understand. Principles, relationships, consequences, transferable ideas — the why behind the content. The cognitive operation is reasoning.
  • Do. Skills, procedures, performances, produced artifacts — what the student can execute or demonstrate. The cognitive operation is behavioral output.

The framework is widely deployed in outcome-writing guidance at the K–12 and undergraduate levels, where its three categories are valued for being faculty-readable without sacrificing the discrimination needed to distinguish a course that teaches definitions from one that teaches reasoning from one that teaches performance.

Why not Bloom: the decision is the goal, not the grain

The choice between KUD and Bloom is settled by what the framework is for. Its job is to trace a competency from one course to the next, through an entire curriculum, and into a career destination — a single continuous chain expressed on one set of axes. That goal — not a preference for fewer categories — is what rules Bloom out and KUD in. Bloom's taxonomy (Bloom, 1956†, whose six categories were Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis and Evaluation; revised by Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001† as Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) classifies the cognitive demand of objectives. Its authors intend it for alignment within a unit, course or curriculum as well as for single assessments (Krathwohl, 2002, pp. 212–213, 218), so the case against it here is not about scale. It is the wrong instrument for an end-to-end, curriculum-to-career mapping for two reasons, neither of which is about how many categories there are.

First, vocabulary fit. Careers are not written in Bloom's cognitive-process vocabulary, and the career side is half of the mapping. The three KUD categories are the native vocabulary of career competence — the destination this framework maps toward. Occupational models describe a role at the domain tier as required knowledge, work activities, and tasks — the tier this framework already treats as legitimate demand-side validation (see the occupational-frameworks discussion), as against the context-free “generic-skill” tier §4 rejects. KUD is near-isomorphic to that domain tier: Know ↔ required knowledge, Do ↔ work activities and tasks, Understand ↔ the transferable reasoning that carries either into an unfamiliar case. Mapping curriculum coverage onto a career destination is therefore a same-axis comparison rather than a translation. Bloom's six cognitive-process levels align with neither side of that mapping: built to classify objectives and the tasks that assess them, they force “Analyze vs. Evaluate vs. Create” onto “is this graduate prepared for the role,” a lossy translation at exactly the supply-meets-demand seam the framework exists to measure.

Second, the Do axis — and with it, a way to measure where problem-solving can develop. The Do dimension is load-bearing, and Bloom cannot accommodate it. This framework treats problem-solving not as a generic skill but as a program-level emergent property, built through repeated cycles of depth × productive failure × reflection (§4) — and the KUD+ depth scale is precisely the instrument that makes the opportunity for that development visible and measurable. The upper Do and Understand anchors (D-4's transfer to novel conditions, D-5's creative judgment and ability to guide others, U-5's critique and extension) mark exactly the conditions under which problem-solving forms, so the tool can locate — course by course and across the sequence — where those conditions are present, thin, or absent. That is a measurement Bloom by itself simply cannot give you: its hierarchy is purely cognitive, with no behavioral axis on which a habit of deployment-under-difficulty could be observed accumulating — and its “higher-order thinking” framing invites the very category error §4 is written to prevent, treating problem-solving as a cognitive level a single task can certify rather than a disposition built by doing, over time, across courses. Bloom can label whether one assignment analyzes or creates; it cannot tell you whether the curriculum gives students the repeated, deepening opportunities to become problem-solvers. KUD+ is built to measure exactly that.

Granularity is a side note, not the reason — though it is the point usually raised first. Bloom is also finer-grained than program mapping needs: across the hundreds of (course × competency × dimension) cells a program audit must score and faculty must be able to dispute, a finer rubric shifts disagreement onto category boundaries (Apply vs. Analyze, Evaluate vs. Create) rather than the substantive question of whether the course develops the competency at all. (The EU Tuning project's degree profiles use a comparably compact competency set for the same reason, distinguishing holistic competence from the level achieved.) But grain is the lesser issue; fit to the goal is the decisive one. Bloom remains the right lens for assessment design at the course level — its verbs continue to inform the prompts the auditor uses to probe each cell.

The three KUD categories give faculty enough resolution to express the dissociations that matter for curriculum work (a course can teach vocabulary without conceptual grasp, conceptual grasp without performance, or performance without articulation) while keeping inter-rater agreement tractable across a 28-course program.

§ 6The depth extension (KUD+)

KUD as written treats coverage as essentially binary. A course either addresses a competency at the Know level or it does not; either at the Understand level or not; either at Do or not. Early piloting on the GC curriculum confirmed the limitation predicted by this design: when nearly every relevant course "covers" a competency, the binary cells collapse and the matrix loses its ability to discriminate. The prerequisite analyzer cannot distinguish a course that briefly mentions a skill from one that requires students to perform it independently; career-path alignment becomes a recitation of intersections rather than a depth-aware judgment.

KUD+ extends each of Know, Understand, and Do with a 0–5 depth scale anchored to observable student attainment. The scale's structure is intentionally uniform across dimensions; the anchors at each level differ because the underlying cognitive operations differ.

KNOW — recall and identification of content 0 Not present in this course 1 Exposure — student encountered it (heard about, read about), not tested 2 Recognize — student can identify the term/fact when shown options 3 Recall — student can produce the term/fact on cue, without prompt 4 Use correct terminology when discussing the domain 5 Fluent across the full vocabulary, including conventions and edge cases UNDERSTAND — reasoning about the why 0 Not present 1 Restates the explanation as given 2 Explains the rationale in own words 3 Predicts consequences (if X then Y, because…) 4 Reasons through novel cases not previously seen 5 Critiques the principle, identifies limits, extends to new domains DO — behavioral output 0 Not present 1 Performs with per-step direction or supervision 2 Performs using a reference or checklist 3 Performs independently in familiar conditions 4 Adapts performance to new conditions or constraints 5 Performs creatively with critical judgment; can guide others

Three properties of this scale are deliberate and worth naming explicitly.

Zero means absence, not weakness

A score of 0 indicates the dimension is not present in the course at all — a structurally different claim from a low non-zero score. Rendered in the coverage matrix, 0 produces an empty cell; 1 produces a faintly filled cell. This distinction matters for prerequisite analysis: a downstream course that needs students to use a term cannot rely on a prior course that scored only K1 (exposure).

Exposure (K1) is exclusively a Know event

Hearing a principle stated, watching a demonstration, reading about a technique — these are encounters with content the student has not yet engaged. They score K=1 with U=0 and D=0, because awareness is the definition of a Know event and the other dimensions require engagement. The scale enforces this asymmetry: the Understand and Do scales begin at active engagement (restating, performing with direction); they do not include a passive-exposure step. This prevents the inflation common in coverage rubrics where lecture-only content acquires a misleading depth score.

The scale is uniform in shape but distinct in anchor

Each dimension's level descriptors are specific to that dimension. "Mastery" of a fact (K5: fluent vocabulary command) is not the same achievement as mastery of a principle (U5: critique and extension) or mastery of a skill (D5: creative independence). Using the same numerical range across the three permits a clean coverage-matrix rendering; anchoring the levels differently keeps the model honest about what each dimension is measuring.

Two further facts about the scale round out the picture — one a representational choice, one a boundary on what a depth score can claim.

Integer judgments in, continuous derivations out

The 0–5 scale is ordinal with anchored rungs: each integer names a defined behavior (a 3 on Do is "performs independently in familiar conditions"), and there is no defined behavior halfway between two rungs. Faculty judgments are therefore entered, stored, and displayed as integers — a 3.5 would invent a precision the anchors cannot support, and the evidence thresholds (§7) are themselves integer cut-points. But the moment scores are combined — a prerequisite gap (expected-entry minus supplied depth, §12), a program-level roll-up, a career coverage-versus-demand comparison (§11) — the operation yields a continuous quantity, and the honest representation of that derived value is a real number carrying an uncertainty band, not a rounded integer. Integer at the point of judgment; continuous (with intervals) in the model built on top of it. In storage terms that is two layers, not one: the integer judgment persists as the immutable observation, and the durability- and transfer-adjusted depths live as a separate derived float computed from it — the inferred depth never overwrites the judged one, so provenance (observed vs. inferred) is preserved. Treating the ordinal levels as evenly spaced for those derivations is a deliberate simplification — the same one a grade-point average makes — and §7's discipline applies: a derived difference smaller than the instrument's measurement error is a tie, not a ranking.

Depth is instantaneous, not durable

A depth score records what a course develops at the moment it is taught — not the durable depth a student still carries at graduation. These are genuinely different quantities, and the gap between them is large enough to matter. Trained skills fade with disuse: a meta-analysis summarizes the loss in its abstract as ranging from d = −0.01 immediately after training to d = −1.4 after more than a year of non-use, across all task types, with cognitive tasks decaying more than physical ones (δ = −1.15 vs. −0.75) (Arthur et al., 1998). How fast depth fades, and in what shape, is not settled. Replicating Ebbinghaus, Murre and Dros found that the forgetting curve “is not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24 hour data point,” and several candidate equations fit the data with only small differences (Murre & Dros, 2015); so “decay is front-loaded” is a working assumption here, not an established finding. In a 50-year study of school Spanish, what survived the first 3–6 years then stayed stable for up to 30 years without use, and the share reaching this “permastore” grew with the level of training and the grades earned; the author attributes the durability to re-exposure “over long time periods” that “produces a cumulative effect which eventually gives permanence to responses” (Bahrick, 1984, pp. 1, 25). Learners with the least training and lowest grades kept almost none of their recall vocabulary there (p. 23). If so, brittle instantaneous depth and consolidated durable depth are distinct states, and the force that moves one to the other is spaced, varied reinforcement across the sequence (the desirable-difficulties evidence in §4) — so a competency hit once at depth and never revisited is not, by graduation, the depth its lone snapshot records.

The operational consequence is a reading discipline: the coverage and depth maps report opportunity at the moment of each course, reinforced or not by the rest of the curriculum — a lone D-5 reached freshman year is an opportunity, not a guarantee of graduation-time mastery. Whether this ever becomes a measured durability layer rather than a standing caveat is an open research direction, recorded but not built: the same repeated-snapshot history that builds the maps is, in principle, what a decay-and-consolidation model could be fit against — spaced-repetition memory models such as Half-Life Regression and FSRS supply a borrowable form, in which an item's half-life grows exponentially with repeated exposure, weighted by the learner's history of correct and incorrect recalls (Settles & Meeder, 2016, p. 1851) — but its parameters are empirical unknowns, and the external graduate-outcome study constrains them (and validates the direction) without — on its destination data alone — being able to fit them: a fitted decay curve would additionally need a competency-level retained-capability signal, never hard-coded values. It is named here as a known boundary of the instrument, not a feature of it.

§ 7Student-side anchors and the evidence rule

Each level on each scale describes what the student does or grasps, not what the syllabus claims the course will teach. This is the most important practical commitment in the framework: aspirational syllabus language alone is not sufficient evidence for any score above the lowest meaningful level on its dimension. A syllabus statement that "students will understand X" does not by itself justify a score above U=1; the evidence above U=1 must come from student-produced reasoning (a graded written rationale, an oral defense, a journal entry, a memo that requires explaining the principle).

The evidence requirement is formalized in the scoring rule. All nonzero scores require evidence, but the kind of evidence differs by level:

  • k_depth = 1 requires evidence of delivery — a lecture topic listed, a textbook chapter assigned, a Canvas page covering the content.
  • k_depth > 1 requires an evidence excerpt naming where the student is tested or measured on the content.
  • u_depth > 0 requires an evidence excerpt showing student-produced reasoning.
  • d_depth > 0 requires an evidence excerpt showing student-produced behavioral output.

This anchoring is consistent with the second stage of Wiggins and McTighe's backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005, p. 149, Figure 7.2): outcomes are paired with the evidence that would demonstrate them. It is also the practical mechanism by which the binary collapse described in §6 is avoided. When the AI is required to cite evidence before assigning a level, lecture-only content cannot acquire above-K1 scores by accident, and the depth distribution across the program becomes informative.

What KUD+ scores measure — and what they don't

KUD+ operates on three distinct levels of evidence, and keeping them distinct matters for how the scores are interpreted:

  • Course materials evidence intended and assessed opportunities — what the course is designed to develop, as expressed in assignments, rubrics, prompts, and modules. This is the primary source for the audit.
  • Instructor confirmation during the audit conversation improves construct validity — it upgrades "the rubric suggests students wrote rationale" to "the instructor confirms this was graded reasoning, not template compliance." This closes the gap between what was assessed and what was actually developed, per the instructor's account.
  • Student attainment requires student work, rubric-scored performance, or external outcome evidence — what students demonstrably learned, not what the course aimed to develop.

A KUD+ score derived from course materials and instructor confirmation is a course-opportunity score: it characterizes the depth of learning opportunities the course creates and assesses. It becomes a student-learning claim only when linked to assessed student work or longitudinal outcomes — which is precisely what the Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study proposal addresses. This distinction should be carried forward wherever KUD+ scores are cited in program-level analysis: the coverage matrix reports the depth the curriculum was designed to develop; it does not yet directly report the depth graduates achieved.

What K=1 specifically requires

The evidence rule formally requires excerpts above K=1, which leaves K=1 itself uncovered by the rule and creates the appearance that K=1 can be assigned from syllabus mention alone. The framework's position is more constrained than this. The scoring rule in full:

  • K=1 can be supported by delivery evidence — lecture slides, module item titles, reading lists, or instructor confirmation that the topic was covered in class. A topic listed only in the aspirational syllabus, with no module, lecture, or assigned reading that actually delivered it, does not qualify for K=1 and scores K=0.
  • K>1 requires an evidence excerpt showing where the student is tested or measured on the content.
  • U>0 requires an evidence excerpt showing student-produced reasoning.
  • D>0 requires an evidence excerpt showing student-produced behavioral output.

K=1 does not require evidence of student engagement — it marks presence-without-engagement. The auditor's job at this level is distinguishing actual delivery from aspirational mention.

An acknowledged measurement bias

The student-side evidence rule introduces a predictable measurement bias the framework should name explicitly: courses that assess through valid but text-poor methods — studio critique, oral defense, observed performance, portfolio pattern across multiple artifacts — produce less quotable rubric language than courses with written assignments and explicit rubric criteria. Both kinds of assessment can validly evidence high U and D depths; only the latter produces easily-cited text. Naïve application of the evidence rule would systematically under-score the former kind of course. The framework's mitigation is the instructor-confirmation step in the audit conversation: when the auditor cannot find a text-quotable excerpt but the instructor describes a graded oral defense or critique that demonstrates the depth, the instructor's transcript statement becomes the evidence excerpt. This works in practice but depends on the auditor probing for it. The audit conversation explicitly asks about non-text assessment modes for this reason.

This bias is not random noise — it is correlated with tacitness, which makes it the framework's most consequential measurement seam. The competencies assessed through text-poor channels are disproportionately the somatic / craft kind (in Collins' taxonomy of tacit knowledge†, somatic tacit knowledge “is tacit because it is tied to the human body,” as summarized by Bijker (2011) — the press operator's touch, and arguably the designer's eye), and they compound with the LLM auditor's keyword-inference tendency (crediting a competency when its vocabulary appears): tacit craft is by definition low-verbal, so the two biases point the same way, at the same competencies — which in a studio- and press-heavy program like GC are among the highest-value skills the curriculum builds. The portfolio-assessment literature is the reassuring counterweight: artifact/portfolio assessment of craft can be valid, but only with a defined rubric, multiple assessors, and outcome alignment — which is precisely the depth-scale-rubric plus instructor-confirmation discipline above, so the mitigation is empirically endorsed, not merely plausible. The operational consequence worth stating: a low Do score on a craft competency whose evidence is materials-only should be read as a floor, not a ceiling — suspected under-measurement, not confirmed shallowness. Whether that warrants a standing provenance marker (an "evidence-limited D" flag on text-poor craft cells) is gated on a cheap read-only diagnostic — how many such cells actually exist in the captured corpus — rather than assumed. The full analysis, and the deliberate decision not to import contested knowledge-conversion theory or an axis-based durability rule, is in the tacit-knowledge exploration memo.

External evidence supports this discipline. Xu et al. (2026), evaluating LLMs and expert annotators on mapping 200 postsecondary courses to 38 competencies across three frameworks (including O*NET), report two findings that bear directly on the evidence rule. First, two of the three competency frameworks were unreliable to score at first — agreement between the two trained annotators started at a Cohen's κ of 0.29 and 0.17 on the O*NET and ESDC frameworks (against 0.84 on the third) — until the coding guidelines spelled out that competencies “clearly outside the instructional scope” were coded 0 while “potentially relevant competencies lacking sufficient textual evidence” were coded NA, after which agreement rose to κ = 0.94 and 0.92. That distinction is close to the one this framework draws between a foundational scored zero and one recorded null, and between evidence and aspiration. Xu et al.'s rule was about whether course context should inform the inference, so reading it as the evidence/aspiration line is our analogy; but in both cases a clear rule about missing evidence resolved most of the disagreement. Second, no model reached human-level precision on fine-grained pedagogical judgment; one of the two main error sources the authors identify is “systematic over-interpretation of instructional keywords” (the abstract's “instructional keyword inference”) — crediting a competency because its vocabulary appears. That is precisely the failure the evidence-above-zero rule exists to prevent. Their task operates one layer up from this one — judging whether a course document expresses a competency, the course-opportunity side — so the finding reinforces, rather than substitutes for, this framework's further step to assessed student evidence.

This cuts close to home, and the framework should say so plainly: the tool's own auditor is an LLM, so it is exposed to exactly the “instructional keyword inference” Xu documents — crediting a competency because a syllabus uses its vocabulary. That bias cannot be voted away. LLM judges' errors are correlated: more so for models from the same developer or architecture, and, the authors stress, “larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors, even with distinct architectures and providers” (Kim et al., 2025); a nine-model panel behaves like roughly two independent votes, and the single best judge matches or beats the full panel (Kohli, 2026). So a larger panel of model judges cannot cancel a bias the judges hold in common. The same logic, which is ours rather than these papers', applies to self-consistency passes and to any vote among judges who share the bias. The only check on that error class is comparison against a signal the model did not generate: assessed student artifacts (evidence-ladder levels 3–4) and, ultimately, the external graduate-outcome study. This is the structural reason that study is necessary rather than a nice-to-have — the evidence-above-zero rule mitigates the keyword bias but cannot, by itself, eliminate it.

A related discipline governs how scores are reported. Some of the residual disagreement on abstract competencies is likely genuine ambiguity in the construct — what Hüllermeier and Waegeman call aleatoric uncertainty, “the irreducible part of the uncertainty,” which better prompts cannot remove. How much of the residual is of that kind is an open question. The same authors caution that the aleatoric/epistemic split is “context-dependent” and “should not be seen as absolute” (Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021), and the closest study of LLM curriculum scoring traces much of the models' error to causes that could be reduced, such as over-reading instructional keywords (see the Open Hypotheses companion, §4). The honest output is therefore a confidence-stamped band, not a false-precise point: program-level depth is already reported in bands rather than integers, and two scores that differ by less than the instrument's measurement error should be read as a tie, not a ranking.

Two further results from measurement theory shape how the framework treats disagreement, and both cut against the intuition that a better adjudicator is the fix. First, there is no clean human oracle either. Agreement among trained human raters on open-ended scoring is often modest. In the Xu et al. study above, the two trained annotators began at κ = 0.29 and 0.17 on two of the three frameworks; studies reported elsewhere put essay raters at a mean r of 0.54 (Cohen, 2017) and a high-stakes teacher performance assessment (PACT, the predecessor of edTPA) at weighted κ = 0.17 across 19 candidates (Lyness, Peterson & Yates, 2021) (both cited in the Open Hypotheses companion, §4). Such figures fall short of the 0.61–0.80 band Landis and Koch label “substantial,” cut-points the authors themselves call “clearly arbitrary” (Landis & Koch, 1977) — so the loop is not "the human corrects the AI to the truth"; both are imperfect judges of an ambiguous construct, which is why the framework leans on triangulation (evidence, instructor confirmation, convergence across independent signals) rather than on any single authority. Second, generalizability theory says where reliability actually comes from (Cronbach et al., 1972; Brennan, 2001)†: decomposing score variance into rater, item, and occasion facets, the decision-relevant result is conditional: when tasks vary more than raters do, adding raters buys little reliability while adding evidence items or tasks buys a great deal. Which facet dominates is an empirical question for each design. If task variance dominates in KUD scoring, then when two readings of a course disagree, the high-value response is more and better assessed evidence, not more adjudicators arguing over the same thin materials. (Where rater severity genuinely varies, it is best modeled as a calibrated facet rather than averaged away as noise (Linacre, 1989)†.) None of this licenses false confidence; it is the reason the framework reports bands, triangulates signals, and treats the external graduate-outcome study — not a larger panel of judges — as the load-bearing check.

§ 8Foundational competencies

The KUD model fits cognitive and procedural outcomes cleanly. It fits affective and dispositional outcomes — agency, attention to detail, resilience, curiosity, communication — less cleanly, because there is no meaningful "Know resilience" or "Understand attention to detail" event in undergraduate practice. Students do not recall facts about resilience or reason their way through it in the moment; they exhibit it.

The framework treats five baseline foundational competencies as program-wide threads and scores each one on Do only:

  • Agency — initiative, ownership of decisions, drive.
  • Attention to Detail — care, accuracy, error-catching, follow-through.
  • Resilience — persistence through failure, adaptive recovery.
  • Curiosity — substantive questioning, inquiry beyond requirements.
  • Communication — written, oral, and visual articulation.

Their Know and Understand cells are recorded as null rather than zero; zero would imply the course tried to develop them at the K or U level and failed, which is not the right reading. A foundational with d_depth = 0 is a valid and informative finding: it means the course does not create the conditions (open-ended challenges, revision cycles, productive failure, peer-facing communication) that develop the disposition.

This shape is also why problem-solving — which employers name as often as any disposition — is deliberately not a sixth foundational. A foundational is scored on Do alone because its Know and Understand cells are vacuous: one exhibits resilience; one does not “know” it. Problem-solving is the inverse — it is irreducibly K×U×D, the deployment of principle-organized knowledge, reasoning through novelty, and adaptive performance together, with no existence apart from the domain content it operates on (§4). Modeling it as a Do-only attribute would amputate the organized knowledge that constitutes it, and scoring it as one cross-cutting line would re-commit the free-floating-skill error §4 dismantles. The framework instead captures both halves of what “problem-solving” colloquially fuses, in two places it already has: the disposition to engage hard problems lives in foundationals already in this list — Resilience (persistence through productive failure), Curiosity (inquiry into the unfamiliar), and Agency (self-directed deployment) — while the competence lives in the upper depths of the domain competencies (D-4/D-5, U-4/U-5). A sixth “problem-solving” foundational would double-count the first and re-abstract the second; the property is therefore read at the program level as an emergent pattern (§4), not stored in a per-course cell.

The selection of the five baselines

The choice of these five is a framework commitment, not a derivation from a single source. Three inputs shaped it: the NACE Career Readiness framework (Communication appears directly; Critical Thinking is handled as a program-level emergent property per §4); the dispositional-learning literature, which converges on initiative, persistence, attention regulation, and curiosity as the compact core; and GC-specific advisory-board feedback, which consistently names Attention to Detail as the most domain-relevant disposition employers cite for this program.

Five rather than a larger set reflects a practical constraint: every baseline is scored in every snapshot, so each addition compounds the audit load, and inter-rater agreement on Do-only dispositional scoring degrades as the list grows. The five are fixed and scored in every capture; the auditor may add further foundationals (e.g., Collaboration, Professionalism, Ethical Judgment) per-course when materials evidence them, allowing the data to surface dispositions the baseline did not anticipate. Collaboration is the most consistently mentioned candidate for promotion to the baseline in a future revision if repeated captures confirm it is program-wide.

§ 9Program-level structure: the 3-Act model

Program-level analyses — scaffolding, prerequisite-gap, problem-solving capacity — require an organizing structure for the program against which the analyses can be run. A flat list of 28 courses is too undifferentiated for the scaffolding analysis to surface useful findings; a faculty-readable structure that says where in the program each course sits is what makes "this course expects mastery of something never introduced earlier" a tractable diagnostic. The framework requires a program-organization model. It does not prescribe a specific one.

The structure this framework proposes for the GC program is a three-act model: Foundations & Agency (learn the field), Integration & Mastery (integrate the field), Specialty & Application (apply a focus). Like every other commitment in this framework, the 3-Act model is a proposal — a working organizing structure offered for faculty deliberation, not an existing institutional policy or a settled curriculum design. The proposed design intent is to accelerate students through foundations into integration as fast as the field allows — anchored on the empirical observation that GC 4400 and GC 4060 are the courses where graduating students consistently report that "it all came together" and they first saw where they could add professional value. Completing Act 2 by mid-Junior year is proposed so that students would cross this integrative threshold while they still have 1.5+ years remaining in the program to develop career-path-specific skills in Act 3. This would invert the cornerstone-capstone bookend prevalent in engineering education (where integration is reserved for the senior capstone, leaving no time for post-integration specialization): the 3-Act structure deliberately positions integration earlier so specialization can follow.

The 3-Act proposal is consistent with established curriculum-design traditions but is not derived from any single one. The strongest theoretical parallels are Bruner's spiral curriculum (where each act revisits the disciplinary content at higher complexity), the engineering cornerstone-capstone model (with the 3-Act structure adding the named middle act that cornerstone-capstone leaves implicit), the modern medical-education three-phase structure (preclinical → clinical → specialty, though with externally-validated gates the 3-Act structure does not yet have), and cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989†; checked against the earlier 1987 technical-report version), which adapts the methods of traditional apprenticeship to cognitive skills because “skills and knowledge taught in schools have become abstracted from their uses in the world” — drawing on Jean Lave’s ethnography of craft apprenticeship. (It predates the Lave & Wenger† (1991) situated-learning critique of decontextualized foundational instruction; the 3-Act companion uses it to answer that critique.) The framework explicitly acknowledges these as consistency relationships, not derivations — the design choices (three acts specifically; the named pairing of Foundations with Agency; the mid-Junior year timing) are proposed commitments anchored in program-coordinator judgment and observed student experience, not literature prescriptions.

The program has long described itself as a “spiraling curriculum,” and the 3-Act structure is best understood as that description made explicit. The term traces to Jerome Bruner's The Process of Education† (1960), which argued that a curriculum should be organized around the structure of a discipline and began from the hypothesis that “any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of development” (Bruner, 1960, p. 30, as quoted in Gibbs, 2014). The spiral curriculum is the structural consequence: learners revisit the discipline's core ideas repeatedly — in Gibbs's summary, “students revisit a topic, theme, or subject several times throughout their schooling, where the complexity of the topic is increased with each visit so the new learning is connected to the old learning” (Gibbs, 2014) (elaborated in Bruner, Toward a Theory of Instruction, 1966†).

The 3-Act model is that spiral instantiated for Graphic Communications: Act 1 introduces the disciplinary core — design, typography, production, color, workflow — in foundational form; Act 2 revisits that same core at the level of integration (the GC 4060 / GC 4400 “it all came together” courses); Act 3 revisits it once more at the level of specialized application. Each act is a turn of the spiral on the same content, at higher complexity, depending on the act before it — which is exactly what the scaffolding analysis checks when it flags an Act-3 course that expects mastery no earlier act builds. Bruner supplies the principle (revisit-with-increasing-complexity) and its rationale (readiness as actively taught, not waited for); the number of acts, the mid-Junior integration target, and the five career tracks remain the program's own design choices, offered for faculty deliberation rather than derived from Bruner.

The scaffolding analysis treats act boundaries as analytical waypoints. A senior-level (Act 3) course that expects mastery of a competency no Act 1 or Act 2 course actually introduces is flagged as a brittle scaffold — the diagnostic concept that operationalizes prerequisite-network analysis (an established methodology in computing-education research) at the level of named program phases. The framework's contribution at this level is pairing the diagnostic logic with depth-aware K/U/D scoring in a design curriculum; the diagnostic logic itself has antecedents in the broader curriculum-mapping literature.

The full research synthesis — including the corrections an adversarial review surfaced, the substantive response to Lave & Wenger's situated-learning critique via cognitive apprenticeship, the distinction between SDT (a motivational-environment theory) and self-regulated-learning (an agency-as-developmental-outcome theory), and an honest accounting of which 3-Act design choices are research-supported vs. stipulated — is in the companion document The 3-Act Program Structure: A Research Synthesis. The framework's program-level views (the coverage matrix shipped in Phase 1A, the scaffolding analysis specified for Phase 1B) read against the 3-Act structure; if the structure is revised, the diagnostics revise with it.

Inputs consumed

The capture session pulls everything the system already holds about the course: the catalog row (description, prerequisites, stated objectives, listed major projects, required incoming skills); any uploaded materials (rubrics, project briefs, lab handouts, syllabus PDFs) with their extracted text; Canvas materials as detailed in the source table above; and linked external documents. No re-ingestion is required at capture time. Where prerequisite courses have themselves been captured previously, their confirmed profiles are included as authoritative evidence of what students arrive with.

The audit conversation

An AI auditor opens the session with a brief summary of what the materials show and the single most consequential gap or contradiction it noticed, then asks one targeted question at a time. The audit covers six areas, worked through across the conversation:

  1. Prerequisite sufficiency. The auditor walks the catalog's required incoming skills one at a time, asking whether each is genuinely arrived with or re-taught in this course. Where a prerequisite course has a captured profile, the auditor reads it directly and cites its depths rather than asking the instructor to recall.
  2. Stated objectives vs. evidenced outcomes. The auditor performs the comparison in both directions: objectives without material evidence (aspirational), and material outcomes not captured in the stated objectives (taught but unstated).
  3. Cross-source overlaps and contradictions. Differences in point values, assignment lists, or outcomes language between the syllabus, Canvas, and uploaded materials are surfaced as findings, with one source designated authoritative by the instructor.
  4. Bloom's-level probe per major assignment. For the highest-stakes assignments, the auditor asks which Bloom verb most accurately describes what students are doing, and what evidence supports that classification.
  5. Threshold concept. The conceptual shift that separates students who genuinely understand the course from students who memorized its content.
  6. Foundational competency conditions. For each of the five baseline dispositions, the auditor probes whether the course creates the behavioral conditions that develop the disposition, rather than asking whether the course "teaches" it.

The instructor sees a readiness signal on every turn: a 0–100 score reflecting the auditor's honest self-assessment of how defensibly the profile could be generated right now, with short labels for what is covered and what is still being probed. The instructor may stop the session at any point; the score is advisory, not gating.

Inaccessible content

The source table above identifies the pipeline's current reach. Sources listed as "Not supported" — principally video platforms other than YouTube (Panopto, Canvas Studio, Vimeo, Kaltura) and external LTI tool content — produce no retrievable text. Where materials reference such sources, the auditor is required to detect them, surface them with their specifics (title, URL, location in the materials, estimated content, scoring implication), and keep scoring conservative pending instructor confirmation. Above-zero scores that depend on inaccessible content are flagged in the rationale fields accordingly.

Output: the Course Outcome Profile

On instructor command, the system produces a structured profile:

  • 5–15 technical competencies discovered from the materials, each scored on K/U/D depth with evidence excerpts and a short rationale per competency.
  • All five baseline foundational competencies scored on Do only (with d_depth = 0 when the course does not develop the disposition), plus any additional foundationals the materials evidenced.
  • Audit notes covering prerequisite gaps, objective misalignments, cross-source conflicts, and suggested objective revisions.
  • If requested, a draft revision of the learning objectives grounded in the materials evidence; this is informational only and does not modify the catalog automatically.

The profile is reviewed by the instructor in a structured editor — sliders per dimension, evidence visible per cell, audit notes in a sidebar — and confirmed before downstream views consume it.

Methodological note: build-time pedagogical encoding

The auditor's reasoning is not free-form. Each AI integration point in the system is governed by a version-controlled system prompt that encodes one or more named pedagogical skills from an external library — Manning's Education Agent Skills Library (Manning, 2025). The prompts encode procedural skill definitions from this open GitHub-based library. It is used as an implementation scaffold, not as independent validation of KUD+ — and the library itself cautions that “the prompts themselves have not been empirically validated as AI interventions.” The scholarly grounding for the analytical commitments the prompts encode — backward design (Wiggins & McTighe), assessment validity, evidence-first planning — is the pedagogical research those prompts operationalize; Manning's library is the implementation artifact through which that grounding is applied to AI agent behavior.

The encoding is build-time, not runtime: the skill definitions (Backwards Design, KUD Chart Authoring, Coverage Audit, Developmental Band Translator, Assessment Validity Checker, Disciplinary AI Literacy, KUD Knowledge Type Mapper) are read at design time and their reasoning frameworks are embodied directly in the prompt bodies. Each prompt names the skills it encodes in its frontmatter as the contract that the encoding happened. The intent is to make the AI's analytical disposition inspectable and citable rather than opaque. Where Manning frameworks describe the analytical scaffold (how to read evidence, how to type knowledge correctly, how to weigh validity threats), the depth scales described in §6 remain the operational rubric — Manning frames the question; KUD+ supplies the answer space.

§ 11Career-path alignment

The career-path side of the framework is implemented through a parallel CareerCapture workflow that produces a Role Outcome Profile for each named career destination. The current scope identifies five destinations specific to the GC program — Account Management, Brand Strategy, Production & Operations, Creative Generalist, and AI Workflow Orchestrator — with the understanding that the set is subject to revision as evidence accumulates.

From employer input to Role Outcome Profile

The CareerCapture workflow Live (faculty trial) follows the same capture-and-confirm pattern as CourseCapture, with employer-side inputs in place of course materials: a job description, plain-language descriptions of typical work and first-six-month expectations, common graduate gaps, evidence of readiness, and (optionally) interview questions or portfolio prompts. The system drafts a Role Outcome Profile scoped to the same KUD+ structure used on the course side, with depth values describing entry-level expectation rather than course delivery. The profile is reviewed and amended by the employer respondent before being used for alignment.

At scale, the framework intends for each career destination to be informed by multiple employer inputs (a working triangulation target of N ≥ 3 per role before the synthesized profile is treated as anything but a draft), and for the synthesis layer to surface consensus views and minority dissent rather than averaging into a single number.

Alignment to the curriculum

This comparison is possible at all only because both sides are measured on the same ruler: the KUD+ depth scale describes what the curriculum develops and what the role demands in the identical 0–5 vocabulary, so career-readiness becomes a literal same-axis comparison — demand minus attainment — rather than a translation between a syllabus and a job posting. With Course Outcome Profiles on one side and Role Outcome Profiles on the other, alignment becomes a depth-aware comparison: for each sub-competency the role requires at a given level, the program reports the chain of courses that develop the sub-competency and at what depth.   • Scaffolding analysis. Occupational frameworks (O*NET, ESCO): where they fit, and where they don't

A natural question is whether the career-destination side should be anchored in a standardized occupational framework — the US Department of Labor's O*NET or the EU's ESCO — rather than in bespoke, employer-derived Role Outcome Profiles, and whether the sub-competency vocabulary the program maps against should be sourced from one. The answer the evidence supports is a split verdict: these frameworks are useful at one tier and on one side, and actively counter-indicated at another.

Rejected — the generic-skill tier as a structural backbone. O*NET's 35 cross-occupational "Skills" (Critical Thinking, Complex Problem Solving, Active Listening) are precisely the context-free, transferable-skill construct §4 of this document spends its length rejecting — and they are rejected by the same authorities §4 already cites (Willingham; Perkins & Salomon; Gick & Holyoak). The independent National Academies review of O*NET (NRC, 2010) reached the same conclusion from the measurement side: whereas the Abilities descriptors “reflect a long history of psychological research,” “many of the descriptors in the Skills domain lack such an extensive research base,” and descriptors overlap across domains without clear distinctions (the review's example: “problem solving” appears in the Abilities, Skills, Work Styles, and Generalized Work Activities domains, and, between the Abilities and Skills versions, the content model “does not clearly distinguish the different meanings”) — under-specified enough to threaten reliable rater differentiation — a targeted content-model weakness the review identifies (and a predicted research-need), not a measured low-reliability finding, and one inside an otherwise useful system. Deeper still, the competency-based-education critique — Gonczi's distinction (Gonczi, 1994) between behaviorist skill-decomposition and integrated competence, and Winch's argument that achieving an occupational profile's individual skills does not by itself constitute occupational competence, because it strips the integration and context that constitute it (Brockmann, Clarke, & Winch, 2008) — warns against anchoring any curriculum to an external skill checklist as its backbone. The clearest empirical datum is the UK's National Vocational Qualifications (first awarded in 1988): an explicit competency-checklist credential whose Level 2 delivered poor-to-negative wage returns on its classroom route (roughly 8% lower earnings) even as its workplace, on-the-job route fared better (roughly 7% higher) (Dearden, McGranahan, & Sianesi, 2004) — the checklist-style route underperforming just as an atomized-competence design would predict (ability-bias confounds part of the raw effect). Atomizing competence into an external list optimizes for adequacy — the central tendency of current practice — rather than the depth and integration that produce expertise and prepare students to learn what the framework has not yet named.

Aligned — the domain tier, and the demand side. O*NET's domain-anchored layers — Knowledge areas, Generalized and Detailed Work Activities, and occupation-specific Tasks — describe the within-field content where the transfer literature says near transfer is reliable, and where occupational frameworks have the most construct validity. Used at that tier, an occupational framework is a defensible external-validation vocabulary: a sourced checklist against which a hand-curated, GC-specific sub-competency set can be cross-checked for omissions. And O*NET's Importance and Level ratings are a genuine career-demand signal — they belong on the demand side, where they can seed a Role Outcome Profile so that employer input refines a baseline rather than originating one from scratch. Two limits keep this bounded: O*NET is not a ready-made GC vocabulary (its 33 Knowledge areas are far too coarse — there is no Typography, Color Science, or Press Chemistry; the GC-specific content must be hand-extracted from the Tasks and Detailed Work Activities of GC-relevant SOC occupations), and at least two GC destinations (an emergent "AI Workflow Orchestrator"; a cross-occupational "Creative Generalist") have no clean SOC home at all.

The reframe that matters. Every occupational framework — and every commercial skills-intelligence system built on one — shares a documented blind spot: it records the presence of a skill but not its depth. O*NET's Level scale is a job-demand rating, not a learner-attainment measure; ESCO encodes no within-skill proficiency at all. The KUD+ depth scale — attainment scored at graduated depth against student-side evidence — is that missing layer. The framework is therefore not behind the skills-intelligence field for lacking a large standardized skills taxonomy; it is ahead on the dimension that field is documented to be missing, and the design risk runs the other way: atomizing the evidence-anchored depth core into an external checklist would trade the framework's actual differentiator for the field's actual weakness. The operational consequence — keep the hand-curated, evidence-anchored sub-competencies as the backbone; use occupational frameworks only at the domain tier as a sourced checklist and on the demand side as a benchmark; and never push them into the evidence-first capture interview — is developed, with the full research base and a utilization plan, in the companion occupational-frameworks research memo.

A national curriculum-to-skills benchmark, and what the skill-mapping field shows

One external resource is worth naming because it changes what is feasible on the demand side. The Course-Skill Atlas (Javadian Sabet et al., 2024) computed national O*NET Detailed-Work-Activity coverage by major across roughly 3.16 million U.S. course syllabi — a ready-made, independently-built map of what each major is nationally evidenced to teach, in the same work-activity vocabulary O*NET uses on the demand side. It offers two things the bespoke approach lacks: a benchmark (the national GC-curriculum work-activity profile, against which our captured coverage can be compared — "is this program emphasizing what GC programs broadly do, and where does it deliberately differ?"), and an empirical prior (the Atlas finds roughly nine syllabi suffice for a stable major-level profile — a useful estimate of how many course captures stabilize a program-level aggregate). It does not replace the hand-curated, depth-anchored sub-competencies; it carries the same presence-not-depth limitation as O*NET, and so is used the way §11 uses occupational frameworks throughout — a sourced starting point and cross-check that refines a baseline, never the backbone itself. This is also the most direct way to keep the graduate-outcome study from starting the destination side from scratch: a graduate's coded occupation already has an O*NET / Atlas work-activity profile that can seed that destination's competency demand, leaving faculty and employer input to refine it — with the standing caveat that where a GC specific (Typography, Color Science, Press Chemistry) finds no clean match, that absence is itself a finding, not a defect to paper over.

The broader skill-mapping literature also bears on the framework's method, in two directions. On the curriculum side, benchmarked curriculum-to-competency matching is now an active task (UniSkill (Musazade et al., 2026); "From Course to Skill" (Xu et al., 2025)), and its central empirical finding is consistent with this tool's architecture: in Xu et al. (2025), pure embedding-similarity matching reached a precision of only about 24% (the share of its top-ten skills that raters scored 4 or higher on a 1–5 alignment scale), while retrieve-then-reason — an embedding shortlist judged by an LLM — reached roughly 70–80%. That is the same shortlist-then-reason split the capture and analysis pipeline uses, though the paper used no depth scale and does not separate how much of the gain comes from the retrieval step and how much from the reasoning step. On the demand side, skill extraction and classification from job postings is an active field that its own survey calls “emerging”; matching postings to ESCO, the EU's skills/occupations standard, is common in the classification work and, the survey notes, “gaining popularity” (Senger et al., 2024) — a complementary, demand-first way to characterize what a destination requires, against which O*NET-seeded targets can be triangulated. These are cited here as external grounding and available resources; the framework's own design for a weighted, match-quality-stamped competency→career cross-walk built on the Atlas is a forward proposal, developed in the Open Hypotheses & Research Agenda companion, not a built feature.

Underneath both the curriculum-side threads and the career cross-walk sits one question — how the framework decides two competencies are related at all — and the proposed answer is the same retrieve-then-reason discipline: embedding similarity proposes candidate relations; depth-aware reasoning adjudicates them. Only part of this exists today, and only for course materials: the cross-course evidence spine embeds every course's materials into a shared space so the analysis can retrieve related content across the program, not just within a course. Finding relations between competencies — stitching a competency's appearances into a thread across courses, and matching that thread to a career destination — is designed but not yet built or validated.

The confidence this warrants is moderate and specific. The external support is one study on a related but different task: in the course-to-skill matching of Xu et al. (2025) described above, an embedding shortlist judged by an LLM was far more precise than embedding similarity alone; that study used no depth scale and did not test threads across courses or matching to careers. Our own evidence is a small internal pilot — seven competencies and sixteen faculty-labeled cases — in which sharper competency definitions removed thematic false matches without losing the genuine ones; promising, but the feature it tested is not yet built. The same pilot showed why faculty stay in the loop: two AI judges from different model families agreed in rejecting false matches, but both missed a genuine one, consistent with the correlated errors documented for LLM judges (Kim et al., 2025) (Kohli, 2026). Embedding proximity is in any case a shortlist, not a verdict: raw similarity over-credits, happily rating “understand the bigger picture” as near-equivalent to “interpret a technical diagram.” So any proposed relation would be adjudicated by the reasoning pass, confirmed by faculty, and bounded by the authored competency structure (the prerequisite/surmise relations of §12) rather than left to proximity alone. A domain-tuned embedder might sharpen the shortlist on in-vocabulary matches like color management ↔ color measurement — a real but bounded gain, to be established by a bake-off on our own data rather than a general benchmark. The full relatedness engine (weighted, match-quality-stamped, depth-gated) is a forward proposal in the research-agenda companion.

Nodes and edges: identity vs. relatedness, similarity vs. complementarity

Two distinctions decide which mechanism fits which task — the first established by a 2026-07 measurement on this project's own data, the second a design position the same logic implies but which this project has not itself measured (a forward proposal). First (measured), identity is not relatedness. Deciding whether a captured course competency is the same skill as a canonical career sub-competency — the supply→canonical match Q1 depends on — was measured at roughly 70–90% wrong when performed by an LLM against thin node descriptions (judge-robust across a three-model panel), and an embedding second-opinion rescued only about 1 in 34 of those errors. Identity is a reject/rule/dimension judgment — is this a disposition or a technical skill? the same skill or merely the same topic? — and topical-proximity embeddings, whether general or domain-framed, reproduce the very over-crediting that causes the errors (raw similarity happily rates “deliver a presentation” as near “develop a proposal”). One proposed remedy is not a better vector but a better node: canonical competencies given explicit boundaries, positive and negative examples, and sibling distinctions, so the identity judgment has something precise to test against. Its load-bearing assumption — that richer node definitions make an LLM better at the identity judgment — is unproven, and two data points cut against it: Xu et al. (2026) found that adding human-defined competency descriptions to LLM prompts “does not improve LLM performance,” and in this project's own measurement a one-line model upgrade cut error more than the prompt fix did. Enrichment is therefore pilot-gated and additive, not the primary remedy. An enriched node would be the substrate's unit — the nodes of a competency graph, echoing the node-and-connection structure of Dynamic Learning Maps (Kingston & Broaddus, 2017) and the surmise-linked items of Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon & Falmagne, 1985).

Second (established in the literature at scale, though not yet on this project's own data), on the edges of that graph, similarity is not complementarity. Two relationships matter and pull apart: skills that overlap (vector illustration ↔ Adobe Illustrator — captured by semantic similarity, e.g. an ESCO-trained skill embedder such as Skill-Sim, and the domain-tuned-embedder bake-off noted above) and skills that stack (structural packaging design ↔ flexographic prepress — distant in meaning yet jointly demanded in real work). A conventional similarity embedder places complementary pairs farther apart, so it is the wrong instrument for scaffolding, prerequisite, and skill-bundle reasoning, where complementarity is the load-bearing relation. That the two relations genuinely diverge is measured, not merely asserted: Liu et al.'s 65-million-advert UK skills co-occurrence network (Liu, Clarke, Rohenkohl, & Barahona, 2024) found data-driven clusters that strongly co-occur yet carry low intrinsic semantic similarity — co-occurrence is "not aligned with the generic semantic understanding from language models," and their clusters "differ from expert-authored categorisations … important relationships between skills are not captured by expert assessment alone." Complementarity is therefore recovered not from meaning but from co-occurrence — the Skill2vec approach (Le, Vo, & Dang, 2017) that learns skill vectors from skills that co-occur in the same job posting, Liu et al.'s national network, and the Course-Skill Atlas on the curriculum side. This cuts two ways for the substrate: relationship edges want data (experts demonstrably miss co-occurrence links), while identity and depth at the nodes remain the expert-and-evidence judgment the rest of this framework defends. The full measurement-driven proposal for this substrate — the audited coverage-alignment failure, the node-enrichment proposal, and its pilot gate — is developed in the research-agenda companion, §5. The relatedness engine is therefore a two-vector design — a semantic-similarity vector for overlap, a co-occurrence vector for complementarity — sitting on the shared node substrate: coverage-identity consumes the nodes; relatedness/stacking consumes the edges. Both are forward proposals beyond the live cross-course retrieval; the node-enrichment increment (crisp boundaries, examples, aliases; AI-drafted, faculty-confirmed) is the first slice, gated on re-measuring the same supply→canonical alignment fixture.

A further open proposal — exploration, not a commitment — pushes this substrate one step on: treat O*NET's Detailed Work Activities as an explicit shared coordinate frame ("snap-to-grid, with a snap tolerance") that both course competencies and career demands are addressed to, so cross-course threads are declared rather than inferred — and, its highest-value use, so the capture interview can be directed to probe the threads a course's role implies instead of documenting whatever surfaces first, with the evidence gate intact and off-grid GC specifics (Typography, Color Science, Press Chemistry) kept as first-class "color-outside-the-lines" nodes. A two-pass adversarial review and a targeted literature review left it gated on a cheap diagnostic (does a shared address frame raise capture coverage without inflating over-crediting, and do skills snap consistently?) and pointed to CTDL / Rich Skill Descriptors as the interoperability envelope rather than a bespoke schema. Full development, guardrails, and go/no-go: the skill-fabric exploration memo.

§ 12Prerequisite-gap method

The prerequisite-gap analysis answers Q2 at the course level: do the skills students bring in from declared prerequisite courses actually support what this course's assignments require? The method is implemented as a set of persisted, faculty-confirmed prerequisite edges from which a deterministic gap engine reads. This section explains the methodological commitments that distinguish this approach from simpler alternatives.

Direct edges, not authored transitivity

Each prerequisite edge is a direct, skill-tagged relationship: it connects one focal course to one prerequisite course and names the specific sub-competency the focal course's assignments expect, along with the K/U/D depths it presupposes. These edges are the only authored artifact. Transitivity — the fact that a course two levels back in the prerequisite chain may also contribute to the expected depth — is never authored explicitly; it is derived at analysis time by traversing the graph of direct edges. This design choice prevents the combinatorial maintenance burden that arises when transitive dependencies are hand-curated and prevents double-counting: each course's contribution is counted once, at its position in the chain, not once per downstream consumer.

Ordinal-MAX aggregation: why depths are not summed

When multiple prerequisite courses all develop the same sub-competency, the gap engine computes the gap by taking the maximum attained depth across all direct prerequisites, then comparing that maximum against the focal course's expected depth. It does not sum the depths across prerequisites.

The reason is structural: KUD+ depth values are ordinal levels, not quantities. A student who has encountered a skill in two separate courses at depth 3 each has not thereby attained depth 6 — they have attained depth 3, perhaps reinforced. What matters for prerequisite sufficiency is whether any course in the student's background has developed the skill to the required level; additional courses developing the same skill at the same or lower level add practice value but do not change the attained depth for the purpose of the gap calculation. Summing ordinal levels would produce a number that has no interpretable meaning in the depth-scale framework and would cause the analysis to erroneously judge students well-prepared when no single course reaches the required depth.

Two qualifications are worth recording — as hypotheses about what richer aggregation might one day capture, not as current behavior. First, that “practice value” may be more than a footnote: repeated and varied development of the same skill plausibly increases the durability of the attained depth, so a competency reinforced at depth 3 across several courses, while no deeper at the moment of each course than one hit once at depth 3, is more likely to still be there at graduation (the instantaneous-vs-durable distinction in §6). Second, depth in an adjacent, higher-level competency may actively lift a related skill — the knowledge-integration effect in which well-organized expertise pulls its neighbors up. Both are deliberately outside the current gap calculation, which stays conservatively Ordinal-MAX: durability and neighbor effects are empirical unknowns that would have to be calibrated against graduate-outcome data before they could justifiably move a sufficiency judgment, and crediting them prematurely would re-introduce the very false-precision the MAX rule exists to avoid. They are developed as open hypotheses in the Open Hypotheses & Research Agenda companion (durability / “bleed-off” and the depth-gated “pull-up”).

The intended-vs-attained distinction and the basis stamp

Every gap computation carries a basis field that distinguishes three conditions:

  • measured — the prerequisite course's attained depth comes from a confirmed, evidence-backed Course Outcome Profile (a captured snapshot). This is the highest-quality basis: it reflects what students who completed the course actually demonstrated, per instructor confirmation during an audit conversation.
  • intended — the prerequisite course's depth is estimated from the catalog description, syllabus, or the focal course's incoming_expectations field, without a confirmed snapshot. This is intended coverage — what the course claims or is understood to provide — not attained depth. Per the evidence-above-zero rule (§7), a syllabus statement is not by itself evidence of student attainment; the intended basis exposes this caveat transparently rather than presenting the estimate as equivalent to measured depth. In the gap view, intended-basis cells are rendered as claimed — matching the evidence ladder's terminology — and are flagged as unverified.
  • none — the prerequisite course has not been captured and no intended estimate is available. The gap engine returns no data for this edge, not a gap. This is the honesty commitment: an uncaptured prerequisite cannot be assumed to provide zero coverage (which would make every unprobed edge look like a critical gap) or assumed to provide full coverage (which would make the absence of data invisible). no_data cells are displayed explicitly with a "not yet captured" label; the burden of resolving them is to capture the prerequisite course, not to infer from absence.

The intended-vs-measured distinction maps directly onto the evidence-above-zero rule described in §7: measured depth has the same evidentiary status as a confirmed Course Outcome Profile cell; intended depth has the status of a syllabus claim (K=1 in the framework's evidence hierarchy). Program-level analyses that aggregate across courses should weight these differently and surface the distinction, not collapse them into a single gap count.

What the gap engine computes

For each (focal course, sub-competency) pair where the focal course specifies an expected incoming depth, the engine:

  1. Collects all direct prerequisite edges on that sub-competency.
  2. For each edge, looks up the prerequisite course's attained or intended depth for that sub-competency, with its basis.
  3. Selects the edge with the maximum depth (if multiple edges share the same sub-competency) — ordinal-MAX, no double-count.
  4. Computes the gap as expected_depth − max_attained_depth per dimension (K, U, D). A positive result is a gap; zero or negative means the requirement is met or exceeded at that depth.
  5. Stamps the result with the basis of the max-depth edge, since the reliability of the gap judgment is that of its best-evidenced source.

The result is a per-(course × sub-competency × dimension) gap record, stamped with its evidence basis, that feeds both the per-course gap view and any future program-wide rollup.

Design status

The prerequisite-edge method described here is implemented in the tool as of 2026-06-05. The per-course gap view is live at /courses/[code] for confirmed snapshots. The program-wide graph visualization is deferred.

The intended-basis rough pass is also live as of 2026-06-05. A one-LLM-call-per-course extractor (intended-skills-extract) reads catalog fields (description, learning objectives, major projects, required skills) against the sub-competency catalog and stores syllabus-asserted K/U/D depths in a separate course_intended_coverage table, banded claimed on the evidence ladder. This is the mechanism that produces intended coverage for uncaptured courses, feeding the gap engine's basis:'intended' seam so cold-start gap views are non-empty before any instructor has been audited. The binding rule applies without exception: intended coverage is reference-only in CourseCapture. The credibility band leaves claimed only through real evidence gathered in the audit conversation — never by the instructor confirming what the syllabus already says. This rule prevents acquiescence-bias laundering, where an instructor's natural inclination to agree with their own syllabus would otherwise launder aspiration into attainment.

§ 13Validation: how we'd know if this works

The framework makes substantive commitments that established research does not directly validate. The depth scales are working-hypothesis instruments. The 3-Act program structure is a proposed organizing model. The claim that cumulative depth + productive-failure scaffolding + reflection produces transferable problem-solving competence four years out is theoretically coherent but empirically untested at the program scale this tool operates at. The framework's stance is not that these commitments are settled — it's that the tool is itself the research instrument that generates the evidence to test them.

Three layers of validation are designed into the framework's data shape.

Internal validation: the data the tool generates about itself

Every captured snapshot is a dated, evidence-backed record of what one course developed at one moment in time. Snapshots accumulate. Each program-level analysis (the coverage matrix, the per-course prerequisite-gap view, and the scaffolding strip today; the scaffolding AI-narrative layer and the advising view still to come) reads against the snapshot corpus and produces diagnostic outputs that are themselves measurable: does the matrix surface gaps that faculty review confirms as real gaps, or does it surface patterns that faculty review dismisses as noise? Does the scaffolding analysis flag brittle scaffolds that hold up under interrogation, or does it produce false positives at a rate that erodes trust? The flag-and-dispute pipeline is the framework's internal validation surface — every AI judgment can be disputed, disputes persist, and patterns of disagreement update the AI's prompts over time. But it is important to be precise about what that loop can and cannot do: internal disputes can calibrate the maps and reduce false positives, yet they cannot on their own validate the construct claim — that the surfaced patterns predict graduate problem-solving — because an internal loop supplies no criterion the model did not generate, and model judges' errors are correlated (Kim et al., 2025) (Kohli, 2026) (those papers measure correlation between different models; applying it to this loop is our reasoning). The tool instruments the predictor side of the experiment; it becomes a research instrument for the construct claim only when its snapshot history is linked to the external graduate-outcome criterion. The internal loop calibrates; the external study validates.

External validation: graduate-outcome data against career-target competencies

The empirical question the framework most needs answered is whether graduates who pass through a 3-Act-structured curriculum, with the coverage and scaffolding patterns the matrix surfaces, actually achieve the career-target competencies the framework predicts. The companion Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study Data collected; second-coder check pending describes the external-data check: a dataset of Clemson GC graduates — a 268-record pilot coded for 2022–2025, now extended to 1,337 validated graduates, with 672 coded first jobs in the study's 2016–2026 window spread across 103 occupations — used to test whether the framework's career-target classifications and competency predictions match actual graduate employment trajectories. This is the closest the framework can come to a randomized comparison — the alternative-program comparison-group data the literature would ideally call for does not exist for graphic communications at the scale needed. The graduate-outcome dataset is the framework's path to evidence-against-an-external-standard.

Process validation: faculty and advisory-board review

Curriculum-design decisions are not the kind of empirical claim that can be settled purely by data. Career-target descriptors, sub-competency definitions, act boundaries, foundational-competency lists — these are choices made by faculty leadership and refined by advisory-board input. The framework's validation surface at this layer is whether the categories the tool uses match the categories faculty and advisory partners actually think with. When the matrix surfaces a finding that contradicts faculty intuition, the resolution may be that the matrix is wrong; it may be that the intuition is wrong; it may be that both are pointing at a real ambiguity in how the program defines what it's trying to develop. The framework treats all three resolutions as productive — each one teaches something — provided the disagreement is named and acted on rather than dismissed.

What "wrong" would look like

The framework should be able to fail informatively. Concrete signals that would warrant revising or replacing it:

  • The coverage matrix consistently surfaces gaps that faculty review uniformly dismisses as artifacts — across multiple snapshots, multiple targets, multiple semesters. (Matrix scoring would need recalibration or the underlying KUD+ depth scales would need revision.)
  • Graduates whose curricular history matches the framework's "well-scaffolded" pattern do not, on the graduate-outcome dataset, actually perform better in their target career destinations than graduates whose history doesn't. (The framework's central scaffolding claim would be falsified.)
  • The brittle-scaffold diagnostic flags Act 3 courses as expecting unsupported mastery, faculty redesign those courses to address the diagnostic, and student performance in those courses does not improve. (The diagnostic isn't tracking what it claims to track.)
  • Inter-rater reliability on the AI's K/U/D scoring proves unacceptably low — two captures of the same course by the same instructor with the same materials produce systematically divergent profiles. (The instrument isn't measuring stably.)

None of these would invalidate the underlying ambition of evidence-backed program-level curriculum mapping. They would tell the framework which of its specific design choices need to change. That is what it means for a tool to be a research instrument in good faith: the framework commits to revising its own design when the data tells it to.

§ 14What this framework is not

Several limitations and scope decisions are worth naming explicitly.

  • Not an evaluation of faculty or students. Depth scores describe what the course's materials and assignments require, not how well any instructor delivers the course or how well any student performs in it.
  • Not an automated curriculum designer. The framework surfaces what is and supports faculty deliberation; redesign decisions remain committee work informed by the evidence, not generated by it.
  • Not a replacement for faculty judgment. The auditor drafts, the instructor confirms. Every depth value is editable in the review step; reviewer status is recorded on each profile.
  • Not empirically validated yet. The framework's specific scales, the 3-Act structure, and the cumulative-scaffolding claim are working hypotheses. The framework's response to the absence of validation is structural rather than evasive: the tool generates the longitudinal evidence its own commitments would need from external research. The full three-layer validation approach — internal flag-and-dispute pipeline Live, external graduate-outcome comparison Planned, faculty/advisory-board review — is described in §13 above, with the graduate-outcome dataset path detailed separately in the Graduate Outcome Criterion-Relevance Study proposal.
  • Not extensible to fully automated assessment scoring. Bloom's taxonomy and AAC&U VALUE rubrics remain the appropriate lenses for evaluating student work; this framework is designed for describing what a course develops, not for grading what a student produced.
  • Not insensitive to material limitations. The pipeline's source coverage — what it can retrieve, what it cannot, and the scoring implication for each — is documented in the canonical ingestion table in §10. In brief: Canvas materials (assignments, rubrics, pages, discussions, quizzes, modules, file attachments), linked Google Docs/Sheets/Slides and Drive PDFs, YouTube captions, direct uploads, and catalog values are all within reach. Video platforms other than YouTube (Panopto, Canvas Studio, Vimeo, Kaltura) and external LTI content are not. The auditor is required to detect references to unsupported sources, flag them with their specifics, and keep scoring conservative pending instructor confirmation; profiles produced from incomplete materials carry that uncertainty in their rationale fields.

§ 15Open hypotheses and the research agenda

Everything above is the framework as it currently stands and is prepared to defend. This section gathers, in one place, the forward work — open hypotheses and design directions that are deliberately not part of the validated case. They are unbuilt, and most are gated on the same external dependency: the graduate-outcome study (§13). They are summarized here so the research arc is visible rather than scattered through the sections; each is developed in full, with its literature, in the companion Curriculum Measurement — Open Hypotheses & Research Agenda.

  • KUD+ as a unit of account (the keystone). That one K/U/D × depth currency makes course output, prerequisite expectation, program coverage, and career demand commensurable — so the threads through the curriculum and the cross-walk into careers are the same move. Bounded in-body at §1 (the common-metric framing).
  • Durability and “bleed-off” (B). Instantaneous depth ≠ durable graduation-time depth: depth decays unless reinforced, and repeated, varied reinforcement may consolidate it. A structural read (lone-peak vs. reinforced-thread) is conceivable now; a quantitative decay-and-consolidation model — borrowing spaced-repetition forms such as Half-Life Regression / FSRS — is gated on outcome data. Bounded in-body at §6 (the durability boundary) and §12 (the pull-up qualification).
  • Competency→career cross-walk (A). A weighted, match-quality-stamped mapping from the program's competencies to occupational demand, seeded from the national Course-Skill Atlas, with “no clean match” treated as a first-class finding rather than forced. Bounded in-body at §11.
  • Uncertainty and convergence discipline (C). There is no reliable oracle, human or AI; some of the residual is likely irreducible, though how much is an open question; the honest output is an interval; and the one error class no ensemble can cancel — correlated LLM-judge bias — is exactly why the external graduate-outcome study is structurally necessary, not optional. Its core (no clean oracle; correlated LLM errors) is the best-supported part of the hypotheses; the irreducible share is open. Bounded in-body at §7.
  • Skill relatedness and embeddings. Deciding when two competencies are related by retrieve-then-reason — embeddings propose candidates, depth-aware reasoning adjudicates, bounded by authored structure. A domain-tuned embedder (a recall-stage gain) and a full relatedness engine are forward directions. Bounded in-body at §11.
  • Scale representation. Integer faculty judgments at the boundary; continuous, interval-carrying values in any derived (decay, aggregation, alignment) computation. Bounded in-body at §6.

The posture is the one §13 already states: the tool is built to be its own research instrument, and these are the agenda it generates the evidence to test. None is asserted as settled — the companion document marks throughout what is consistent with the literature versus what is a stipulated design choice awaiting validation.

§ 16KUD+ in the AI-augmented workplace

KUD+ was designed to fix three problems in how courses are described. The 0–5 depth scale replaces a yes-or-no “covered” checkmark. Scoring know, understand and do separately shows when a course builds one without the others, such as vocabulary without the reasoning behind it. The evidence rule keeps a score from resting on what the syllabus promises rather than on what students actually showed. Recent workplace research suggests these same three distinctions now matter for a second reason: they line up with how generative AI is changing which parts of knowledge work people still do. This does not revise the framework; it may shift its emphasis, and it sharpens one measurement risk. A caution first: every study cited here observed experienced professionals using AI to do work, not students using AI to learn; the findings are recent and, in one case, contested. They bear on career-demand emphasis (§11) and on the evidence discipline (§7), not on the learning claims §4 grounds.

Understand and Do come apart under AI

A preregistered field experiment at Procter & Gamble (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026) found that AI raised the quality of the solutions professionals produced — the Do-side output. Yet participants using AI were 9.2 percentage points less likely to expect their solution to rank in the top 10%, which the authors read as “a disconnect between actual and perceived performance.” In their Figure 10 the gap sits with individuals working alone (about 17% with AI against 26% without); teams working with AI expected slightly more than the control group. Two limits matter. The finding is under-confidence about one’s own AI-assisted work, not evidence that people cannot direct or critique AI. And for individuals working alone with AI, the gain in top-10% solutions was itself “not statistically significant.” The paper makes no claim about judgment as a skill or about labor markets. A related pattern appears in a survey of 319 knowledge workers reporting 936 examples of their own AI use: higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking, and the authors report “a shift in cognitive effort as knowledge workers increasingly move from task execution to oversight when using GenAI” (Lee et al., 2025). That is self-report, and it describes how effort shifts within tasks, not what employers pay for. (The claim, sometimes attributed to these studies, that AI users produce less diverse outputs is earlier work Lee et al. cite as “mechanised convergence”, not one of their findings.) In this framework’s terms, judging the quality of one’s own work is an Understand-side capacity; neither paper uses that framing. The framework already refuses to collapse Understand and Do into one “covered” bit and names the D-high/U-low dissociation — craft without articulation (§5) — as a case to surface; these studies suggest AI may widen that seam.

A forward-looking hypothesis, not a finding

One reading of this evidence is that as AI takes over more production, evaluative judgment — directing, checking and critiquing work, the Understand side — becomes the scarcer human contribution. If so, a program that certifies polished output (Do) without the judgment to direct and critique it would be certifying the part AI most readily supplies. No source cited in this section measures how the labor market prices judgment against production, so this is a hypothesis to test, not a result. What each source shows, and does not show:

  • Dell’Acqua et al. (2026) show that AI raised output quality, that AI “can effectively substitute for the expertise and guidance typically provided by team members that are familiar with the task at hand,” and that AI users under-rated their own output. They do not address judgment as a skill that the market prices, or labor markets at all.
  • Lee et al. (2025) show a self-reported shift from task execution to oversight, and an association between confidence in AI and less critical thinking. They do not measure what employers value.
  • Autor & Thompson (2025) show, over four decades of automation, that removing an occupation’s inexpert tasks raised wages and reduced employment in it. They do not study generative AI or creative work, and they do not say that the remaining expertise is evaluative judgment; applying their model here is our inference.
  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2026) show early-career employment declines in AI-exposed occupations and a codified/tacit-knowledge gradient. They do not address evaluative judgment, and their tacit-knowledge reading (discussed below) partly cuts the other way: it suggests that capability built through practice, the Do axis’s territory, is the protected half rather than the de-priced one. Their tacit index also bundles judgment-and-decision-making activities with hands-on ones, so it does not map cleanly onto Understand versus Do.

The two readings fit together only if the Do that keeps its value is deployment under varied conditions (the upper anchors) rather than a single polished artifact, and that is itself untested. The graduate-outcome study is one place where the hypothesis could begin to be checked.

AI inflates the Do-evidence signal

The evidence rule in §7 was built against one adversary: aspirational syllabus verbs. AI introduces a second. A competent-looking artifact no longer establishes student attainment, because the artifact can be produced by the tool while the underlying knowledge remains un-internalized. This does not weaken evidence-above-zero; it sharpens which evidence counts. The upper-band anchors — reasoning through novel cases, adapting to new conditions, critiquing and extending (§6) — probe deployment under variation rather than production of an artifact, and are correspondingly harder for AI-assisted output to counterfeit. In an AI-saturated setting those deployment-probing anchors carry more diagnostic weight, and a Do score resting on a single polished deliverable warrants more suspicion than it once did.

Destinations are cross-occupation bundles, not fixed occupations

An analysis of work-related AI use (Chin & Richmond, 2026) found that 16.8% of all work-related messages — roughly 44% of the occupation-specific ones, once the 61.5% that is generic work like drafting email is set aside — concern tasks historically belonging to another occupation; occupational boundaries blur while each role keeps a recognizable core, and — squarely in GC’s neighborhood — design draws most heavily on cross-occupation tasks while marketing and engineering tasks travel furthest into other roles. This is external support for the split verdict §11 already reaches: anchoring the destination side in a federal occupational taxonomy would under-count the cross-boundary task bundles graduates actually enter, and the fastest-growing GC-adjacent roles — an emerging Creative-/Workflow-Technologist cluster — have no SOC code at all — though evidence from the alumni corpus sharpens that: the failure is not absence but incoherent placement. Across 1,337 validated graduates (October 2026), 42 positions held by 34 people carry workflow/automation/technologist vocabulary in the title, only 4 of them first jobs, and the family scatters across seven SOC major groups (11, 13, 15, 17, 41, 43, 51) — “Workflow Automation” to Prepress Technicians, “Senior Workflow Engineer” to Industrial Engineers, “Workflow Coordinator” to Web and Digital Interface Designers, “Workflow Support Assistant” to Office and Administrative Support; two titles are coded two incompatible ways outright. An absent code is a visible null; incoherent placement is invisible and silently corrupts anything aggregated by major group, which is the more damaging failure. A taxonomy-lag the graduate-outcome study treats as a finding rather than a defect. The evidence-derived Role Outcome Profile, not the SOC crosswalk, is the construct that survives this shift.

That task-level picture is not one vendor’s artifact. A second platform reporting against the same taxonomy — the Anthropic Economic Index (Massenkoff et al., 2026), which classifies a million sampled conversations against O*NET tasks — finds that roughly 49% of jobs have seen at least a quarter of their tasks performed using that assistant, and that usage keeps diversifying (its ten most common tasks fell from 24% to 19% of consumer conversations in three months). Two independent platforms measuring against the same task taxonomy is what turns “AI use is task-level and does not respect occupational boundaries” into a triangulated observation rather than a single company’s. Both remain usage data from self-selected users, not economy-wide behavior, and neither speaks to workers who use no AI at all. But that limitation is also the point, and it suggests why the crosswalk lags. One reading, ours rather than either report’s, is that usage is a window into an earlier stage of job evolution, where workers recombine tasks before firms revise job descriptions or invent new titles. Read that way the SOC gap is a leading indicator of occupational change rather than a defect in the taxonomy — the classification is not wrong, it is downstream. Two further details from Chin & Richmond bear directly on where GC graduates land: crossover runs higher at small firms (18.9% of messages in 2–5-seat workspaces vs 16.3% at 101+, among mid-volume users), which is much of the studio and in-house market; and the flow has a direction the headline percentages hide — marketing and engineering tasks travel furthest into other occupations, while design, sales and HR are the heaviest borrowers. GC sits on both sides of that split.

The entry rung, and why Q1 tightens

The most consequential — and least settled — signal concerns the entry level. US payroll evidence (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, & Chen, 2026) reports relative employment declines for early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations while experienced-worker employment holds, concentrated where AI automates rather than augments; by mid-2026, workers aged 22–25 in the most-exposed quintile had fallen about 18 percentage points behind the least-exposed. A central critique is that AI exposure may proxy for macro-sensitivity, and the wider literature now contests the direction as well as the size (next paragraph). A second caveat is structural and belongs beside it: while the employment outcomes are administrative payroll records, both of the study’s primary AI-exposure measures are themselves LLM-derived — GPT-4-rated task exposure from Eloundou et al. (2024), who collected human ratings in parallel and caution that “none should be considered the definitive ground truth,” and Claude conversation shares from the Anthropic Economic Index. Which occupations count as “AI-exposed” therefore rests on model judgment, the same correlated-error concern §7 raises about LLM ensembles; non-model exposure measures appear only as robustness checks. The authors additionally flag three patterns themselves: divergent trends that predate ChatGPT (around COVID), estimates that attenuate when controlling for occupational education — which they note “may reflect either an alternative explanation or the very channel through which AI operates” — and a divergence more pronounced in their payroll panel than in national survey benchmarks.

The wider evidence, a year on. That finding no longer stands alone, and the studies around it disagree. Supporting it: an adoption-based design, which observes which firms actually integrate generative AI rather than assigning an exposure score, finds junior employment at adopting firms about 9% below non-adopters within six quarters, through slower hiring rather than separations, while senior employment continues its prior trend (Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger, 2026) — though the adopters are large professional-services firms, not the small studios and in-house teams many GC graduates join. Census records for about 6.7 million bachelor’s graduates (6,665,500) show the most AI-exposed tenth of majors starting 5 points less likely to be employed and with about 13% lower initial earnings after ChatGPT, roughly half of it from starting in lower-paying sectors, with the earnings gap narrowing to about 5% after two years in the authors’ summary (3.3 log points in their regression estimate) (Orr, Tucker, & Warren, 2026). A UK degree-level study finds the junior share of employment in exposed degrees’ destination occupations falling, though job postings show only weak evidence (Vossos et al., 2026). Against it: Norway’s entire private-sector workforce through February 2026 shows no employment gap between the most and least exposed occupations (0.1% against 0.3% growth since October 2022, a gap the authors call “small and not statistically significant” and say differential pre-trends make “even more uncertain”); for workers aged 21–30 the exposure gradient is, in their words, “a relatively precise null”; a gap for 22–25-year-olds appears only from spring 2025, and the authors call it “an emerging pattern rather than an identified effect” (Hernæs & Kostøl, 2026). Two confounds remain open. On timing, unemployment risk in AI-exposed occupations rose from early 2022, months before ChatGPT, and graduates from 2021 onward entered AI-exposed jobs at lower rates, with gaps opening before late 2022; a salary-based comparison moved the same way after 2022, which the authors read as “a broader early-career tightening that is not specific to LLM-exposed workers.” That timing fits the 2022 tech and interest-rate downturn as well as AI (Frank et al., 2026). On remote work, adding remote-work exposure absorbs the AI effect in résumé and job-posting data (Lambert & Schindler, 2026), while the same control leaves the AI gradient intact in payroll data (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, & Chen, 2026); neither side has shown whether the data source or the definition of seniority drives the difference. Every study here is a working paper, and most score exposure with the same GPT-4 task ratings, so their agreement is less independent than their number suggests. The defensible reading is narrower than a single headline: in US data, early-career hiring into AI-exposed work has weakened; how much of that AI caused is unresolved; and aggregate employment shows no disruption.

Closest to GC. The Census by-major table gives the nearest outside view of this program’s graduates; its per-major figures are event-study coefficients the authors present “for reference,” not headline causal estimates (Orr, Tucker, & Warren, 2026, Table A3). Between 2022 and 2024, Commercial Art and Graphic Design, which the exposure index places in its sixth decile, inside the paper’s less-exposed comparison group (deciles 1–6), showed one of the largest relative earnings declines in the creative and communications cluster: −0.070 log points against the average major. Communications, Marketing, Mass Media, Advertising and Journalism declined by 0.04 to 0.07. Industrial Production Technologies, the nearest proxy for GC’s production side, moved the other way (+0.026). These per-major contrasts cannot separate AI from the advertising and tech slowdown of the same years, and no Graphic Communications or printing major is large enough to appear. They do caution against treating the exposure index as a forecast for GC, because a design major the index groups with the less-exposed majors is among those whose graduates’ pay fell most. Two further cautions belong here. Graduates with more AI-exposed coursework had higher first-job pay and shorter job searches after ChatGPT, an association the authors state is not causal (Frank et al., 2026). And UK applicants have moved toward AI-exposed subjects since ChatGPT, against what human-capital theory predicts (Vossos et al., 2026); that study excludes design and the creative arts for lack of data, so it says nothing about GC’s own field.

Read alongside the Cybernetic Teammate result, where non-specialists working alone with AI matched teams that included a specialist (which the authors say “suggests that AI can effectively substitute for the expertise and guidance typically provided by team members that are familiar with the task at hand”), a plausible mechanism appears even where the number is disputed: the execution-apprenticeship rung — the traditional on-ramp by which judgment was acquired after hiring — may be the part most exposed (our reading, not a measured result). If that rung thins, “does the curriculum build students toward the careers we claim to prepare them for” becomes necessary but no longer sufficient: the program may need to supply more of the judgment the first job used to build. Autor and Thompson (2025) supply the mechanism that association alone does not: in their expertise framework, the direction of the effect depends on which tasks automation removes. Strip an occupation’s less-expert tasks and the surviving job demands more expertise — wages rise, employment falls, and fewer workers qualify to enter; strip its expert tasks and requirements fall, admitting less-expert workers at lower wages. Applied to the creative occupations this program feeds, the first case is the one to watch: if AI absorbs production execution and routine comping, the model predicts that the remaining work demands more expertise, held by fewer people. Whether that remaining expertise is mainly evaluative judgment, and whether the on-ramp that built it is the part being absorbed, is our reading, not theirs. Two caveats belong with the citation: their empirical result covers four decades of automation, not generative AI specifically, so the application here is an inference about mechanism rather than a demonstrated AI finding; and their model is deliberately stylized (unidimensional expertise, exogenous task bundling). The same payroll evidence now proposes a mechanism for why the young are affected and the experienced are not, and it lands squarely on this framework’s territory: AI “substitutes more effectively for codified knowledge — formal, standardized, documented knowledge that can be taught through education, textbooks, or written procedures — while complementing tacit knowledge acquired through practice, mentorship, and repeated exposure to real situations.” Empirically they find employment declines for young workers in codified-knowledge occupations, while tacit-knowledge occupations see faster employment growth for experienced workers. That is an external, administrative-data echo of the position the problem-solving synthesis reaches from the learning-science side, and it sharpens what a curriculum can do about it: the part AI substitutes for is the part a syllabus is best at delivering, and the part it complements is the part that requires productive failure, mentorship and repeated exposure — which is what the Do axis is built to detect and what §6’s upper depths describe. A nuance from their own footnote decides which half of the mechanism is load-bearing: the codified gradient is not significant once college share is controlled, “demonstrating its overlap with education”, while the tacit gradient for experienced workers survives. Codified knowledge overlaps heavily with education; the durable half is the tacit half — which is precisely the half a syllabus cannot deliver alone and the Do axis exists to detect. (This is the tension flagged in the hypothesis box above: on this reading, practice-built Do is the protected half, not the de-priced one.) Two readings matter and a third does not. Recent GC graduates are more than 5× over-represented in occupations the framework expects to grow with AI (64.6% against 11.9%), and they are almost entirely absent from the quiet half of the economy — 3.8% against 47.3% — so about 96% of first destinations sit in a bucket where AI is expected to act at all, against roughly 53% nationally. What is not true, and was corrected before it traveled: GC graduates are not unusually exposed to automation risk. For the ten-year window the share is 16.9% against 17.7% nationally, essentially the national rate. Across all cohorts it is 20.9%, and the difference is the older prepress destinations, not a recent trend. (An earlier pass compared a share of people against an unweighted count of occupation codes, which halved the apparent national automation-risk share and would have supported a “double the national risk” claim that is false.) Caveats that must travel: the framework's archetypes rest on model-generated inputs — its demand-elasticity term is an LLM “price-response prior,” explicitly “not a full forecast,” without confidence intervals — its authors state the categories “are not job-loss forecasts” but a map, and we read occupations near the elasticity boundary (~1.0) as unclassified rather than assigned (our caveat, not the authors’). In the data download, GC's two signature occupations sit clear of that boundary (Graphic Designers at 1.08, above the maximum found anywhere in the higher-risk bucket; Prepress Technicians at 0.56, mid-bucket), which is why the split between them is reportable at all. The occupation coding behind these shares has not yet been checked by an independent second coder, a step the study's pre-registration requires before any confirmatory claim; treat them as descriptive.

Their own caveat travels with it: the codified and tacit indices are proxies (required formal education and taught-content domains against required work experience, on-the-job training and experiential domains), offered as “a simple descriptive exercise” — suggestive mechanism evidence, not measurement. That is not a new mandate for the framework — it is the §4 problem-solving thesis (deployable, well-organized domain knowledge developed through productive failure) now with a possible labor-market rationale as well as a pedagogical one.

Net. Three consequences follow, all refinements of emphasis rather than new machinery, and the first is provisional: consider giving Understand more weight as a readiness signal in AI-augmented destinations, a consideration pending the graduate-outcome study rather than a settled rule; harden the Do-evidence rule against AI-authored artifacts by leaning on the deployment-probing upper anchors; and keep the destination side evidence-derived rather than taxonomy-anchored. The framework’s response to a world where AI can manufacture the artifact is to raise the evidentiary bar, not to lower it.
